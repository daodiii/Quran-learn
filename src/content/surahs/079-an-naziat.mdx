# An-Naziʿat (Those Who Drag Forth)

## Overview

- **Revelation:** Meccan
- **Verses:** 46
- **Theme:** The Day of Resurrection and the story of Pharaoh's defiance - warnings about the certainty of judgment and the fate of tyrants who reject divine authority.
- **Grammar Focus:** Oath constructions with active participles, narrative past tense sequences, interrogative-rhetorical structures, idha and amma clauses, temporal adverbs (yawma'idhin), and complex embedded clauses.

<Callout type="tip" title="Surah Architecture">
An-Nazi'at is built on a five-section rhetorical arc, each marked by a distinct grammatical shift. It opens with a powerful cascade of five oaths (verses 1-5) constructed from wa-/fa- plus feminine plural active participles and cognate accusatives — a sustained qasam sequence. The surah then pivots to eschatological imagery (verses 6-14) using temporal adverbs (يَوْمَ, يَوْمَئِذٍۢ) and the particle of sudden occurrence (فَإِذَا). The central narrative of Moses and Pharaoh (verses 15-26) employs sequential past-tense verbs linked by fa-, imperative mood for divine commands, and direct-speech nominal sentences culminating in Pharaoh's blasphemous self-deification. A creation argument section (verses 27-33) deploys rhetorical interrogatives (ءَأَنتُمْ) with tamyiiz, Form IV causative verbs, and objects of unspoken verbs (ishtighal). The closing judgment scene (verses 34-46) weaves conditional أَمَّا...فَإِنَّ parallelism, the restrictive إِنَّمَا, and the comparative كَأَنَّ to compress all of worldly life into "an afternoon or its morning." Throughout, active participles serve as substantive nouns — a signature morphological feature unifying the surah from its oath-opening to its eschatological climax.
</Callout>

#### Word-by-Word Breakdown

<GrammarTable>
| # | Arabic | Transliteration | Root | Morphology | I'rab | Meaning |
|---|--------|----------------|------|------------|-------|---------|
| 1 | وَٱلنَّـٰزِعَـٰتِ | wa-n-naazi'aati | ن ز ع | Particle + Noun - feminine plural, definite, active participle | Oath wa (the verb “I swear” is left unsaid) + Object of oath (muqsam bihi) - genitive (majrur) | By those who extract |
| 2 | غَرْقًا | gharqan | غ ر ق | Noun - verbal noun (masdar), indefinite, accusative | Maf'ul mutlaq standing for the verbal noun - accusative (mansub); al-Jadwal also quotes a view that it is a hal | Violently/drowning |

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The verse opens with the oath wa-, which governs the genitive: ٱلنَّـٰزِعَـٰتِ is the object of the oath (muqsam bihi). The phrase belongs to an unspoken verb, “I swear” (أُقْسِمُ). The noun غَرْقًا is accusative as a maf'ul mutlaq standing for the verbal noun; al-Jadwal says it takes the place of the masdar because it meets the participle in meaning, and it quotes another view that makes it a hal. The answer to the oath (jawab al-qasam) is left out. al-Jadwal supplies لَتُبْعَثُنَّ, “you will surely be raised”, so the answer stays open across the five oath clauses. al-Jadwal says ٱلنَّـٰزِعَـٰتِ are the angels.

**Sarf (Morphology):** ٱلنَّـٰزِعَـٰتِ is derived from the root ن-ز-ع on the pattern فَاعِلَات, the sound feminine plural of the Form I active participle. The alif-ta' ending marks the plural. غَرْقًا is a verbal noun (masdar) from غ-ر-ق on the pattern فَعْل (al-Jadwal also allows a name of the action of أَغْرَقَ), carrying the sense of deep immersion or drowning.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The surah opens in medias res with an oath by unnamed agents, generating immediate dramatic tension. The choice of active participles rather than finite verbs presents the action as an enduring attribute of these beings, not a one-time event. The cognate accusative غَرْقًا intensifies the extraction to an extreme degree, evoking the image of pulling something deeply embedded — the soul from the body.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The second oath clause mirrors the first: a definite feminine plural active participle (muqsam bihi, genitive) + an indefinite accusative masdar (maf'ul mutlaq). al-Jadwal reads this wa- as a joining wa- ('atifa), not as a new oath wa-: the oath is made once, in verse 1, and the later oaths are joined to it. The parallel structure still binds the clauses into one oath sequence.

**Sarf (Morphology):** ٱلنَّـٰشِطَـٰتِ derives from ن-ش-ط on the Form I active participle pattern فَاعِلَات. The root carries the meaning of untying or loosening with ease. نَشْطًا is the masdar on pattern فَعْل, reinforcing the verbal meaning embedded in the participle.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The juxtaposition of غَرْقًا (violent extraction, verse 1) with نَشْطًا (gentle release, verse 2) creates a deliberate contrast (muqaabala). Commentators often hear in it the hard pulling of the souls of unbelievers against the gentle release of believers; al-Jadwal itself only says that all of these are the angels. The parallel grammatical structure underscores the thematic contrast — same syntactic form, opposite semantic content.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The third oath clause continues the identical pattern: a joining wa- ('atifa) + definite active participle (genitive, muqsam bihi) + indefinite masdar (accusative, maf'ul mutlaq). All three clauses (verses 1-3) are syntactically parallel, creating a tripartite oath structure before the shift to fa- in verse 4. al-Jadwal reads the wa- and the fa- alike as joining particles; only the first wa- is the oath wa-.

**Sarf (Morphology):** ٱلسَّـٰبِحَـٰتِ is from the root س-ب-ح on the pattern فَاعِلَات. The root originally means "to swim" and is extended metaphorically to mean gliding or moving swiftly through space. سَبْحًا is its masdar on the فَعْل pattern, identical in form to the previous masdars.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The image of "swimming" through the cosmos is a powerful metaphor (isti'aara) that transforms the abstract concept of angelic movement into a vivid sensory experience. The sustained repetition of the same grammatical pattern across three verses builds rhythmic momentum (tarsi'), creating an incantatory effect appropriate to the oath form.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The link changes from wa- to fa-, but al-Jadwal reads this fa- as a joining particle ('atifa) too, the same job as the wa- before it. The overall pattern (definite participle + accusative masdar) is maintained. A fa- can add the sense of “then”, one after the other, so it may hint at a progression, but the grammar book calls it a plain joining fa-, not a fa- of cause.

**Sarf (Morphology):** ٱلسَّـٰبِقَـٰتِ derives from س-ب-ق on the Form I active participle pattern فَاعِلَات. The root denotes outstripping or surpassing. سَبْقًا is its verbal noun on the فَعْل pattern. The morphological consistency across all four oath clauses (فَاعِلَات + فَعْل) creates a tight structural unity.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The transition from wa- to fa- may mark a rhetorical escalation (taraqqi) — the actions can be heard to intensify from extraction, to gentle release, to gliding, to racing. Each new oath adds energy and speed. The fa- joins this oath to the one before, and the oaths move on toward the final one in verse 5.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** This final oath clause breaks the pattern: أَمْرًا is not a cognate accusative (maf'ul mutlaq) but a direct object (maf'ul bihi) of the transitive participle ٱلْمُدَبِّرَٰتِ. The answer to the entire five-part oath (jawab al-qasam) is left out. al-Jadwal supplies لَتُبْعَثُنَّ, “you will surely be raised”, from what follows, which leaves the oath's force open until verse 6.

**Sarf (Morphology):** ٱلْمُدَبِّرَٰتِ is from the root د-ب-ر on the Form II active participle pattern مُفَعِّلَات. Form II (فَعَّلَ) adds intensification or causation to the base meaning, so مُدَبِّر means "one who arranges/manages thoroughly." This is the only Form II participle in the oath sequence; all others are Form I.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The culmination of the oath series with "those who arrange every matter" provides a grand conclusion (khaatima) to the sequence. While the first four oaths describe physical actions (extracting, releasing, gliding, racing), this fifth oath elevates to cosmic administration. The shift from cognate accusative to direct object mirrors this thematic elevation — these beings do not merely perform actions but govern affairs.

<GrammarTable>
| # | Arabic | Transliteration | Root | Morphology | I'rab | Meaning |
|---|--------|----------------|------|------------|-------|---------|
| 1 | يَوْمَ | yawma | ي و م | Noun - masculine, singular, indefinite, accusative | Adverb of time (zarf zaman) - accusative (mansub); it is tied to the unspoken answer of the oath, and the clause after it is its mudaf ilayhi | On the Day |

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** يَوْمَ is an adverbial accusative of time (zarf zaman) tied to the unspoken answer of the oath (لَتُبْعَثُنَّ); al-Jadwal also allows an unspoken “remember”. The clause after it is its mudaf ilayhi. The verbal sentence تَرْجُفُ ٱلرَّاجِفَةُ places the verb before the subject (fi'l + fa'il), the standard Arabic verbal sentence order. The verb agrees with its feminine singular subject in gender. This verse is therefore not the answer to the oath: the answer is left out, and يَوْمَ names the time of that unspoken answer.

**Sarf (Morphology):** تَرْجُفُ is a Form I present tense verb from ر-ج-ف meaning "to shake violently." ٱلرَّاجِفَةُ is its active participle on the فَاعِلَة pattern, used as a substantive noun referring to "the shaking event" — the first trumpet blast. The derivation of both verb and subject from the same root creates a figura etymologica.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The phrase تَرْجُفُ ٱلرَّاجِفَةُ employs the rhetorical device of jinas ishtiqaaqi (cognate paronomasia) — the verb and subject share the same root, creating an emphatic internal echo. The effect is to make the shaking feel self-referential and absolute: the very essence of shaking shakes. The adverbial يَوْمَ drops the listener into the Day of Judgment without transition. al-Jadwal calls the phrase a figurative attribution (isnad majazi): the blast is only the cause of the shaking, yet the shaking is given to it as if it did it.

The image of turning one's back (أَدْبَرَ) while simultaneously exerting effort (يَسْعَىٰ) creates a portrait of willful, energetic rejection.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The antithesis (tibaaq) between darkening night and bringing out daylight is a classic Quranic rhetorical device, presenting divine power through contrasting pairs. The choice of أَغْطَشَ (a rare, evocative word for darkening) over the common أَظْلَمَ creates linguistic freshness (gharaaba) that captures attention. The verbs present night and day not as automatic cycles but as actively managed by God — each evening He darkens, each morning He brings forth light.

The use of past tense for a future event is a powerful rhetorical device: by grammatically treating the Day of Judgment as already accomplished, the Quran makes it feel inevitable and as certain as history. The sequence from remembering deeds (verse 35) to seeing Hellfire (verse 36) traces the psychological journey of that Day.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** This is a nominal sentence with fronted predicate (khabar muqaddam): the prepositional phrase إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ is the predicate, placed first for emphasis on exclusive divine knowledge. مُنتَهَىٰهَآ is the delayed subject (mubtada' mu'akhkhar, nominative) with the possessive pronoun هَآ referring to ٱلسَّاعَة. The fronting creates restriction (hasr): its finality belongs to your Lord alone. al-Jadwal reads the sentence as the reason for the denial in the question of verse 43.

**Sarf (Morphology):** مُنتَهَى is a noun of place from Form VIII (اِنْتَهَى) of the root ن-ه-ي, on the مُفْتَعَل pattern. Form VIII (اِنْتَهَى) means "to reach the end". مُنتَهَى thus means "the place or time of ultimate conclusion."

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The fronting of إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ creates grammatical restriction: the knowledge of the Hour belongs exclusively to God. The possessive رَبِّكَ reaffirms the Prophet's relationship with God while marking the boundary of prophetic knowledge. The verse answers verse 42's question not with a date but with a principle: this knowledge is God's domain alone. The word مُنتَهَى conveys absolute finality — not merely "when" but "the ultimate endpoint of all things."

**Nahw (Syntax):** إِنَّمَآ is a restrictive particle (kaaffa + makfuufa) that prevents إِنَّ from governing the accusative, so أَنتَ remains in the nominative as mubtada'. مُنذِرُ is the predicate (khabar, nominative) in the first part of an idafa construction, with مَن as the mudaf ilayhi (genitive). يَخْشَىٰهَا is the verb of the relative clause (sila al-mawsul) with the attached pronoun هَا referring to ٱلسَّاعَة. al-Jadwal reads the sentence as a second reason for the denial in verse 43.

**Sarf (Morphology):** مُنذِرُ is a Form IV active participle (مُفْعِل) from the root ن-ذ-ر meaning "to warn." Form IV (أَنْذَرَ) makes the base meaning transitive/causative. The active participle مُنذِر means "one who warns." يَخْشَى is Form I from خ-ش-ي, a defective verb in the present tense with the attached object pronoun هَا.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The restrictive إِنَّمَآ defines and limits the Prophet's role with precision: he is ONLY a warner, not a timekeeper or fortune-teller. This restriction is not a diminishment but a clarification — warning is the Prophet's noble mission, and specifying the Hour's timing is not within it. The qualification مَن يَخْشَىٰهَا ("those who fear it") creates a paradox: the warning benefits only those already disposed to heed it, which may hint that those of verse 42 who only ask about timing are not the ones who benefit.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** كَأَنَّهُمْ is a compound particle (كَ comparative + أَنَّ emphatic) governing the attached pronoun هُمْ as its subject (ism ka-anna, accusative); it opens a new sentence. يَوْمَ is an adverb of time, and the clause يَرَوْنَهَا is its mudaf ilayhi; al-Jadwal ties يَوْمَ to a hal from the doer of the negated يَلْبَثُوا. لَمْ is a negative jussive particle governing يَلْبَثُوٓا۟ (jussive mood, with the deleted nun as jussive marker), and the clause لَمْ يَلْبَثُوا is the khabar of كَأَنَّ. إِلَّا makes the sentence a restriction (hasr): they stayed nothing but عَشِيَّةً (an adverb of time tied to يَلْبَثُوا), and ضُحَىٰهَا is joined to it by أَوْ.

**Sarf (Morphology):** يَرَوْنَ is Form I from ر-أ-ي (to see), a defective verb with the ي radical dropped in the present tense. يَلْبَثُوا is Form I from ل-ب-ث meaning "to remain/stay," in the jussive mood after لَمْ. عَشِيَّة is from ع-ش-و on the فَعِيلَة pattern, meaning "an afternoon/evening." ضُحَى is from ض-ح-و on the فُعَل pattern, meaning "mid-morning brightness."

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The final verse delivers the surah's most powerful temporal compression: an entire lifetime reduced to "an afternoon or its morning" — not even a full day, but half a day. The particle كَأَنَّ (as though) presents this not as fact but as subjective perception, yet the perception will feel absolutely real. The phrase ضُحَىٰهَا ("its morning") ties the morning to the afternoon with a pronoun; al-Jadwal says this is allowed because both belong to one day. This closing image resonates with the surah's opening themes of angels extracting souls and the sudden blast of resurrection — the entire human experience, framed between these bookends, is breathtakingly brief.


## Practice Exercises

<ExerciseBox question="In verse 1 (وَٱلنَّـٰزِعَـٰتِ غَرْقًا), identify the grammatical function of وَ and explain the iʿrāb of غَرْقًا. What type of oath construction is this?">

The وَ is wāw al-qasam (oath particle), a preposition governing the following noun in the majrūr case, with the verb “I swear” left unsaid. ٱلنَّـٰزِعَـٰتِ is the muqsam bihi (object of oath) — an active participle (ism fāʿil) from Form I نَزَعَ, in the feminine plural (فَاعِلَات pattern), majrūr by the wāw. غَرْقًا is manṣūb as a mafʿūl muṭlaq standing for the verbal noun (al-Jadwal; it quotes another view that makes it a ḥāl) — meaning “pulling out vigorously/with great force.” Only the first wāw is the oath wāw. In verses 2-5 the wāw and fāʾ are joining particles (ʿāṭifa), so the oath is made once and the next four clauses are joined to it. The answer to the oath is left out (al-Jadwal supplies لَتُبْعَثُنَّ, “you will surely be raised”).

</ExerciseBox>

<ExerciseBox question="In verse 40 (وَأَمَّا مَنْ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِۦ وَنَهَى ٱلنَّفْسَ عَنِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ), parse the conditional structure and explain the role of مَنْ and how the two verbs خَافَ and نَهَى function grammatically.">

أَمَّا is a particle of condition and detail (ḥarf shart ghayr jāzim, “as for”). مَنْ is a relative pronoun (ism mawsūl) and the mubtadaʾ; al-Jadwal says its predicate is left unsaid, as in verse 37. خَافَ is Form I māḍī (past tense) serving as the verb of the ṣila (the clause that completes مَنْ) — its fāʿil is the ḍamīr mustatir (hidden pronoun) referring back to مَنْ. مَقَامَ رَبِّهِ is the mafʿūl bihi (direct object) in an iḍāfa construction with the possessive pronoun. وَنَهَى is joined to خَافَ via wāw al-ʿaṭf — also Form I māḍī, in the same ṣila. ٱلنَّفْسَ is its mafʿūl bihi, and عَنِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ is a jārr wa majrūr (prepositional phrase) tied to نَهَى. The answer of أَمَّا is left out (al-Jadwal: “he enters Paradise”); the clause in verse 41, فَإِنَّ ٱلْجَنَّةَ هِىَ ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ, gives the reason for it and is introduced by fāʾ al-taʿlīl (the fāʾ of reason).

</ExerciseBox>

## Grammar Summary

<Callout type="note" title="Key Grammar Concepts in This Surah">

**Major Grammatical Constructs:**

1. **Oath Sequences (verses 1-5):** One oath (the first wa-) followed by four joining clauses (wa- and fa-, both 'atifa), with active participles (ٱلنَّـٰزِعَـٰتِ, ٱلنَّـٰشِطَـٰتِ, etc.) and maf'ul mutlaq masdars (غَرْقًا, نَشْطًا); the fifth takes a direct object. The answer to the oath is left out. Classical qasam structure.

2. **Narrative Past Tense (verses 15-26):** The story of Moses and Pharaoh uses sequential past tense verbs with fa- connectives: نَادَىٰهُ, أَرَىٰهُ, كَذَّبَ, عَصَىٰ, أَدْبَرَ, etc. Demonstrates Arabic narrative prose.

3. **Time and Condition Particles:**
   - إِذَا (verses 11, 34) as an adverb of time whose mudaf ilayhi is the clause after it, with the answer left unsaid
   - إِذْ (verse 16) for definite past time
   - أَمَّا (verses 37-41), a particle of condition: مَن has an unspoken predicate, and the فَإِنَّ clause gives the reason for it

4. **Interrogative-Rhetorical Structures:**
   - هَلْ (verses 15, 18) introducing questions
   - أَءَ (verses 10, 11, 27) for incredulous questions
   - أَيَّانَ (verse 42) questioning time
   - فِيمَ (verse 43) rhetorical "in what [position]"

5. **Emphasis Particles:**
   - إِنَّ and its sisters (verses 10, 17, 26, 39, 41) for emphatic nominal sentences; in verses 13 and 45 the ما of إِنَّمَا stops إِنَّ from working
   - لَـ emphatic prefix (verses 10, 26)
   - إِنَّمَا (verses 13, 45) for restriction/limitation

6. **Active Participles as Substantives:** Throughout surah - ٱلنَّـٰزِعَـٰتِ, ٱلسَّـٰبِحَـٰتِ, ٱلْمُدَبِّرَٰتِ, ٱلرَّاجِفَة, ٱلطَّآمَّة - participles functioning as nouns

7. **Temporal Adverbs:** يَوْمَ (verses 6, 35, 46), يَوْمَئِذٍۢ (verse 8), بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ (verse 30) - establishing time contexts

8. **Objects of Unspoken Verbs (Ishtighal):** وَٱلْأَرْضَ (verse 30), وَٱلْجِبَالَ (verse 32) - the noun comes first and an unspoken verb works on it, which the spoken verb explains

9. **Form IV Causatives:** أَرَىٰهُ (showed him), أَغْطَشَ (darkened), أَخْرَجَ (brought forth), أَرْسَىٰهَا (anchored) - common in creation descriptions

10. **Idafa Constructions:** حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰ, مَقَامَ رَبِّهِۦ, نَكَالَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ وَٱلْأُولَىٰ - possessive/descriptive relationships

**Cross-references:** This surah exemplifies advanced oath constructions (see Grammar Lesson on Qasam), clauses with إِذَا and أَمَّا, narrative tense sequencing, and rhetorical interrogatives.
</Callout>
