# At-Takwir (The Overthrowing)

## Overview

- **Revelation:** Meccan
- **Verses:** 29
- **Theme:** Vivid depiction of cosmic signs preceding the Day of Judgment, followed by revelation of the Quran through the angel Gabriel
- **Grammar Focus:** Extensive idha conditional chains (12 consecutive clauses), passive voice constructions, interrogative structures, oaths with emphasis particles

<Callout type="tip" title="Architecture of Cosmic Suspense">
At-Takwir is one of the most rhetorically powerful surahs in the Quran. It builds an extraordinary 12-clause إِذَا conditional chain (verses 1-13) before finally delivering the apodosis (jawab) in verse 14. Every clause uses a passive verb, concealing the Agent (Allah) while the natural order collapses. The listener is suspended through cosmic destruction, moral reckoning, and eschatological preparation before receiving the devastating conclusion: "A soul will know what it has brought." The surah then pivots to a triple oath (verses 15-18) validating the Quran's divine origin, before closing with one of the Quran's most piercing rhetorical questions: فَأَيْنَ تَذْهَبُونَ — "So where are you going?"
</Callout>

#### Word-by-Word Breakdown

<GrammarTable>
| # | Arabic | Transliteration | Root | Morphology | I'rab | Meaning |
|---|--------|----------------|------|------------|-------|---------|
| 1 | إِذَا | idha | - | Particle - time word for the future, with a condition in it | Adverb of time, mabni; accusative in place, tied to the answer عَلِمَتْ in verse 14. The sentence after it is its mudaf ilayhi (al-Jadwal) | when |

<Callout type="rule" title="إِذَا + a Noun, an Unspoken Verb, and a Passive Verb">
After إِذَا, al-Jadwal does not read الشَّمْسُ as a mubtada' with كُوِّرَتْ as its khabar. It says الشَّمْسُ is the subject (fa'il) of a verb that is left out, for example انطوت (“folded up”), and that كُوِّرَتْ comes after it only to explain it. The explaining clause كُوِّرَتْ has no place in the grammar, and the whole sentence with the unspoken verb is the mudaf ilayhi of إِذَا. The same reading holds for all 12 clauses. al-Jadwal also notes that some commentators take each noun as the نائب فاعل of an unspoken passive verb of the same kind as the one shown.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Form II Passive: Intensity of Cosmic Transformation">
كُوِّرَتْ is the passive of Form II (فُعِّلَ), which carries intensive meaning. The root ك-و-ر means "to wrap/fold," and Form II intensifies this: the sun is not merely dimmed but thoroughly wrapped up, extinguished completely. This intensive passive sets the tone for the escalating destruction that follows.
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** This is a conditional sentence introduced by إِذَا. al-Jadwal reads إِذَا as an adverb of time for the future, in the accusative position, tied to the answer عَلِمَتْ in verse 14. The sentence after it is its mudaf ilayhi: الشَّمْسُ is the fa'il of an unspoken verb (such as انطوت), and كُوِّرَتْ is an explaining clause with no place in the grammar. The jawab (apodosis) of this إِذَا is delayed all the way to verse 14 (عَلِمَتْ نَفْسٌ مَّا أَحْضَرَتْ), creating long syntactic suspense across 13 verses. The answer does not put its verb in the jussive (shart ghayr jazim).

**Sarf (Morphology):** كُوِّرَتْ derives from root ك-و-ر on the Form II passive pattern (فُعِّلَ). The original active would be كَوَّرَ (to wrap intensely). The passive is formed by changing the vowel pattern to ضُمَّة on the first radical and كَسْرَة on the second: كُوِّرَتْ. The تْ suffix marks feminine singular agreement with الشَّمْسُ.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** Opening with إِذَا rather than إِنْ signals certainty — these events will happen, not hypothetically but inevitably. The passive voice conceals the divine Agent, focusing the listener entirely on the terrifying events themselves. Starting with the sun — the most constant, dependable celestial body — being "wrapped up" immediately signals that the entire cosmic order is collapsing.

<Callout type="rule" title="Form VII (انْفَعَلَ): Reflexive/Passive Meaning">
انكَدَرَتْ follows Form VII (انْفَعَلَ) from root ك-د-ر. Form VII adds a reflexive/passive dimension: the stars do not merely fall — they fall of their own undoing, as if the cosmic order itself unravels. Unlike the explicit passive (فُعِلَ) used in verse 1, Form VII implies the action happens to the subject intrinsically.
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The وَ conjunction coordinates this clause with verse 1, continuing the إِذَا chain. As in verse 1, النُّجُومُ is the fa'il of an unspoken verb that the next word explains, and انكَدَرَتْ is the explaining clause. All these clauses are tied to the same distant answer in verse 14.

**Sarf (Morphology):** انكَدَرَتْ is Form VII (انْفَعَلَ) from root ك-د-ر, meaning "to become turbid/fall." The Form VII prefix انـ adds reflexive meaning. The تْ suffix is the feminine singular ending: a plural of things takes a feminine singular verb. Form VII verbs cannot be made passive (they already carry passive/reflexive sense), distinguishing them from the Form II passives in adjacent verses.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** Moving from the singular sun to the plural stars escalates the imagery. The choice of Form VII (self-acting) rather than a passive (acted upon) subtly varies the rhetorical texture — the stars scatter as if of their own volition, adding to the chaos.

<Callout type="rule" title="Form II Passive: Causative Intensity">
سُيِّرَتْ is the passive of Form II from root س-ي-ر (to walk/travel). Form II adds causative and intensive meaning: سَيَّرَ means "to make something travel/move." The passive سُيِّرَتْ means the mountains are forcibly made to travel — the most immovable objects in creation are set drifting like clouds.
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** Third coordinated إِذَا clause following the same pattern. الْجِبَالُ is the fa'il of an unspoken verb (al-Jadwal suggests انتثرت, “scattered”) and سُيِّرَتْ is the explaining passive verb. The syntactic parallelism across all these verses creates a rhythmic, hypnotic effect.

**Sarf (Morphology):** سُيِّرَتْ follows the Form II passive pattern فُعِّلَ from root س-ي-ر. The medial ي (a weak letter) is retained in this conjugation. The active would be سَيَّرَ (to cause to travel). Form II here carries its causative sense: something external causes the mountains to move.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The progression from celestial (sun, stars) to terrestrial (mountains) brings the destruction closer to human experience. Mountains symbolize permanence and stability in Arabic poetry and Quranic discourse — their movement signals the absolute reversal of the natural order.

<Callout type="tip" title="Cultural Context: The Most Prized Possession">
الْعِشَارُ refers to she-camels in their tenth month of pregnancy — the most valuable livestock in Arabian society. Their neglect would be unthinkable under any normal circumstances. By using this culturally specific image, the Quran speaks directly to its first audience: the terror of that Day is so overwhelming that even the most precious worldly possession is abandoned.
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** Continues the إِذَا chain with identical structure. الْعِشَارُ is the fa'il of an unspoken verb (al-Jadwal suggests سرحت وحدها, “went free on their own”) and عُطِّلَتْ is the explaining passive verb. The consistency of this syntactic pattern across verses creates a cumulative, relentless effect.

**Sarf (Morphology):** عُطِّلَتْ is Form II passive from root ع-ط-ل (to be idle/neglect). The active عَطَّلَ means "to leave idle, to abandon." Form II here carries intensive force: these camels are completely, utterly abandoned. الْعِشَارُ is the plural of عُشَرَاء (pattern فُعَلَاء), and it follows the broken plural pattern فِعَال.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The shift from cosmic destruction (sun, stars, mountains) to the human/social realm (pregnant camels) is a rhetorical descent from the universal to the particular. This grounds the apocalyptic imagery in lived experience, making it viscerally real for the original audience.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** Wild beasts, creatures that are by nature scattered and solitary, being gathered together intensifies the sense of cosmic reversal. If even untamed animals cannot escape divine gathering, what hope has humanity of avoiding accountability?

This verse is the jawab (answer/apodosis) of the entire إِذَا chain spanning verses 1-13. In Arabic grammar, when إِذَا introduces a condition, the jawab provides the consequence. Here, after 12 conditional clauses describing cosmic upheaval, the consequence is devastatingly simple: each soul will know what it brought. The use of the indefinite نَفْسٌ (a soul, any soul) makes it universal — no soul is excluded.
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** This is a verbal sentence serving as the jawab (apodosis) of the 12-verse إِذَا chain. عَلِمَتْ is the main verb, نَفْسٌ is the subject (fa'il, nominative with tanwin). مَا is a relative pronoun in the accusative position, the object (maf'ul bihi) of عَلِمَتْ, and أَحْضَرَتْ is its relative clause (silah), which has no place in the grammar. The pronoun that returns to مَا is left out (أَحْضَرَتْهُ). مَا means "what/that which."

**Sarf (Morphology):** عَلِمَتْ is Form I from root ع-ل-م (to know). أَحْضَرَتْ is Form IV from root ح-ض-ر (to be present); Form IV أَحْضَرَ means "to make present, to bring forth." The use of the past tense for future events (prophetic past) is a common Quranic device expressing certainty.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** After 13 verses of elaborate, escalating imagery, the conclusion is breathtakingly simple — four words. This contrast between the elaborate protasis and the terse apodosis is itself a rhetorical masterpiece. The indefinite نَفْسٌ (any soul) creates universality, while مَا أَحْضَرَتْ (what it brought forth) leaves the content unspecified, forcing each listener to confront their own deeds.

All five attributes span three verses but modify the same noun — an impressive example of extended adjectival chains in Arabic.

**Sarf (Morphology):** مُطَاعٍ is a passive participle (اسم مفعول) of the Form IV verb أَطَاعَ, from root ط-و-ع, on the مُفْعَل pattern. Its original shape was مُطْوَع: the fatha of the و moved to the ط, and the و became ا because the letter before it has a fatha. It means "the one who is obeyed." أَمِينٍ follows the فَعِيل pattern from root أ-م-ن, meaning "trustworthy/faithful."

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The final attribute — trustworthy — is the most critical for a messenger. Ending with أَمِينٍ creates a climactic sequence: noble → powerful → secure → obeyed → trustworthy. The rhetorical purpose is clear: if the messenger is this credentialed, the message he carries must be genuine.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** This verse complements verse 22: the Prophet is neither mad (مَجْنُون) nor withholding (ضَنِين). Its special plural form (with ين) follows the pattern used for rational beings and certain cosmic terms.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The description of the Quran as "a reminder for the worlds" — not just for the Arabs or the Quraysh — universalizes its message. The indefinite ذِكْرٌ (without الـ) is left without a limit, while لِلْعَالَمِينَ expands its scope to all of creation across all time.

| 5 | أَن | an | - | Particle - masdar/infinitive | Not declinable (mabni), masdar particle that puts the verb in the subjunctive | to |

</GrammarTable>

<Callout type="rule" title="المصدر المؤوّل: أَنْ + Subjunctive as Verbal Noun">
أَن يَسْتَقِيمَ forms a مصدر مؤوّل (interpreted infinitive/verbal noun). The particle أَنْ + the subjunctive verb together function as a single noun phrase equivalent to الاسْتِقَامَة (uprightness). This interpreted infinitive is the object of شَاءَ: "whoever wills uprightness." The subjunctive mood (منصوب) is marked by the fatha on يَسْتَقِيمَ.
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** لِمَن is a badal (substitute) of لِلْعَالَمِينَ in verse 27, with the preposition repeated (al-Jadwal): the reminder is for the worlds, and in particular for whoever of them wills. مَن is a relative pronoun, and شَاءَ is the verb of its relative clause (silah). مِنكُم is tied to a hal from the hidden subject of شَاءَ. أَن يَسْتَقِيمَ is a مصدر مؤوّل in the accusative position, the object of شَاءَ.

**Sarf (Morphology):** يَسْتَقِيمَ is Form X (اسْتَفْعَلَ) from root ق-و-م. Form X (اسْتَفْعَلَ) often means "to ask for" something, but here it gives the sense of holding firmly to a state: اسْتَقَامَ = to stay straight, to be upright. The medial و of the root becomes ي in Form X: قَوَمَ → اسْتَقَامَ → يَسْتَقِيمُ. In the subjunctive (mansub) the final damma becomes fatha: يَسْتَقِيمَ.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** This verse introduces the concept of human free will — the reminder is available to whoever "wills" (شَاءَ) to be upright. This sets up the theological counterpoint in verse 29, creating a balanced statement on the relationship between human choice and divine will.

<Callout type="rule" title="Subordination of Human Will to Divine Will">
مَا تَشَاءُونَ إِلَّا أَن يَشَاءَ اللَّهُ uses the same root (ش-ي-أ) for both human and divine willing, creating a direct grammatical relationship between them. The إِلَّا (except) makes human will conditional upon divine will. أَن يَشَاءَ is another مصدر مؤوّل (interpreted infinitive). al-Jadwal reads it as genitive in place, with an unspoken بِ, tied to تَشَاءُونَ: "You do not will except by the will of Allah." It also allows an accusative reading as a time: "except at the time Allah wills." This is one of the Quran's most precise theological statements, expressed through syntax.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The Theological Balance of Verses 28-29">
Verse 28 affirms human choice (لِمَن شَاءَ — for whoever wills), and verse 29 subordinates it to divine will (إِلَّا أَن يَشَاءَ اللَّهُ — except that Allah wills). Together, they articulate the Islamic theological position: humans have real agency (verse 28), but that agency operates within the framework of divine sovereignty (verse 29). The grammar carries the theology — neither verse can be read in isolation without distortion.
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** A verbal sentence with restriction. The وَ opens a new sentence (isti'naf). مَا negates, تَشَاءُونَ is the main verb (present indicative, marfu' with نون for الأفعال الخمسة). إِلَّا marks the restriction (hasr). أَن يَشَاءَ اللَّهُ is a مصدر مؤوّل: أَن + subjunctive verb يَشَاءَ, with اللَّهُ as fa'il (subject, nominative). al-Jadwal puts this مصدر مؤوّل in the genitive position, with an unspoken بِ, tied to تَشَاءُونَ ("except by the will of Allah"); it also allows an accusative reading as a time ("except at the time Allah wills"). رَبُّ الْعَالَمِينَ is a na't (adjective) of اللَّهُ — both nominative. رَبُّ is mudaf, الْعَالَمِينَ is mudaf ilayhi (genitive with يَاء). The object of both verbs is left out, for example "uprightness" (al-Jadwal).

**Sarf (Morphology):** تَشَاءُونَ and يَشَاءَ both derive from the same root ش-ي-أ (to will). تَشَاءُونَ is 2nd person masculine plural present indicative; يَشَاءَ is 3rd person masculine singular present subjunctive (after أَن). The deliberate use of the same verb for both human and divine willing emphasizes the parallel. رَبُّ is from root ر-ب-ب (to nurture/lord over).

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The surah concludes with the ultimate theological statement, using the most concise possible syntax. The repetition of the root ش-ي-أ (sha'a) in both the human and divine contexts creates a lexical mirror: your willing and Allah's willing are not independent phenomena but interconnected realities. The descriptive phrase رَبُّ الْعَالَمِينَ (Lord of the worlds) echoes لِلْعَالَمِينَ from verse 27, creating a frame: the reminder is for the worlds (v.27), and the Lord of the worlds is sovereign over all will (v.29).

## Practice Exercises

<ExerciseBox question="The إِذَا chain in verses 1-13 uses predominantly passive verbs. Identify three different verb forms (I, II, IV, VII) used in the passive across these verses, give one example of each, and explain how the form affects the meaning of the passive.">

**Form II Passive — كُوِّرَتْ (verse 1, root ك-و-ر):**
- Pattern: فُعِّلَ (passive of فَعَّلَ)
- Form II adds intensive/causative meaning. The passive كُوِّرَتْ means the sun was intensely/thoroughly wrapped up — not a gradual process but a complete, forceful transformation.

**Form VII — انكَدَرَتْ (verse 2, root ك-د-ر):**
- Pattern: انْفَعَلَ (inherently reflexive/passive)
- Form VII is not technically a passive of another form but carries its own reflexive/passive meaning intrinsically. انكَدَرَتْ means the stars fell/scattered of themselves. Unlike Form II passive (which implies an external agent acting intensely), Form VII implies the action happens to the subject from within.

**Form IV Passive — أُزْلِفَتْ (verse 13, root ز-ل-ف):**
- Pattern: أُفْعِلَ (passive of أَفْعَلَ)
- Form IV adds causative meaning. The passive أُزْلِفَتْ means Paradise was caused to be brought near — an external agent (Allah) makes Paradise approach the righteous, rather than the righteous traveling to it.

**Key insight:** The variety of verb forms in the passive creates subtle distinctions: Form II passive emphasizes intensity of the action done to the subject, Form VII emphasizes the subject's own transformation, and Form IV passive emphasizes an external agent causing the action. The Quran selects each form deliberately to convey the precise nature of each eschatological event.
</ExerciseBox>

<ExerciseBox question="Analyze the three emphatic negation constructions in verses 22, 24, and 25 (all using مَا...بِ). What accusation does each deny, and why is the emphatic بِ important to the argument?">

**Verse 22: وَمَا صَاحِبُكُم بِمَجْنُونٍ**
- **Accusation denied:** Madness/insanity of Prophet Muhammad
- **Noun of مَا:** صَاحِبُكُم (your companion) — referring to Muhammad

**Verse 24: وَمَا هُوَ عَلَى الْغَيْبِ بِضَنِينٍ**
- **Accusation denied:** Withholding or distorting revelation
- **Noun of مَا:** هُوَ (he) — referring to Muhammad

**Verse 25: وَمَا هُوَ بِقَوْلِ شَيْطَانٍ رَّجِيمٍ**
- **Accusation denied:** Satanic origin of the Quran
- **Noun of مَا:** هُوَ (it) — referring to the Quran itself

**The role of emphatic بِ:**
The بِ in these constructions is بَاء زائدة (extra/pleonastic preposition). Grammatically, it puts the khabar in the genitive in form (al-Jadwal: accusative in place, because مَا works like لَيْسَ), but it adds emphasis to the negation. Without بِ, the negation would be مَا هُوَ مَجْنُونًا (he is not mad). With بِ, it becomes مَا هُوَ بِمَجْنُونٍ (he is absolutely/emphatically not mad). The consistent use of this emphatic pattern three times creates a systematic, comprehensive defense where each accusation is not merely denied but forcefully rejected.
</ExerciseBox>

<ExerciseBox question="Compare the jawab (apodosis) structure of the two main sections: (1) the إِذَا chain (verses 1-13 → jawab in verse 14), and (2) the oath sequence (verses 15-18 → jawab in verse 19). How does the grammar of each jawab differ, and what does this reveal about their rhetorical purposes?">

**إِذَا Chain Jawab (Verse 14): عَلِمَتْ نَفْسٌ مَّا أَحْضَرَتْ**
- **Grammar:** Verbal sentence. Past tense verb عَلِمَتْ + indefinite subject نَفْسٌ + relative clause مَا أَحْضَرَتْ
- **Structure:** Verb-first (V-S-O pattern), no emphasis particles
- **Subject:** Indefinite (نَفْسٌ = any soul) — universalizing the consequence
- **Rhetorical purpose:** After 13 verses of cosmic spectacle, the conclusion is devastatingly understated. No emphasis particles are needed because the weight of the 12-verse buildup provides all the emphasis. The indefiniteness of "a soul" means every listener must apply this to themselves.

**Oath Jawab (Verse 19): إِنَّهُ لَقَوْلُ رَسُولٍ كَرِيمٍ**
- **Grammar:** Nominal sentence with إِنَّ + لَ double emphasis. إِنَّ governs the subject (هُ, accusative), لَ intensifies the predicate (قَوْلُ, nominative)
- **Structure:** Emphasis particle + nominal sentence (S-P pattern) with double emphasis
- **Subject:** Pronoun referring to the Quran — specific and definite
- **Rhetorical purpose:** The oaths (by stars, night, dawn) validate a specific claim that the audience might resist: the Quran is from a noble messenger. Double emphasis (إِنَّ + لَ) is needed precisely because this claim faces denial.

**Key contrast:** The إِذَا chain uses understatement (no emphasis particles, indefinite subject) because its power comes from the cumulative buildup. The oath sequence uses overstatement (double emphasis) because it must overcome active resistance. The grammar mirrors the rhetorical strategy: accumulated evidence needs no decoration, while contested claims need explicit reinforcement.
</ExerciseBox>

## Grammar Summary

<Callout type="note" title="Key Grammar Concepts in This Surah">
- **Extensive conditional chains**: 12 consecutive idha clauses (verses 1-13) with single apodosis (v.14) - after each idha the noun is the subject of an unspoken verb, explained by the passive verb that follows
- **Passive voice dominance**: Most idha clauses use passive verbs (kuwwirat, inkadarat, suyyirat, etc.) emphasizing divine agency
- **Oath constructions**: Oath sequence (verses 15-18): fa-la uqsimu bi- (a new sentence, with an extra la), then wa joins more things sworn by
- **Emphasis particles**: inna...la structure (v.19), wa-la-qad (v.23: wa, oath lam, qad), ma...bi negation (vv.22,24,25: ma works like laysa, bi is extra)
- **Restriction constructions**: in...illa hasr pattern (v.27)
- **Divine name attributes**: Multiple attributes for Gabriel (vv.19-21) demonstrating na't (adjective) agreement patterns
- **Interrogatives**: Powerful rhetorical questions (v.9: bi-ayyi dhanbin, v.26: fa-ayna tadhhabun)
- **Masdar mu'awwal**: an + subjunctive forming verbal nouns (vv.28-29)
- **Subordinate clauses**: Human will subordinated to divine will (v.29) - complex theological syntax
</Callout>
