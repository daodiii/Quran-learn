# Al-Ghashiyah (The Overwhelming)

## Overview

- **Revelation:** Meccan
- **Verses:** 26
- **Theme:** The overwhelming terror of Judgment Day, contrasting fates of believers and disbelievers, signs in creation pointing to the Creator
- **Grammar Focus:** Interrogative hal, nominal descriptive sentences with adjective agreement, active and passive participles as adjectives, a-fa-la interrogative-negative constructions, commands to reflect on creation

<Callout type="tip" title="What Makes Al-Ghashiyah Unique for Grammar Study">
This surah is one of the densest collections of active and passive participles in the entire Quran. Nearly every descriptor is a participle: khashi'ah, 'amilah, nasibah, hamiyah, aniyah, na'imah, radiyah, 'aliyah, laghiyah, jariyah (all active participles on the fa'ilah pattern; al-Jadwal notes that laghiyah may instead be a verbal noun) alongside marfu'ah, mawdu'ah, masfufah, mabthuthah (all passive participles on the maf'ulah pattern). The surah builds a powerful contrast between two sets of faces -- the humbled (vv.2-7) versus the delighted (vv.8-16) -- using parallel nominal structures. The four creation signs (camel, sky, mountains, earth in vv.17-20) employ an identical rhetorical framework with the rare a-fa-la interrogative-negative construction and passive kayfa clauses, making divine agency central while the created objects are grammatically passive recipients. Finally, the Prophet's role is defined through two opposing participles: mudhakkir (reminder, Form II active participle) versus musaytir (controller) -- he IS the first, he is NOT the second.
</Callout>

<GrammarTable>

<Callout type="rule" title="Rhetorical hal and the Active Participle al-Ghashiyah">
- hal ataka: al-Jadwal calls this hal a particle of suspense (tashwiq): it draws the listener in rather than asking for information. He also allows hal to mean qad (the story has indeed reached you), which makes it a statement
- al-ghashiyah: active participle from ghashiya (to overwhelm/cover) on the fa'ilah pattern -- the Day that overwhelms all
- The same opening, hal ataka hadithu ..., is found in 20:9, 51:24, 79:15 and 85:17
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The sentence is a verbal interrogative: hal (interrogative particle) + ataka (verb) + hadithu (subject/fa'il). The verb ataka includes the attached pronoun ka (you), functioning as the direct object (maf'ul bihi). Hadithu al-ghashiyah is an idafah construction where hadithu is the mudaf (nominative/marfu') and al-ghashiyah is the mudaf ilayhi (genitive/majrur). In al-Jadwal's reading hal is a particle of suspense (tashwiq) and the whole sentence is an opening sentence (ibtida'iyyah) with no place in the grammar of what follows.

**Sarf (Morphology):** al-ghashiyah is an active participle (ism fa'il) from the root gh-sh-y on the fa'ilah pattern. The ta marbuta marks feminine gender, agreeing with the implied feminine noun (al-sa'ah or al-qiyamah). ataka is from the defective root a-t-y (weak final radical ya), conjugated in the past tense with the alif maqsurah ending.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** Al-Jadwal reads hal here as a particle of suspense (tashwiq): it stirs interest before the story is told. He also allows hal to mean qad, which would make the line a statement: the story has indeed reached you. The choice of hadith (story/account) rather than khabar (news) suggests a narration that unfolds, creating dramatic buildup. Naming the Day al-ghashiyah (the Overwhelming) rather than yawm al-qiyamah sets the emotional register for the entire surah -- this is not just a day but an event that engulfs and overwhelms everything.

<GrammarTable>

|---|--------|----------------|------|------------|-------|---------|
| 1 | وُجُوهٌ | wujuhun | و ج ه | Noun - feminine, plural, indefinite | Subject (mubtada') - nominative (marfu'); its khabar is the sentence tasla in v.4 | faces |

<Callout type="rule" title="Nominal Sentence with Compound Adverb">
- wujuhun yawma'idhin khashi'atun: wujuhun is the subject and khashi'atun is its adjective (na't); the real predicate is the verb sentence tasla in v.4, so vv.2-4 are one long sentence
- yawma'idhin: compound adverb (yawma + idhin) -- "the day of then" = "that Day." The yawma is a zarf (adverbial of time) in the accusative, and idhin is in the genitive as mudaf ilayhi with tanwin standing for the omitted clause (idh takunu al-ghashiyah). Al-Jadwal says the whole word is accusative (or indeclinable) and attached to khashi'atun
- khashi'ah: active participle showing continuous state of humiliation -- not a momentary action but an enduring condition
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** This is a nominal sentence (jumlah ismiyyah). wujuhun is the mubtada'; it may open the sentence although it is indefinite, because it is described (by khashi'atun, 'amilatun, nasibatun). khashi'atun is a na't (adjective) of wujuhun, and yawma'idhin is a zarf attached to khashi'atun. The khabar is not khashi'atun: al-Jadwal takes the verb sentence tasla naran in v.4 as the khabar, and adds tusqa (v.5) and laysa lahum ta'amun (v.6) as the second and third khabar. He calls the whole an explanatory new sentence (isti'naf bayani). The indefiniteness of wujuhun also means "some faces" (not all), implying other faces with a different fate (introduced in v.8).

**Sarf (Morphology):** wujuhun is the broken plural of wajh (face), on the fu'ul pattern typical of triliteral nouns. khashi'atun is an active participle from khasha'a (to be humble/submissive) on the fa'ilah pattern, with the ta marbuta for feminine agreement with wujuh (a broken plural of things, which takes a feminine singular adjective).

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** Beginning with "faces" rather than "people" is a powerful metonymy (majaz mursal) -- faces are the most expressive part of the body, the site where humiliation is most visible. The indefinite wujuhun (without al) creates suspense: which faces? The reader must continue to find out. The active participle khashi'ah (rather than a verb like takhsha'u) presents humiliation as an inherent, ongoing quality of these faces, not a temporary event.

<Callout type="rule" title="Stacked Adjectives (Na't) of One Noun">
- Three adjectives (na't) for one noun (wujuhun): khashi'atun (v.2), 'amilatun, nasibatun (v.3) -- all describe the same faces at once. Al-Jadwal reads all three as na't, not as khabar
- 'amilah nasibah: working hard but to no avail (in Hellfire) -- the combination implies futile exertion, effort that produces only exhaustion
- Active participles describing continuous state -- these faces are perpetually laboring and perpetually weary
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** Both words are adjectives (na't) of the mubtada' wujuhun in v.2, like khashi'atun before them; al-Jadwal reads all three as na't. They are nominative with tanwin, agreeing with the indefinite noun they describe, and wujuhun still waits for its khabar, which comes in v.4.

**Sarf (Morphology):** Both are active participles on the fa'ilah pattern. 'amilah from the root 'a-m-l (to work/act) and nasibah from n-s-b (to toil/be weary). The fa'ilah pattern for both creates morphological unity, reinforcing that these are parallel descriptions of the same state.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The juxtaposition of 'amilah (working) and nasibah (exhausted) creates a devastating image: labor without reward, effort without relief. Classical scholars debate whether this refers to their futile worldly worship (working hard at wrong beliefs) or their punishment in the Hereafter (dragging chains, climbing only to fall). Either reading produces the same rhetorical effect: the tragic futility of misdirected effort. The asyndeton (no conjunction wa between the two words) makes them feel like a single compound description, inseparable.

<Callout type="tip" title="The Khabar Arrives: a Verbal Sentence">
- tasla naran: they enter/burn in fire -- the shift from the adjectives (khashi'ah, 'amilah, nasibah) to a verbal sentence adds dynamic action to the static description
- hamiyah: active participle from hamiya (to be intensely hot) -- the fire itself is described as an active agent of heat
- In al-Jadwal's reading this sentence is the khabar of wujuhun (v.2): the three words before it describe wujuhun, and the khabar finally arrives here
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The sentence shifts from nominal (the adjectives) to verbal (tasla), adding a dynamic element. The verb sentence tasla naran hamiyatan is the khabar of wujuhun (v.2). The verb tasla takes naran as its direct object (maf'ul bihi, accusative/mansub). hamiyatan is a sifah (adjective) modifying naran, also accusative to agree with it. The verb is 3rd person feminine singular (tasla with the alif maqsurah) and refers back to wujuh.

**Sarf (Morphology):** tasla from root s-l-y means to enter fire or be exposed to its heat. The present tense indicates ongoing action. hamiyah from root h-m-y on the fa'ilah pattern is an active participle describing the fire as an active source of intense heat -- not passively hot but actively burning.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The indefiniteness of naran (a fire, not THE fire) is significant: it suggests a fire beyond known description, one that defies specification. Modifying it with hamiyah (intensely hot) creates what rhetoricians call tabyin ba'd al-ibham (clarifying after leaving vague) -- the fire is specified only by its most terrifying quality. The unbroken chain of descriptors from v.2 through v.4 builds relentlessly: humbled, working, exhausted, burning -- each worse than the last.

<Callout type="rule" title="Passive Voice: Forced Action">
- tusqa: passive verb -- they are forcibly given drink (not choosing to drink, but compelled)
- 'aynin aniyatin: spring that has reached extreme boiling point -- the active participle aniyah shows the spring is actively at boiling temperature
- The passive voice emphasizes their helplessness: they cannot refuse this drink
- The sentence tusqa min 'aynin aniyatin is the second khabar of wujuhun (v.2), according to al-Jadwal; min 'aynin is attached to tusqa
</Callout>

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** tusqa is a passive verb (mabni li-l-majhul), where the original subject (the one who gives drink) is suppressed. The na'ib al-fa'il (deputy subject) is the hidden pronoun referring to wujuh. min 'aynin is a prepositional phrase indicating the source, and aniyatin is a sifah (adjective) for 'aynin, in the genitive to agree. The whole sentence is the second khabar of wujuhun (al-Jadwal).

**Sarf (Morphology):** tusqa is the passive of tasqi (Form I, root s-q-y). The passive is formed by changing the vowel pattern: ta-s-qi (active) becomes tu-s-qa (passive) with damma on the prefix and fatha before the final radical. aniyah from root a-n-y on the fa'ilah pattern means "having reached the utmost point of boiling."

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The shift to passive voice (from the active tasla in v.4) is rhetorically devastating: in v.4 they actively burn (some agency), but in v.5 they are passively given drink (no agency at all). The contrast between 'aynin aniyah (boiling spring) here and 'aynun jariyah (flowing spring) in v.12 creates an antithetical parallel that structures the entire surah's contrast between punishment and reward. Both describe springs, but one boils and the other flows pleasantly.

- laysa lahum ta'amun: negative existential sentence with the predicate (lahum) fronted before the subject (ta'amun) -- this inversion emphasizes their deprivation

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** laysa is one of the "sisters of kana" (incomplete verbs). Its predicate lahum (khabar laysa) is fronted before its subject ta'amun (ism laysa), which remains nominative despite the inversion. Al-Jadwal reads illa as a particle of restriction (hasr): there is no food for them except food from dari'. He also allows an exception (istithna'), where min dari'in describes the understood excepted food (illa ta'aman min dari'in, or the nominative ta'amun min dari'in). In neither case is min dari'in a badal. lahum is attached to the khabar of laysa, and the sentence laysa lahum ta'amun is the third khabar of wujuhun (v.2); the pronoun hum refers to the owners of the faces.

**Sarf (Morphology):** dari' from root d-r-' on the fa'il pattern is a noun describing a specific thorny plant (identified as dried shubrum or shibriq) that is poisonous and inedible. ta'am from root t-'-m on the fa'al pattern is the general word for food/sustenance.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The rhetorical brilliance of this verse lies in the false exception: the "except" (illa) creates an expectation of relief ("they have no food... except..."), but what follows is worse than having nothing at all. Al-Jadwal gives two readings of the rhetoric. One: it is a kinaya or majaz for food that even camels dislike, so it does not clash with the zaqqum or ghislin of other verses. Two: it means they have no food at all, since dari' is not food even for animals, as one says "he has no shade except the sun".

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** Al-Jadwal calls the second clause a completion (tatmim): without wa-la yughni min ju'in, one might think dari' still satisfies hunger even though it does not fatten. The double negation (la...wa-la) is a comprehensive denial covering both aspects of sustenance: physical nourishment (yusminu) and hunger satisfaction (yughni). These represent the two basic functions of food: building the body and relieving hunger. By negating both with causative verbs, the verse says dari' fails to produce even the smallest effect in either direction. The present tense of both verbs indicates this is a permanent, ongoing reality -- it will never nourish and never satisfy.

This is the first passive participle in the Paradise description (vv.8-16), marking a shift from active participles (na'imah, radiyah, 'aliyah, jariyah) to passive participles (marfu'ah, mawdu'ah, masfufah, mabthuthah). surur is a broken plural of sarir on the fu'ul pattern.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The shift from active to passive participles at v.13 is significant: the people and natural features of Paradise are described with active participles (they actively experience delight, the springs actively flow), but the furnishings are described with passive participles (raised, placed, arranged, spread) -- they are things that were set up (the participle does not say by whom). This grammatical distinction separates the living from the arranged, the experiencer from the experienced.

**Sarf (Morphology):** khuliqat is the passive (mabni li-l-majhul) of khalaqa. **Balagha (Rhetoric):** The sudden shift from Paradise and Hell to camels, sky, mountains, and earth is a masterstroke of Quranic rhetoric. After describing unseen realities (the afterlife), the surah pivots to visible signs that prove those unseen realities. The camel is often said to be listed first because it was the most important animal in Arabian life -- the audience's most familiar sign of divine power. The passive voice khuliqat removes human agency entirely: the focus is not on WHO created (that is assumed) but HOW the creation was accomplished -- its engineering, its design, its perfection.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The verse contains two clauses: (1) fa-dhakkir -- an imperative clause whose fa ties it to an unspoken condition ("if the disbelievers do not take the lesson from the signs of God's power, remind them"); the clause is the answer to that condition, and (2) innama anta mudhakkirun -- a nominal sentence with innama (inna + ma kaffa: the ma stops inna from working, so anta stays nominative). The innama clause provides the reason (ta'lil) for the command: "remind, BECAUSE you are only a reminder." The nominal sentence structure (anta + mudhakkirun) makes the Prophet's identity as a reminder a permanent, defining characteristic.

**Sarf (Morphology):** dhakkir is a Form II imperative from root dh-k-r. Form II (fa''ala) carries an intensive/causative meaning: dhakara means "to remember," while dhakkara means "to cause to remember / to remind." mudhakkir is the Form II active participle (mufa''il pattern), meaning "one who reminds." The cognate relationship between the imperative dhakkir and the participle mudhakkir is morphologically transparent: same root, same form, one a verb and one a noun.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The use of innama (only/merely) is theologically precise: it simultaneously affirms one role (reminding) and negates all others (controlling, compelling, judging). The cognate pair dhakkir/mudhakkir creates what rhetoricians call jinasmu'tariz (paronomasia) -- using two forms of the same root for emphasis. The effect is: "Your entire being is summed up in this one act: reminding." In al-Jadwal's reading the fa at the beginning answers an unspoken condition: if they still do not take the lesson from the signs, remind them anyway.

This prioritizes Allah's sovereignty over human agency. The verse transitions from the Prophet's limited role (vv.21-22) to Allah's unlimited authority (vv.25-26): the Prophet only reminds, but Allah has final jurisdiction.

| 6-7 | laysa lahum ta'amun illa min dari'in | Negative clause with restriction (third khabar); v.7 describes dari' | Deprivation |

**Delighted Faces (vv.8-16):**

| Verse | Description | Grammar | Type |
|-------|-------------|---------|------|
| 8 | na'imatun (delighted) | Active participle, na't of wujuhun | Internal state |
| 9 | radiyatun (satisfied) | Active participle, khabar of wujuhun | Emotional reward |
| 10 | fi jannatin 'aliyatin | Prepositional phrase, second khabar | Location |
| 11 | la tasma'u fiha laghiyatan | Verbal clause, negation; describes jannatin | Purity of environment |
| 12 | fiha 'aynun jariyatun | Inverted nominal sentence; describes jannatin | Water (flowing) |
| 13 | fiha sururun marfu'atun | Inverted nominal sentence; describes jannatin | Furniture (raised) |
| 14 | akwabun mawdu'atun | Noun joined to sururun, with its na't | Drinks (placed) |
| 15 | namariqu masfufatun | Noun joined to sururun, with its na't | Comfort (arranged) |
| 16 | zarabiyyu mabthuthatun | Noun joined to sururun, with its na't | Luxury (spread) |

**Grammar of Contrast:**
1. **Length:** Punishment gets 6 verses; reward gets 9 verses -- grace exceeds wrath
2. **Participle types:** Punishment uses only active participles (victims actively suffering); reward uses active then passive (active bliss + passively prepared luxury)
3. **Springs:** aniyah (boiling, v.5) vs. jariyah (flowing, v.12) -- identical grammar (active participle modifying 'ayn), opposite meaning
4. **Effort:** 'amilatun nasibatun (working, exhausted -- futile) vs. li-sa'yiha radiyatun (for effort, satisfied -- rewarded)
5. **Agency:** Punishment includes passive verbs (tusqa -- forced to drink); reward includes passive participles (objects prepared for comfort)

<ExerciseBox question="Verses 17-20 use four parallel passive-voice constructions to present signs in creation. For each verse, (a) identify the passive verb and its active form, (b) explain why passive voice was chosen instead of active, and (c) explain how the specific verb matches its subject uniquely.">

| Verse | Subject | Passive Verb | Active Form | Root |
|-------|---------|-------------|-------------|------|
| 17 | al-ibil (camels) | khuliqat (was created) | khalaqa (created) | خ ل ق |
| 18 | as-sama' (sky) | rufi'at (was raised) | rafa'a (raised) | ر ف ع |
| 19 | al-jibal (mountains) | nusibat (were erected) | nasaba (erected) | ن ص ب |
| 20 | al-ard (earth) | sutihat (was spread) | sataha (spread) | س ط ح |

**(a) Passive formation:** Each follows the same morphological transformation: the active fa'ala pattern (fatha-fatha) becomes the passive fu'ila pattern (damma-kasra): khalaqa becomes khuliqat, rafa'a becomes rufi'at, nasaba becomes nusibat, sataha becomes sutihat.

**(b) Why passive voice?**
- The passive leaves the agent (fa'il) unnamed. One reading is that the EVIDENCE (the creation) is the focus, not the CREATOR.
- The listener is meant to OBSERVE the creation and DEDUCE the Creator -- passive voice forces this intellectual engagement.
- If active voice were used ("Did they not see how Allah created the camels?"), the conclusion would be handed to them. The passive makes them work for it -- matching the surah's theme that the Prophet is a "reminder" (mudhakkir), not a controller (musaytir).

**(c) Verb-subject matching:**
- **khuliqat (created) + camels:** "Created" is the broadest verb -- appropriate for a living creature whose entire biological design (humps, eyelashes, padded feet, water storage) is a marvel of engineering. No more specific verb captures the totality of the camel's design.
- **rufi'at (raised) + sky:** "Raised" perfectly describes the sky's defining characteristic -- it is ABOVE, lifted without visible pillars. You cannot say the sky was "erected" (that implies solidity) or "spread" (that implies flatness).
- **nusibat (erected) + mountains:** "Erected" captures the vertical, solid, planted nature of mountains -- they stand upright like columns driven into the earth. You cannot say mountains were "raised" (they don't float) or "spread" (they are vertical).
- **sutihat (spread) + earth:** "Spread out" describes the earth's defining characteristic for its inhabitants -- its surface is extended flat for habitation. You cannot say the earth was "raised" or "erected."

Each verb is irreplaceable: swapping any two would produce an inaccurate or absurd description.
</ExerciseBox>

## Grammar Summary

<Callout type="note" title="Key Grammar Concepts in This Surah">
- **Interrogative hal**: Opening of v.1; al-Jadwal calls it a hal of suspense (tashwiq) and also allows the meaning qad
- **Nominal descriptive sentences**: One subject (wujuhun) with stacked adjectives (khashi'atun 'amilatun nasibatun) and a khabar that arrives late (tasla in v.4, then tusqa and laysa lahum as further khabars); the second group (vv.8-16) follows the same plan
- **Participles as adjectives**: Almost every adjective is an active or passive participle showing a lasting state (khashi'ah, 'amilah, nasibah, hamiyah, na'imah, radiyah, etc.); only al-ghashiyah (v.1) is used as a noun
- **Inverted nominal sentences**: Predicate before subject (vv.12-13: fiha 'aynun jariyah, fiha sururun marfu'ah, with the nouns of vv.14-16 joined to sururun; vv.25-26: inna ilayna iyabahum)
- **Passive voice constructions**: Divine agency emphasized (khuliqat, rufi'at, nusibat, sutihat in vv.17-20)
- **Parallel structures**: Contrasting descriptions of two groups (vv.2-7 vs. 8-16); four creation signs (vv.17-20)
- **A-fa-la interrogative-negative**: A question of rebuke (v.17: a-fa-la yanzurun), with the fa joining an understood sentence -- "Then do they not...?"; each kayfa clause (vv.17-20) is a badal ishtimal
- **Adjective clauses (na't jumla)**: vv.11-13 describe jannatin (v.10) in turn; v.7 describes dari' (v.6)
- **Fa in two uses**: the fa of v.21 answers an unspoken condition; the fa of v.24 joins an understood sentence
- **Ellipsis**: Repeated verb omitted in parallel constructions (vv.18-20: wa-ila as-sama'i implied yanzurun)
- **Restriction with innama** (v.21): inna + the stopping ma, confining the meaning -- "only a reminder"
- **Emphatic negation**: laysa + bi construction (v.22: lasta 'alayhim bi-musaytirin)
- **Inna with inverted structure**: Verses 25-26 use inna with predicate before subject for rhetorical emphasis
- **Sequential particles**: thumma (v.26) indicating sequence -- first return, then reckoning
- **Form II verbs**: yu'adhdhib (v.24), dhakkir (v.21)
- **Absolute object (maf'ul mutlaq)**: al-'adhaba al-akbara (v.24) -- emphasizing the punishment itself
</Callout>
