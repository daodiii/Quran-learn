# Al-Fajr (The Dawn)

## Overview

- **Revelation:** Meccan
- **Verses:** 30
- **Theme:** This surah opens with a series of oaths by dawn, ten nights, and other phenomena, followed by warnings about the fate of ancient nations ('Ad, Thamud, and Pharaoh) who were destroyed for their arrogance. It emphasizes the test of wealth and poverty, and concludes with the peace of the tranquil soul.
- **Grammar Focus:** Series of oath constructions with وَ (wa), interrogative particles (هَلْ), conditional constructions with إِذَا (idha), the rebuke particle كَلَّا (kalla), narrative past tense verbs, relative pronouns and clauses

<Callout type="tip" title="A Grammar Journey from Cosmic Oaths to Divine Invitation">
Al-Fajr is structured as a grammatical descent from the cosmic to the personal. It opens with four oath constructions (verses 1-4) — all in the genitive, the first after the oath particle وَ and the others joined to it — then pivots through narrative past tense (verses 6-13) describing destroyed nations, shifts to conditional أَمَّا...فَ constructions (verses 15-16) diagnosing human psychology, delivers a rebuke with كَلَّا (verses 17-20) using present tense accusations, builds to an eschatological scene with passive verbs and repeated maf'ul mutlaq (verses 21-26), and finally resolves in the most intimate grammar the Quran offers: a direct feminine vocative يَا أَيَّتُهَا followed by feminine imperatives (ارْجِعِي، ادْخُلِي). The surah's grammar mirrors its theology — from impersonal oaths to God addressing a single soul by name.
</Callout>

| 1 | وَالْفَجْرِ | wa-l-fajri | ف-ج-ر | Noun - masculine, singular, definite | Oath particle wa (harf qasam) + noun in genitive (majrur) after it; the phrase is tied to an unspoken verb, "I swear" | and the dawn |

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The verse is the opening of a qasam (oath) construction. The وَ functions as harf al-qasam (oath particle), not as a conjunction. It governs the following noun الْفَجْرِ in the genitive case (majrur with kasra). al-Jadwal ties the phrase to an unspoken verb, "I swear" (aqsimu). The jawab al-qasam (answer to the oath) is not spoken in al-Jadwal's reading; it gives something like "I will repay every person for what he did". al-Jadwal notes that some scholars make verse 14 (inna rabbaka la-bi-l-mirsad) the answer. It reads verse 5 as a new sentence, not as the answer.

**Sarf (Morphology):** الْفَجْرِ derives from the root ف-ج-ر on the fa'l pattern, carrying meanings of splitting open and breaking forth — dawn "splits" the darkness. The definite article الـ makes it refer to the phenomenon of dawn in general, not a specific morning.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** Opening with a cosmic oath immediately elevates the discourse. Dawn is chosen because it represents the transition from darkness to light — mirroring the surah's progression from the darkness of human corruption (verses 15-20) to the light of divine invitation (verses 27-30). The single-word verse creates dramatic brevity, building anticipation for what follows.

| 1 | وَ | wa | - | Particle - conjunction | Not declinable (mabni), joining wa: it joins لَيَالٍ to الْفَجْرِ (al-Darwish parses it this way) | and |
| 2 | لَيَالٍ | layaalin | ل-ي-ل | Noun - feminine, plural, indefinite | Genitive (majrur) because it is joined to الْفَجْرِ, with tanwin kasratayn (the ya' has dropped) | nights |
| 3 | عَشْرٍ | 'ashrin | ع-ش-ر | Numeral - ten, feminine form (no ة), indefinite | Adjective (na't) of لَيَالٍ, genitive (majrur), with tanwin kasratayn | ten |
</GrammarTable>


#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The وَ here is a joining wa (wa al-'atf), not a second oath particle: the oath particle was in verse 1, and لَيَالٍ is genitive because it is joined to الْفَجْرِ. (This follows al-Darwish.) عَشْرٍ is the na't (adjective) of لَيَالٍ, agreeing in case (genitive) and definiteness (both indefinite with tanwin). The form عَشْرٍ has no ة because numbers 3-10 reverse gender: a feminine counted noun like لَيَالٍ takes the form without ة. The indefiniteness is notable — the oath swears by "ten nights" without specifying which, though scholars identify them as the first ten of Dhul-Hijjah.

**Sarf (Morphology):** لَيَالٍ is the broken plural of لَيْلَة on the irregular فَعَالٍ pattern. The original form would be لَيَالِيَ, but the final ya' drops and tanwin replaces it in the indefinite genitive. Other plurals of this pattern with a weak last letter, like جَوَارٍ, do the same.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** Shifting from the definite الْفَجْرِ (the dawn) to the indefinite لَيَالٍ عَشْرٍ (ten nights) creates variety in the oath series. The indefiniteness serves ta'zim (magnification) — these are nights so sacred they need no specification. The juxtaposition of dawn (singular moment) with ten nights (extended period) expands the temporal scope of the oaths.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** Both وَ are joining wa (wa al-'atf), not oath particles: the oath particle was in verse 1. الشَّفْعِ is joined to الْفَجْرِ and الْوَتْرِ is joined to it, so both are genitive (majrur). (This follows al-Darwish.) The pair together covers all numbers (every number is either even or odd).

**Sarf (Morphology):** الشَّفْعِ from root ش-ف-ع on the fa'l pattern means "pair/even" — the same root gives شَفَاعَة (intercession, where one person "pairs" with another). al-Jadwal adds that الشَّفْعِ may also be a figure of speech for "creation", and lists other meanings the scholars gave (prayers, levels of Paradise, the day of sacrifice, the ten days of Dhul-Hijjah). الْوَتْرِ from root و-ت-ر on the fa'l pattern means "single/odd" — it also carries a sense of uniqueness and aloneness.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** Swearing by "the even and the odd" encompasses totality — all of creation falls into pairs or singularity. This has been interpreted as referring to prayer units (even and odd rak'at), all of creation (paired) versus Allah (One), or the days of Dhul-Hijjah. The abstract quality of these oaths elevates the discourse beyond physical phenomena into mathematical and metaphysical truth.

<GrammarTable>
| # | Arabic | Transliteration | Root | Morphology | I'rab | Meaning |
|---|--------|----------------|------|------------|-------|---------|
| 1 | وَ | wa | - | Particle - conjunction | Not declinable (mabni), joining wa: it joins الَّيْلِ to what comes before (al-Darwish parses it this way) | and |
| 2 | الَّيْلِ | al-layli | ل-ي-ل | Noun - masculine, singular, definite | Genitive (majrur) because it is joined to الْفَجْرِ | the night |
| 3 | إِذَا | idha | - | Particle - conditional/temporal | Not declinable (mabni), a time word (adverb) in the accusative position, with no condition in it here (al-Jadwal); tied to the unspoken verb "I swear"; the clause after it is its muḍāf ilayhi | when |

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The وَ is again a joining wa, and الَّيْلِ is genitive because it is joined to الْفَجْرِ. al-Jadwal reads إِذَا as a time word (zharf) in the accusative position, with no condition in it here, tied to the unspoken verb "I swear": swearing not just by the night, but by the night in a specific state. The clause يَسْرِ is the muḍāf ilayhi of إِذَا, so it is in the genitive position. يَسْرِ is a present tense verb (fi'l mudari') whose subject is the hidden pronoun هُوَ referring to الَّيْل. The verb is shortened from يَسْرِي: al-Jadwal says the final ya' dropped to suit the rhyme of the verse endings (فَوَاصِل) and the well-known readings. The ḍamma that shows the indicative is unwritten on the dropped ya'.

**Sarf (Morphology):** يَسْرِي derives from root س-ر-ي on the Form I present pattern يَفْعِل, meaning "to travel/flow by night." The verb is specifically about nighttime movement — it is the root of إِسْرَاء (the Night Journey). The deletion of the final ya' (حذف الياء) is a common Quranic feature in verse-final positions.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The night is personified as a traveler — it "passes" or "flows" rather than simply existing. This anthropomorphism (majāz) gives the night agency and movement, connecting it to the theme of time passing and accountability arriving. The oath series now spans four phenomena: dawn (beginning), nights (duration), even/odd (structure), and the passing night (motion) — a comprehensive temporal framework.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** هَلْ opens a question that is meant to confirm and affirm (al-Jadwal): "Is there not in that a sufficient oath?" The sentence is a new sentence (isti'naf) with no place in the grammar of the oath before it. It is nominal with a delayed mubtada': the prepositional phrase فِي ذَٰلِكَ is tied to a fronted khabar (predicate), and قَسَمٌ is the mubtada' (subject). لِذِي حِجْرٍ is a prepositional phrase tied to an adjective of قَسَمٌ, where ذِي is one of the الأسماء الخمسة (five nouns) showing genitive case through the long vowel ي. حِجْرٍ is the second part of the idafa.

**Sarf (Morphology):** قَسَمٌ from root ق-س-م on the fa'al pattern is a masdar meaning "oath/swearing." ذِي from ذُو is one of the five special nouns that show case through their final letter: ذُو (nominative), ذَا (accusative), ذِي (genitive). حِجْرٍ from root ح-ج-ر on the fi'l pattern literally means "barrier/restraint" — intelligence is called حِجْر because it restrains a person from foolishness.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** This verse closes the oath sequence by addressing the listener directly — after four cosmic oaths, the question challenges: "Is this not enough for someone with intellect?" The intellect is called حِجْر because it restrains a person from what is not right, so the word hints that only someone who restrains himself will take note of these oaths. The rhetorical question turns the oath from a statement into a challenge.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** أَلَمْ is a compound of the interrogative أَ and the negative/jussive particle لَمْ, creating a negative rhetorical question expecting an affirmative answer ("Have you not seen?" = "You have certainly seen"). تَرَ is in the jussive mood (majzum) after لَمْ, apocopated from تَرَى — the alif maqsura drops as the jussive marker. The seeing is with the heart, which means knowing (al-Jadwal), so تَرَ takes two objects. كَيْفَ is a question word (a noun, not a particle). al-Jadwal reads it as a ḥāl with فَعَلَ as its governing word, and also allows a maf'ul mutlaq ("what great deed did He do"). Because of the question word, تَرَ cannot act directly on the clause (ta'liq), and the clause فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ fills the place of both its objects. بِعَادٍ is tied to فَعَلَ, and عَادٍ takes full tanwin in the genitive.

**Sarf (Morphology):** تَرَ derives from root ر-أ-ي, a doubly-weak verb (hamza + ya'). The jussive تَرَ shows the loss of the final weak letter in jussive mood. فَعَلَ is the quintessential Form I verb — the root itself (ف-ع-ل) is used as the pattern template for all Arabic verb forms.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The shift from cosmic oaths (verses 1-5) to historical narrative marks a new section. أَلَمْ تَرَ addresses the Prophet personally and asks him to see the events with the heart, as if they were in front of him. The use of رَبُّكَ ("your Lord") rather than اللَّه ties the Prophet's own Lord to the punishment of the tyrants.

**Nahw (Syntax):** فَ is a joining fa (fa' al-'atf): the clause صَبَّ is joined to the clause أَكْثَرُوا, and the punishment follows the corruption in time. صَبَّ is a past tense verb, and عَلَيْهِمْ is tied to it. The word order is striking: verb (صَبَّ) → prepositional phrase (عَلَيْهِمْ) → subject (رَبُّكَ) → object (سَوْطَ عَذَابٍ). The subject is delayed after the verb and the prepositional phrase, a marked word order (ta'khir al-fa'il). سَوْطَ عَذَابٍ is an idafa construction functioning as the maf'ul bihi (direct object, accusative).

**Sarf (Morphology):** صَبَّ from root ص-ب-ب is a doubled verb (the second and third radicals are identical). It means "to pour out, unleash." سَوْطَ from root س-و-ط on the fa'l pattern literally means "whip/lash"; al-Jadwal calls it a plain (non-derived) noun for a strip of leather used to flog, and says it is used here for the punishment itself (a figure of speech, majaz mursal). عَذَابٍ from root ع-ذ-ب is a masdar meaning punishment/torment.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** al-Jadwal reads صَبَّ as a figure of speech (isti'ara makniyya): the punishment is pictured as something poured, because it came down so fast, though "pour" is a word for water. Calling what was sent a سَوْط ("whip") shows that, however great it was, it is like a whip next to what is prepared for them in the next life. The delayed subject رَبُّكَ builds suspense — the listener first hears the pouring, then the targets, and only then the source: your Lord. The personal address (your Lord) reminds the Prophet that the same God who protects him destroyed tyrants.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** The first فَ is resumptive (isti'naf): it opens a new sentence. أَمَّا is a particle of condition and breaking down (shart wa-tafsil): it sorts the cases of the human. In meaning it stands for "whatever there is, then the human says…". الْإِنسَانُ is the mubtada' (subject, nominative), and the clause يَقُولُ is its khabar, in the nominative position. إِذَا مَا is a conditional time word with an extra مَا that has no job. al-Jadwal says the whole إِذَا condition, with its verb and its answer, is a parenthetical (i'tiradiyya) that is placed early and is meant to come later. ابْتَلَاهُ رَبُّهُ (verb + object + subject) is the muḍāf ilayhi of إِذَا, so it is in the genitive position. The next two verbs, أَكْرَمَهُ and نَعَّمَهُ, are joined to ابْتَلَاهُ رَبُّهُ by فَ and وَ, so they also sit in the genitive position. The second فَ, in فَيَقُولُ, ties the answer to أَمَّا. **Sarf (Morphology):** ابْتَلَاهُ is Form VIII (ifta'ala) from root ب-ل-و, meaning "to test/try." Form VIII adds a reflexive or intensified meaning. أَكْرَمَهُ is Form IV (af'ala) from root ك-ر-م (to honor). نَعَّمَهُ is Form II (fa''ala) from root ن-ع-م (to bless/favor) — Form II's doubled middle radical intensifies the action. The shift between Forms (VIII → IV → II) within one verse demonstrates Arabic's morphological richness.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** This verse begins the surah's psychological analysis. The أَمَّا...فَ construction creates a case study: "Take the human — when tested with blessings, he says 'My Lord has honored me.'" The irony is devastating: the human mistakes the test (ابْتَلَى) for a verdict (أَكْرَمَ). God tests; the human interprets. The parallel with verse 16 creates a diagnostic pair revealing how humans misread both wealth and poverty.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** وَ joins this sentence to لَا تُكْرِمُونَ from verse 17, and لَا is again a negative particle. The clause لَا تَحَاضُّونَ has no place in the grammar (al-Jadwal). تَحَاضُّونَ is a present tense verb (second person masculine plural) negated by لَا. عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ الْمِسْكِينِ is tied to تَحَاضُّونَ and says what they do not urge each other to do. طَعَامِ is in idafa with الْمِسْكِينِ, both genitive.

**Sarf (Morphology):** تَحَاضُّونَ derives from root ح-ض-ض on Form VI (tafā'ula), which indicates mutual/reciprocal action: "to urge each other, to mutually encourage." The original is تَتَحَاضُّونَ; one ت dropped (al-Jadwal). The doubled ض (shaddah) comes from the root's identical second and third radicals. طَعَام is, in al-Jadwal, a noun of the act standing for إِطْعَام ("feeding"); if it means "food", al-Jadwal adds an unspoken word before it, "giving the food of the poor". الْمِسْكِينِ from root س-ك-ن on the mif'il pattern means "one rendered still/immobile by poverty" — the inability to move or act due to destitution.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The accusation escalates: not only do they fail to feed the poor themselves (which would be bad enough), they do not even encourage each other to do so. Form VI (mutual action) makes the failure communal — it is a collective social breakdown, not just individual neglect.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The verb أَكَلَ (to eat) is used for taking inheritance: it is taken and used up like food. The maf'ul mutlaq أَكْلًا لَّمًّا amplifies the greed: not just eating, but "a devouring that gathers everything." The verse follows the orphan theme (v.17): the accusation moves from neglect (not honoring orphans) to greed for inherited wealth.

The maf'ul mutlaq + adjective pattern mirrors verse 19 (أَكْلًا لَّمًّا), creating structural parallelism between the two verses: greedy consuming and excessive loving are twin symptoms of the same disease. The four-verse accusation (17-20) forms a complete diagnosis: neglect of orphans → neglect of the poor → consuming inheritance → loving wealth. Each step is deeper than the last, moving from omission to commission to the underlying spiritual disease.

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The second كَلَّا turns from the worldly accusations to the Day itself. The repeated دَكًّا دَكًّا is a vivid sound device: the hard كّ sound repeated four times mimics the pounding of the earth. The repetition is emphatic — not just pounding, but pounding upon pounding. The earth they fought over, consumed inheritance from, and loved wealth upon is itself being destroyed.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** جِيءَ is the passive past tense of جَاءَ (to come), used here with بِ for "to bring". In the passive voice, the phrase بِجَهَنَّمَ takes the place of the subject (na'ib al-fa'il). The clause جِيءَ بِجَهَنَّمَ is joined to دُكَّتِ, so it is also a muḍāf ilayhi of إِذَا. The first يَوْمَئِذٍ is a time word (zarf zaman, يَوْمَ + إِذٍ "that day"); al-Jadwal reads it as a badal of إِذَا from verse 21, and the second يَوْمَئِذٍ as its confirmation (tawkid). يَتَذَكَّرُ الْإِنسَانُ is a verbal sentence (verb + fa'il), and it is the answer to إِذَا. The وَ in وَأَنَّىٰ is a ḥāl wa in al-Jadwal's first reading (it also allows a parenthetical wa): the clause أَنَّىٰ لَهُ الذِّكْرَىٰ describes his state, because he remembers while remembering no longer helps him. أَنَّىٰ is an interrogative noun meaning "how/from where"; it and لَهُ are tied to a fronted khabar, and الذِّكْرَىٰ is the delayed mubtada'.

**Sarf (Morphology):** جِيءَ shows the passive voweling of the hollow verb جَاءَ (ji'a instead of ja'a). يَتَذَكَّرُ is Form V (tafa''ala) from root ذ-ك-ر, meaning "to remember, reflect, take heed." Form V adds reflexive meaning to Form II. الذِّكْرَىٰ is a masdar/noun from the same root on the fi'la pattern with alif maqsura, meaning "remembrance, reminder."

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The verse has two parts. First, Hell is brought — the passive voice (جِيءَ) leaves the one who brings it unnamed. Then the human finally remembers — يَتَذَكَّرُ, the very thing he failed to do in life. The rhetorical question أَنَّىٰ لَهُ الذِّكْرَىٰ asks: how can remembrance help him now? The repetition of يَوْمَئِذٍ stresses the temporal finality — on THAT day, too late.

After returning, then enter. ادْخُلِي is a Form I feminine imperative from root د-خ-ل, with hamzat al-wasl and the ي feminine suffix as its subject. فِي عِبَادِي is tied to ادْخُلِي: al-Jadwal reads an unspoken word before عِبَادِي, "in the company of My servants". عِبَادِي is a noun in idafa with the first person pronoun يَ (My servants), genitive after فِي.

**Sarf (Morphology):** ادْخُلِي follows the same formation as ارْجِعِي — Form I imperative with hamzat al-wasl and feminine ي. عِبَادِ from root ع-ب-د is the broken plural of عَبْد (servant) on the fi'al pattern. The first person possessive يَ (My) creates a possessive construction: "My servants."

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The order of the commands is: return to your Lord (v.28), enter among His servants (v.29), then enter His Paradise (v.30). The soul first joins the community of the righteous, then enters Paradise. The possessive عِبَادِي ("MY servants") is deeply honorific: God claims ownership and pride in these servants. Being counted among God's servants is itself a reward, even before Paradise is mentioned.

#### Nahw-Sarf-Balagha Synthesis

**Nahw (Syntax):** وَ joins this command to the earlier commands: al-Jadwal says the clause is joined to the answer to the call (verse 27), like the one in verse 29. ادْخُلِي is repeated from verse 29 — same feminine imperative form. جَنَّتِي is the maf'ul bihi (direct object, accusative) — the verb دَخَلَ can take a direct object meaning "enter a place." The possessive يَ (My) creates an idafa: "My Paradise."

**Sarf (Morphology):** جَنَّتِي from root ج-ن-ن on the fa'lah pattern means "garden/paradise" — the root carries the meaning of concealment and covering (a garden is concealed by its trees). The first person possessive يَ makes it an intimate, direct gift from God: not "the Paradise" but "MY Paradise."

**Balagha (Rhetoric):** The surah ends with two words of great simplicity: وَادْخُلِي جَنَّتِي. After oaths, destruction, moral diagnosis, and eschatological terror, the conclusion is an invitation of ultimate gentleness. The possessive جَنَّتِي ("My Paradise") closes the surah: God claims Paradise as His own and offers it as a personal gift. The three steps (return → join My servants → enter My Paradise) mirror a homecoming: come back, rejoin your family, enter your true home. The surah that opened with cosmic oaths by dawn ends with a personal address — from the grandeur of the universe to the tenderness of divine invitation.

## Practice Exercises

<ExerciseBox question="This surah uses two أَمَّا...فَ constructions in verses 15-16 to diagnose a human flaw. Identify the grammatical components of each construction (the topic, the condition, and the result), and explain how the parallel grammar reveals the theological point.">

**Verse 15: فَأَمَّا الْإِنسَانُ إِذَا مَا ابْتَلَاهُ رَبُّهُ فَأَكْرَمَهُ وَنَعَّمَهُ فَيَقُولُ رَبِّي أَكْرَمَنِ**
- **Topic (mubtada'):** الْإِنسَانُ (the human)
- **Condition:** إِذَا مَا ابْتَلَاهُ رَبُّهُ فَأَكْرَمَهُ وَنَعَّمَهُ (when his Lord tests him by honoring and favoring him). al-Jadwal reads the clause after إِذَا as its muḍāf ilayhi and the condition as a parenthetical; its answer is left out because the answer to أَمَّا shows it.
- **Result (jawab amma):** فَيَقُولُ رَبِّي أَكْرَمَنِ (he says "My Lord has honored me"). The clause يَقُولُ is the khabar of الْإِنسَانُ, and the فَ ties it to أَمَّا.

**Verse 16: وَأَمَّا إِذَا مَا ابْتَلَاهُ فَقَدَرَ عَلَيْهِ رِزْقَهُ فَيَقُولُ رَبِّي أَهَانَنِ**
- **Topic:** the subject of يَقُولُ is an unspoken هُوَ, the human from v.15
- **Condition:** إِذَا مَا ابْتَلَاهُ فَقَدَرَ عَلَيْهِ رِزْقَهُ (when He tests him by restricting his provision)
- **Result:** فَيَقُولُ رَبِّي أَهَانَنِ (he says "My Lord has humiliated me")

**Theological point through grammar:** Both verses share identical structure — the ONLY differences are the middle verb (أَكْرَمَ/قَدَرَ) and the human's conclusion (أَكْرَمَنِ/أَهَانَنِ). The identical grammar exposes the identical flaw: both responses are wrong. Both are tests (ابْتَلَاهُ), not verdicts. The human confuses testing with judgment because he measures God's favor by material outcomes. The parallel grammar makes the error structurally visible.

</ExerciseBox>

<ExerciseBox question="Trace the use of the maf'ul mutlaq (cognate accusative) across verses 19-21 and 25-26. For each instance, identify the verb-masdar pair, state whether it includes an adjective, and explain what rhetorical effect the maf'ul mutlaq creates in each case.">

**Verse 19: وَتَأْكُلُونَ التُّرَاثَ أَكْلًا لَّمًّا**
- **Verb:** تَأْكُلُونَ (root أ-ك-ل) | **Masdar:** أَكْلًا (same root)
- **Adjective:** لَّمًّا (devouring/greedy)
- **Effect:** Intensifies the verb — not just eating but a specific KIND of eating: greedy, all-consuming

**Verse 20: وَتُحِبُّونَ الْمَالَ حُبًّا جَمًّا**
- **Verb:** تُحِبُّونَ (root ح-ب-ب) | **Noun of the act (ism masdar):** حُبًّا (same root)
- **Adjective:** جَمًّا (excessive/immense)
- **Effect:** Intensifies — not just love but excessive, all-encompassing love

**Verse 21: دُكَّتِ الْأَرْضُ دَكًّا دَكًّا**
- **Verb:** دُكَّتِ (root د-ك-ك) | **Masdar:** دَكًّا (same root)
- **Adjective:** None — instead, the masdar is REPEATED
- **Effect:** Repetition creates extreme emphasis — pounding upon pounding, relentless destruction

**Verse 25: لَّا يُعَذِّبُ عَذَابَهُ أَحَدٌ**
- **Verb:** يُعَذِّبُ (root ع-ذ-ب) | **Noun of the act (ism masdar):** عَذَابَ (same root)
- **Adjective:** None — instead, possessive pronoun هُ (His)
- **Effect:** The possessive makes it comparative — "none punishes HIS punishment" = none can match it

**Verse 26: وَلَا يُوثِقُ وَثَاقَهُ أَحَدٌ**
- **Verb:** يُوثِقُ (root و-ث-ق) | **Noun of the act (ism masdar):** وَثَاقَ (same root)
- **Adjective:** None — possessive pronoun هُ (His)
- **Effect:** Parallel to v.25 — "none binds HIS binding" = unmatched restraint

**Pattern:** The maf'ul mutlaq progresses from human vices (greedy eating, excessive love) to eschatological events (earth being pounded) to divine exclusivity (God's unique punishment and binding). The grammar escalates with the theme.

</ExerciseBox>

## Grammar Summary

<Callout type="note" title="Key Grammar Concepts in This Surah">

- **Oath constructions with وَ**: Verse 1 has the oath particle وَ (wa) governing the genitive case, a hallmark of Meccan surahs. In verses 2-4 the وَ joins the next oath nouns to it (al-Darwish parses them this way)
- **Interrogative particles**: هَلْ (hal) in verse 5 asks a question meant to confirm and affirm; أَلَمْ (alam) in verse 6 introduces a negative rhetorical question expecting an affirmative answer
- **إِذَا (idha)**: In verse 4 it is a time word with no condition in it; in verses 15, 16 and 21 it is conditional but does not make the verb jussive. The clause after it is its muḍāf ilayhi
- **Rebuke particle كَلَّا**: Verses 17 and 21 use كَلَّا (kalla) to rebuke and scold the idea just before it
- **Narrative past tense**: Verses 6-13 employ past tense verbs to narrate historical destruction of ancient nations
- **Joining wa and fa**: In verses 9, 10, 12 and 13 the وَ and فَ join one noun or clause to another; the فَ in verse 15 opens a new sentence and the second فَ in verses 15 and 16 ties the answer to أَمَّا
- **Relative pronouns and clauses**: الَّتِي (verse 8), الَّذِينَ (verses 9, 11) introduce relative clauses and are adjectives (na't) of the nouns before them
- **Passive voice constructions**: يُخْلَقْ (verse 8), دُكَّتِ (verse 21), جِيءَ (verse 23)
- **Maf'ul mutlaq (absolute object)**: Used for emphasis in verses 19, 20, 21, 22, 25, 26
- **أَمَّا...فَ construction**: Verses 15-16 demonstrate conditional construction "as for X, then Y"
- **Form IV and Form VIII verbs**: Form IV in أَكْثَرُوا, أَكْرَمَهُ, أَهَانَ, تُكْرِمُونَ, تُحِبُّونَ and يُوثِقُ; Form VIII in ابْتَلَاهُ
- **Vocative structures**: يَا أَيَّتُهَا (verse 27) for honored address
- **Imperative mood**: ارْجِعِي, ادْخُلِي (verses 28-30) for divine commands to the righteous soul
</Callout>
