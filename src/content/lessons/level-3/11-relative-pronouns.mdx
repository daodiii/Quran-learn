Read it from right to left. The word in the middle is ٱلَّذِى (*alladhī*). It means “the one who”.

English has the same kind of word: *who*, *which*, *that*. “The one **who** denies” is not finished until you say what the one does. The Arabic works the same way. The words يُكَذِّبُ بِٱلدِّينِ, “denies the Judgement”, tell you who the one is.

A word like ٱلَّذِى is called a **joining noun**, اِسْم مَوْصُول (*ism mawṣūl*). *Mawṣūl* means “joined”. It comes from the root و ص ل, “to join”. The noun is joined to a clause.

The clause is called the **ṣila**, صِلَة, “link”. It comes from the same root. The ṣila finishes the joining noun. Together they work as one noun phrase: “the one who denies the Judgement”. In other sentences that whole phrase can be a mubtadaʾ, a khabar or an object, like any noun.

<Note kind="remember">A joining noun is not complete alone. Its ṣila finishes it.</Note>

</Block>

      ]}
    />

L1.04 counted these words as nouns, and L1.06 said that their ٱل is part of the word, not the article. This lesson shows why that is right.

- **They do the jobs of a noun.** ٱلَّذِينَ was a mubtadaʾ in L2.02, the ism of inna in L2.10, and a describing word (naʿt) in L2.09. A noun can come after a preposition, and these words can too: لِلَّذِينَ, “for those who”.
- **They are already definite.** They start with ٱل, but they do not take the article the usual way, as ٱلْكِتَابُ does. The word is one piece.
- **The Corpus agrees.** The Quranic Arabic Corpus does not split ٱل off them. It gives them their own tag, “relative” (REL).

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal names this kind in its own words: a “joining noun”, next to the “pointing noun” (*ism ishāra*, L3.10). Both are nouns of a special kind.</Note>

The ṣila says something about “those”: *their hearts shake with awe*. Which word brings “those” into the clause? The pronoun **هُمْ** (“them”) at the end of قُلُوبُهُمْ.

That pronoun is the **returning pronoun**, عَائِد (*ʿāʾid*). It points back to the joining noun. English does not repeat it: we say “those **whose** hearts shake”. Arabic says “those who, their hearts shake”.

Usually the ṣila has one. Without it, the clause would not be about “those” at all. The returning pronoun matches the joining noun: ٱلَّذِينَ is masculine plural, and هُمْ is masculine plural. L3.02 taught the attached pronoun هُمْ. L2.02 showed a pronoun in a khabar that points back to its mubtadaʾ. This is the same idea inside a ṣila.

<Note kind="know">Here the whole sentence إِذَا ذُكِرَ ٱللَّهُ وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ is the ṣila. Al-Jadwal says so, and it also gives its two inner parts their own places. Exercise 7 comes back to this.</Note>

  <Stage label="Same verse as the first look">
    <Tiles
      label="ٱلَّذِى, then يُكَذِّبُ, with a hidden “he” inside the verb"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلَّذِى', en: 'the one who' },

Go back to 107:1. The ṣila يُكَذِّبُ بِٱلدِّينِ has no visible pronoun. But the verb has a doer, and the doer is hidden inside it: يُكَذِّبُ means “**he** denies” (L2.03). That hidden “he” is the returning pronoun. It points back to ٱلَّذِى.

So the returning pronoun can be:

- a separate attached pronoun, like هُمْ in قُلُوبُهُمْ;
- the doer letter on the verb: the و of يَمْشُونَ, “they walk”, in وَعِبَادُ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلَّذِينَ يَمْشُونَ (Al-Furqan 25:63, part);
- a hidden doer inside the verb, as here.

<Note kind="tip">To find it, ask: who does or has what the ṣila says? That “who” is the returning pronoun.</Note>

You pick the form that fits the noun before it, or the people you mean.

Quran Learn counted these words in the Quranic Arabic Corpus. The count adds all the words tagged “relative” whose lemma is ٱلَّذِى (the women’s forms included). There are **1,446**:

- ٱلَّذِينَ: 1,059
- ٱلَّذِى: 304
- ٱلَّتِى: 68
- the women’s plural (ٱلَّٰتِى and ٱلَّٰٓـِٔى): 13
- the dual: 2

So ٱلَّذِينَ is the one you will meet most. The dual is very rare.

<Note kind="know">The Quran’s text writes the dual with one lām: ٱلَّذَانِ (4:16) and ٱلَّذَيْنِ (41:29). Grammar books print it with two: ٱللَّذَانِ. They also list ٱللَّوَاتِي for women, and a feminine dual. The Corpus has no word of either kind. The table shows the feminine dual because grammar books teach it.</Note>

Maryam is feminine and singular, so the verse uses ٱلَّتِى. Al-Jadwal calls it a describing word (naʿt) of مَرْيَمَ, in the place of the accusative. A describing word matches its noun in gender and number (L2.09). A joining noun does the same.

The returning pronouns match too. The ending ـتْ of أَحْصَنَتْ shows a feminine doer: a hidden “she”. And ـهَا in فَرْجَهَا is “her”. Both point back to ٱلَّتِى.

Sometimes there is no noun before the joining noun. Then the form follows the meaning: ٱلَّذِينَ يُؤْمِنُونَ is “those who believe”, a group of people.

      en={['And the two among you who do it: punish them both.']}

This is the one joining noun whose letters change with the case, as any dual does (L1.08). It ends in **-āni** when it is nominative, as here. It ends in **-ayni** in the accusative or genitive, as in the next verse you will see.

The two are the mubtadaʾ here. The returning pronoun is the alif of يَأْتِيَانِ: “they two”. The ـهَا at the end of يَأْتِيَٰنِهَا is “it”: the act named in the verse before. It does not point back to ٱلَّذَانِ.

Al-Jadwal says the word is still **built**: on the alif here, and in the place of the nominative. So the letters change, and the word is analysed as built all the same.

Now the two are the second object of أَرِنَا (“show us them”), so the word is accusative and ends in **-ayni**. Al-Jadwal says it is built on the yāʾ, in the place of the accusative.

The returning pronoun is the alif of أَضَلَّانَا: “they two led”. The نَا at the end is “us”, the speakers. It does not point back.

Look at ٱلَّذِينَ in the three verses. It is nominative in the first, accusative in the second and genitive in the third. The word looks the same each time.

A word whose ending does not change with the case is called **built**, مَبْنِيّ (*mabnī*). The words whose endings change, like the nouns of L2.04 to L2.06, are called declined, مُعْرَب (*muʿrab*). You have met built words already: the pronouns of L1.10 and L3.01 never changed either.

A built word still has a case. The grammar books say it is “**in the place of**” a case, فِي مَحَلّ (*fī maḥall*). For ٱلَّذِينَ the place is nominative in 8:2, accusative in 2:27 and genitive in 3:134.

The ṣila in 107:1 and 114:5 was a **verbal sentence**: a verb with its doer. Here it is a **nominal sentence**: هُمْ فِى صَلَاتِهِمْ خَٰشِعُونَ, with the mubtadaʾ هُمْ and the khabar خَٰشِعُونَ (L2.01). The returning pronoun is the mubtadaʾ itself.

| Kind of ṣila | Example | Verse |
|---|---|---|

A ṣila does not have to be a full sentence. A preposition with its noun, or a place word, can be one. Al-Jadwal says that phrase hangs on a hidden word, like “is found”, and the hidden word is the ṣila. A hidden “he” inside that word is the returning pronoun.

**مَنْ** (*man*) is a joining noun too. It means “who”, “the one who” or “whoever”. It has one shape for one person or many, male or female. So it needs no form for gender or number.

Here it is the doer of يَعْلَمُ, and its place is nominative (al-Jadwal). The ṣila is the phrase فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ. There is no verb in it, so a hidden word like “is” carries the ṣila, and a hidden “he” inside it is the returning pronoun.

**مَا** (*mā*) means “what” or “that which”. It has one shape too. The usual guide is: **مَنْ** for people, **مَا** for things.

Al-Jadwal reads this مَا as a joining noun, in the place of the nominative, and the mubtadaʾ put later. The khabar is لِلَّهِ, put first (L2.01). Each ṣila is a phrase: فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ and فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ.

L3.07 teaches the jussive, the mood of that sukūn. L4.03 and L4.04 teach the condition sentence in full.

| condition | 35:2 (مَّا يَفْتَحِ ٱللَّهُ لِلنَّاسِ مِن رَّحْمَةٍ) | whatever | condition word |
| “not” | 27:65 (وَمَا يَشْعُرُونَ) | they do not know | negative |
| “that” | 2:10 (بِمَا كَانُوا۟ يَكْذِبُونَ) | because they lied | a little word that turns a clause into a noun |

The last one is a little word, not a noun: it has no returning pronoun, and nothing points back to it. Al-Jadwal calls it a verbal-noun particle (*ḥarf maṣdarī*). With the clause after it, it makes a verbal noun, here “their lying”, in the place of the genitive after بِ.

L3.20 teaches verbal nouns. L4.04 teaches the condition use, and L4.10 teaches negation.

<Note kind="know">The Corpus tags the مَا of 2:10 and the مَا of 35:2 as relative (REL). Al-Jadwal reads 2:10 as the particle and 35:2 as a condition word. This lesson follows al-Jadwal.</Note>

<Block id="mistake-this" title="Mistake 1: mixing up “this” and “who”">
  <Stage>

      en={['That one is the one who pushes the orphan away.']}

ذَٰلِكَ and ٱلَّذِى both look like short “that” words. They do different jobs.

- ذَٰلِكَ **points** at something: “that one” (L3.10 teaches these pointing nouns).
- ٱلَّذِى **joins** a clause to a noun: “the one who”.

In this verse they sit side by side. ذَٰلِكَ is the mubtadaʾ. ٱلَّذِى with its ṣila is the khabar, and al-Jadwal gives ٱلَّذِى the place of the nominative.

The test: a joining noun has a ṣila after it. A pointing word does not.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-every" title="Mistake 3: treating every مَا and مَنْ as a joining noun">
  <Stage label="Look at the verb and the clause">
    <Tiles
      label="four times مَا or مَنْ, four different jobs"
      items={[
        { ar: 'مَا ٱلْقَارِعَةُ', en: 'question: what?', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'مَن يَعْمَلْ', en: 'condition: whoever', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'وَمَا يَشْعُرُونَ', en: 'negative: not', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ', en: 'relative: what', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Do not call every مَا and مَنْ a joining noun. Ask four things.

1. Does it have a ṣila, and does a pronoun in it point back? Then it is a joining noun.
2. Does it start a question and wait for an answer? Then it is a question word.
3. Is the verb after it in the jussive, with a second clause that answers? Then it is a condition word.
4. Does مَا mean “not”, or does it turn a clause into a noun? Then it is a little word.

Do not trust the Corpus tag alone. It tags some question words and condition words as relative.
</Block>

<Exercise n={1} q="Find the joining noun, the ṣila and the returning pronoun: ٱلَّذِينَ يَنقُضُونَ عَهْدَ ٱللَّهِ مِنۢ بَعْدِ مِيثَٰقِهِۦ (Al-Baqarah 2:27, start). Meaning: “Those who break the promise of Allah after it was made firm.”">

- **Joining noun:** ٱلَّذِينَ, “those who”: masculine plural.
- **Ṣila:** يَنقُضُونَ عَهْدَ ٱللَّهِ مِنۢ بَعْدِ مِيثَٰقِهِۦ. It is a verbal sentence, and it has no place.
- **Returning pronoun:** the و of يَنقُضُونَ, “they”.
- The ـهِ in مِيثَٰقِهِۦ means “His”, Allah’s. It does not point back to ٱلَّذِينَ.
- **Place:** al-Jadwal says it describes ٱلْفَٰسِقِينَ in the verse before. So it is in the place of the accusative.

</Exercise>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Read the ṣila of ٱلَّذِينَ in إِذَا ذُكِرَ ٱللَّهُ وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ (Al-Anfal 8:2, part). Which pronoun returns, and which part is the ṣila? Meaning: “when Allah is mentioned, their hearts shake with awe.”">

- The returning pronoun is **هُمْ** in قُلُوبُهُمْ.
- The **whole** sentence إِذَا ذُكِرَ ٱللَّهُ وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ is the ṣila of ٱلَّذِينَ. It has no place.
- Inside it, smaller sentences can have a place. Al-Jadwal says ذُكِرَ ٱللَّهُ is in the place of the genitive: it is the second word of an idafah with إِذَا.

The ṣila as a whole has no place, but a sentence inside it can have one.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Is مَنْ for people and مَا for things in these two verses? Look at (a) فَمِنْهُم مَّن يَمْشِى عَلَىٰ بَطْنِهِۦ (An-Nur 24:45, part) and (b) فَٱنكِحُوا۟ مَا طَابَ لَكُم مِّنَ ٱلنِّسَآءِ (An-Nisa 4:3, part). Meaning: (a) “Some of them walk on their belly.” (b) “Marry the women who please you.”">

No. The guide “مَنْ for people, مَا for things” has exceptions, and these are two.

- (a) The animals of 24:45 are called مَن. Al-Jadwal reads it as a joining noun with the ṣila يَمْشِى عَلَىٰ بَطْنِهِۦ. The hidden “he” of يَمْشِى is the returning pronoun.
- (b) Al-Jadwal reads مَا as a joining noun with the ṣila طَابَ لَكُم, and the phrase مِّنَ ٱلنِّسَآءِ says what it means. The hidden “he” of طَابَ is the returning pronoun.

So treat the guide as a first step, and look at the verse.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Join these with the right joining noun, and add the returning pronoun: (a) ٱلْكِتَابُ ___ كَتَبَ ٱلطَّالِبُ___ (b) ٱلرِّسَالَةُ ___ كَتَبَ ٱلطَّالِبُ___. These are made-up examples. Meaning: “the book that the student wrote” and “the letter that the student wrote”.">

- (a) **ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلَّذِى كَتَبَهُ ٱلطَّالِبُ**. ٱلْكِتَابُ is masculine, so the joining noun is ٱلَّذِى and the returning pronoun is ـهُ, “it” (masculine).
- (b) **ٱلرِّسَالَةُ ٱلَّتِى كَتَبَهَا ٱلطَّالِبُ**. ٱلرِّسَالَةُ is feminine, so the joining noun is ٱلَّتِى and the returning pronoun is ـهَا, “it” (feminine).

Here the returning pronoun is an object. The doer is ٱلطَّالِبُ. English leaves out “it”: “the book that the student wrote”.

</Exercise>

  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="joining noun plus ṣila equals one noun phrase"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'joining noun', small: 'اِسْم مَوْصُول' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'ṣila', small: 'the clause that finishes it' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'one noun phrase', small: '“the one who …”' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- A **joining noun** is a “who”, “which” or “what” noun. It needs a clause, the **ṣila**, to finish it.
- The forms are ٱلَّذِى, ٱلَّتِى, ٱلَّذِينَ, the dual ٱلَّذَانِ and ٱلَّذَيْنِ, and the women’s ٱلَّٰتِى and ٱلَّٰٓـِٔى. The form matches the noun before it. The dual changes its ending with the case.
- The ṣila usually has a **returning pronoun**. It can be visible, hidden in the verb, or left out.
- The ṣila can be a verbal sentence, a nominal sentence, or a phrase that works like a sentence.
- A joining noun is **built**: its shape stays the same. It has a place by its job. The ṣila has no place.
- **مَنْ** and **مَا** have one shape. They can also ask a question or start a condition, and مَا can also mean “not” or turn a clause into a noun. Follow the clause, and check for a ṣila.
