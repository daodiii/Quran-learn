  <Stage>

      ]}
      en={['Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves.']}
    />

This is part of a prayer in the Quran. Look at the end of each word. All three end in the same letters: **نَا** (*-nā*).

- In رَبَّنَا, -nā means “our”.
- In ظَلَمْنَا, -nā means “we”.
- In أَنفُسَنَا, -nā means “our” again: “our selves”.

Arabic has no small word for “our” or “we” here. A short ending does that work. These endings are the **attached pronouns**. This lesson teaches all of them.

</Block>

<Block id="what" title="A pronoun joined to a word" level={2}>
  <Stage label="Two kinds of pronoun">
    <Tiles
      label="a separate pronoun and a joined pronoun"
      items={[
        { ar: 'هُوَ', en: 'he: stands alone' },
        { ar: 'كِتَابُهُ', en: 'his book: -hu is joined' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

You know the separate pronouns from L3.01: هُوَ, أَنَا, نَحْنُ. Each one is a word on its own.

An **attached pronoun**, ضَمِير مُتَّصِل (*ḍamīr muttaṣil*), cannot stand alone. It is joined to the end of another word and is written as part of it. *Muttaṣil* means “joined”. The separate pronoun is *munfaṣil*, “apart”.

- English says “his book”, two words.
- Arabic says كِتَابُهُ, one written word.

The joined pronoun is still a noun, a word that stands for a person or thing. It has a job in the sentence, as you will see.

</Block>

There are **twelve** endings. They match the twelve separate pronouns, one for one. The Pronoun column shows the separate pronoun with the same meaning.

A group of men, or men and women together, takes the “men” form. The “women” form is for a group of women.

<Note kind="tip">Learn the two families first (the next block). Then the twelve endings are two groups of five and two odd ones.</Note>

    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Arabic text as on Wikisource.</p>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* is the short grammar book you met in L2.01. It puts the pronouns in its chapter on the object (*mafʿūl bihi*). It says:

- A pronoun is of two kinds: joined and separate.
- The joined pronouns are **twelve**. The book shows them on the verb ضَرَبَ, “he hit”: ضَرَبَنِي, “he hit me”, ضَرَبَنَا, “he hit us”, and so on.

The book lists a set of separate object pronouns too, beginning with إِيَّايَ and إِيَّاكَ. [L3.01](/learn/level-3/01-subject-pronouns/) gives a first look at them.

<Note kind="know">The book shows the joined pronouns on a verb because it is writing about the object. The same twelve endings also join nouns and prepositions. The next step shows all three.</Note>
</Block>

Ten of the twelve endings fall into two families.

- The **k family** means “you”: ـكَ, ـكِ, ـكُمَا, ـكُمْ, ـكُنَّ.
- The **h family** means “he, she, they”: ـهُ, ـهَا, ـهُمَا, ـهُمْ, ـهُنَّ.

Two stand apart: **ـِي** (“my, me”) and **ـنَا** (“our, us”). Each has the same form for men and women.

The last letters tell you who is meant. In the k family, -ka is one man, -ki is one woman, -kumā is two, -kum is men, and -kunna is women. In the h family, -hu is “him”, -hā is “her”, -humā is two, -hum is men, and -hunna is women.

</Block>

<Block id="table" title="The same endings on three kinds of word">
  <Stage label="A noun, a preposition, a verb">

| Who | Ending | On a noun: كِتَاب | On a preposition: بِ | On a verb: ضَرَبَ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| me, my | ـِي (verb: ـنِي) | كِتَابِي | بِي | ضَرَبَنِي |
| us, our | ـنَا | كِتَابُنَا | بِنَا | ضَرَبَنَا |
| you (one man) | ـكَ | كِتَابُكَ | بِكَ | ضَرَبَكَ |
| you (one woman) | ـكِ | كِتَابُكِ | بِكِ | ضَرَبَكِ |
| you (two) | ـكُمَا | كِتَابُكُمَا | بِكُمَا | ضَرَبَكُمَا |
| you (men) | ـكُمْ | كِتَابُكُمْ | بِكُمْ | ضَرَبَكُمْ |
| you (women) | ـكُنَّ | كِتَابُكُنَّ | بِكُنَّ | ضَرَبَكُنَّ |
| him, his | ـهُ | كِتَابُهُ | بِهِ | ضَرَبَهُ |
| her | ـهَا | كِتَابُهَا | بِهَا | ضَرَبَهَا |
| them (two) | ـهُمَا | كِتَابُهُمَا | بِهِمَا | ضَرَبَهُمَا |
| them (men) | ـهُمْ | كِتَابُهُمْ | بِهِمْ | ضَرَبَهُمْ |
| them (women) | ـهُنَّ | كِتَابُهُنَّ | بِهِنَّ | ضَرَبَهُنَّ |

  </Stage>

Read down any column and the same twelve endings come back. The word they join changes, and so does the meaning:

- On a noun (كِتَاب, “book”), the ending means “my, your, his …”: *kitābuhu*, “his book”.
- On a preposition (بِ, “with”), the ending means “me, you, him …”: *bihi*, “with him”.
- On a verb (ضَرَبَ, “he hit”), the ending means “me, you, him …”: *ḍarabahu*, “he hit him”.

The ending itself is one set. The word in front of it decides how English says it. The forms in the table are teaching forms. The verb list is the one in al-Ajurrumiyyah, and ضَرَبَ is a real Form I verb.

<Note kind="know">The first person has two shapes: ـِي on a noun and a preposition, ـنِي on a verb. The block called “ī or nī” later in this lesson explains it.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="cases" title="What job the pronoun does">
  <Stage label="Joined to … the pronoun is …">

| Joined to | Job of the pronoun | Case, in place | Seen in |
|---|---|---|---|
| a noun | muḍāf ilayh: “our Lord” | genitive (jarr) | رَبَّنَا |
| a preposition | the noun after it (majrūr) | genitive (jarr) | لَنَا |
| a verb | the object (mafʿūl bihi) | accusative (naṣb) | هَدَيْتَنَا |
| a past verb | the doer (fāʿil), some endings | nominative (rafʿ) | ظَلَمْنَا |

  </Stage>

A joined pronoun does not show a case ending. But it still has a case, because it has a job. The grammar books say it is **in the place of** that case, *fī maḥall*, as you saw for هُوَ in L2.01 and for the pronoun after a preposition in L2.07.

- On a **noun**, it is the second word of an idafah, so it counts as **genitive** (L2.08).
- On a **preposition**, it is the noun after the preposition, so it counts as **genitive** (L2.07).
- On a **verb**, it is the object, so it counts as **accusative** (L2.03, L2.05).
- Some endings on a **past verb** are the doer, so they count as **nominative**. Step 3 takes this up.

The same letters, -nā, are in the table four times and have two different cases. Look at the word in front of the ending to know which job it does.

</Block>

When the pronoun joins a noun, the two make an **idafah** (L2.08). The noun is the first word. The pronoun is the second word, the *muḍāf ilayh*. In وَإِلَٰهُكُمْ:

- إِلَٰهُ is the first word. It is the mubtadaʾ, so it keeps its own case: nominative, -u.
- كُمْ, “your”, is the muḍāf ilayh. It is genitive in place, as the second word of an idafah is.

Al-Jadwal reads the verse this way: the noun is the mubtadaʾ, and the pronoun is “in the place of” the genitive.

Two rules from L2.08 still hold. The first word has **no tanwīn** and **no al-**: it is إِلَٰهُكُمْ, not إِلَٰهٌ كُمْ. The pronoun also makes the whole phrase definite, so the khabar إِلَٰهٌ stays indefinite.

</Block>

<Block id="noun-changes" title="What happens to the noun">
  <Stage label="Four small changes (made-up forms and real words)">
    <Tiles
      label="a noun before a joined pronoun"
      items={[
        { ar: 'كِتَابٌ', en: 'a book' },
        { ar: 'كِتَابُهُ', en: 'his book' },
        { ar: 'رَحْمَةٌ', en: 'a mercy' },
        { ar: 'رَحْمَتِكَ', en: 'your mercy' },
        { ar: 'أَبٌ', en: 'a father' },
        { ar: 'أَبُوهُ', en: 'his father' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The noun changes a little in front of a joined pronoun.

1. **The tanwīn goes.** كِتَابٌ becomes كِتَابُهُ. The first word of an idafah has no tanwīn (L2.08).
2. **The round ة becomes ت.** رَحْمَةٌ becomes رَحْمَتِكَ, “your mercy”, as in فِى رَحْمَتِكَ (7:151, end). The ة is written ت when something is joined after it.
3. **The noun keeps its own case.** The pronoun shows nothing, so the case shows on the noun: كِتَابُهُ (nominative), كِتَابَهُ (accusative), كِتَابِهِ (genitive).
4. **The five nouns keep their long vowel** (L2.06): أَبُوهُ (nominative), أَبَاهُ (accusative), أَبِيهِ (genitive). A dual or a sound plural noun loses its final nūn before the pronoun, as in any idafah (L2.08).

One pronoun hides the noun's ending: **ـِي**, “my”. It needs a kasra on the letter before it, so the case of the noun is understood but not seen (L2.08). In رَبِّي you cannot tell the case from the noun.
</Block>

You met this in L2.07. A pronoun can be the noun after a preposition, and then it joins the preposition into one word: عَلَىٰ + هِمْ becomes عَلَيْهِمْ.

| Preposition | + him | + them (men) | + me |
|---|---|---|---|
| لِ, “for” | لَهُ (*lahu*) | لَهُمْ (*lahum*) | لِي (*lī*) |
| مِنْ, “from” | مِنْهُ (*minhu*) | مِنْهُمْ (*minhum*) | مِنِّي (*minnī*) |
| عَنْ, “about, away from” | عَنْهُ (*ʿanhu*) | عَنْهُمْ (*ʿanhum*) | عَنِّي (*ʿannī*) |
| عَلَىٰ, “on” | عَلَيْهِ (*ʿalayhi*) | عَلَيْهِمْ (*ʿalayhim*) | عَلَيَّ (*ʿalayya*) |
| إِلَىٰ, “to” | إِلَيْهِ (*ilayhi*) | إِلَيْهِمْ (*ilayhim*) | إِلَيَّ (*ilayya*) |
| بِ, “with, by” | بِهِ (*bihi*) | بِهِمْ (*bihim*) | بِي (*bī*) |

English says “upon them”, two words. Arabic says عَلَيْهِمْ, one. The pronoun is the noun after the preposition. It is genitive in place.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal reads the pronoun in عَلَيْهِمْ as the **hā**, in the place of genitive after عَلَىٰ. It reads the **mīm** as a letter that shows a group of men. This lesson treats -hum as one ending, as the grammar books list it, and the hā is the pronoun inside it.</Note>
</Block>

L2.07 taught this spelling rule for the pronoun after a preposition. It is the same on a noun and on a verb.

The pronouns هُ, هُمْ, هُمَا, هُنَّ become **هِ, هِمْ, هِمَا, هِنَّ** when the sound before them is a **kasra** or a **ي with sukūn**.

- بِهِمْ, فِيهِمْ, عَلَيْهِمْ: a kasra or a yāʾ with sukūn comes before. So *him*.
- رَبِّهِمْ: a kasra comes before. So *him*. In رَبُّهُمْ it is a ḍamma, so *hum*.
- لَهُمْ, مِنْهُمْ: a fatḥa or a sukūn comes before. So *hum*.

The endings هَا, كُمْ and نَا do not change.

<Block id="verb" title="On a verb: the object">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Aʿraf 7:23 (part)"
      groups={[

        { role: 'fiil', label: 'fiʿl + fāʿil + object', hint: '-nā is the object: “us”', words: [{ ar: 'وَتَرْحَمْنَا', en: 'and have mercy on us', say: 'wa-tarhamna', type: 'v' }] },
      ]}
      en={['… for us, and have mercy on us.']}

On a verb, the pronoun is the **object**. In وَتَرْحَمْنَا, the verb is تَرْحَمْ, “you have mercy”, and the ending -nā is who receives the mercy: “us”. You learned the object in L2.03. It is the *mafʿūl bihi*, and it is accusative.

Al-Jadwal says this about تَرْحَمْنَا: **nā** is a pronoun, the object. The pronoun shows no ending, but it counts as accusative, in place.

Compare the endings on the three kinds of word. On a noun, -nā is genitive. On a preposition, it is genitive. On a verb, it is accusative. The letters are the same each time.

<Note kind="know">The doer of تَرْحَمْ is a hidden “you”. It is the one asked for mercy. The pronoun ending is the object.</Note>

<Step id="doer" label="Me, inna, doer" title="Me, inna, and the doer">
<Block id="me" title="ī or nī">
  <Stage label="“Me” on three kinds of word">
    <Tiles
      label="the pronoun me on a noun, a preposition and a verb"
      items={[
        { ar: 'رَبِّي', en: 'my Lord (noun: -ī)' },
        { ar: 'لِي', en: 'for me (preposition: -ī)' },

The pronoun “me, my” has two shapes.

- On a **noun**, it is **ـِي**: كِتَابِي, “my book”.
- On a **verb**, it is **ـنِي**: خَلَقَنِي, “he created me”. The extra nūn is the **protecting nūn**, نُون الْوِقَايَة (*nūn al-wiqāya*). Al-Jadwal names it in this verse: “the nūn is for protection”. The yāʾ needs a kasra before it. The nūn takes that kasra, so the verb keeps its own fatḥa.
- On a **preposition**, it depends. لِ and بِ keep the plain ـِي: لِي, بِي. مِنْ and عَنْ take the nūn: مِنِّي, عَنِّي. إِلَىٰ and عَلَىٰ turn their ى into يَّ: إِلَيَّ, عَلَيَّ.

The shape ـنِي is not “for verbs and prepositions”. It is for verbs, and for مِنْ and عَنْ. For the other prepositions, use ـِي.

The joined pronoun also comes after إِنَّ (*inna*, “indeed”). L2.10 taught that inna makes its first word accusative. When that first word is a pronoun, it joins inna: إِنَّ + كَ gives إِنَّكَ.

Al-Jadwal says the same about the same word in the verse before, 3:8: “the kāf is a pronoun in the place of the accusative, the ism of inna”.

So the set has a fourth place. You will see it often: إِنَّنِي, إِنَّهُ, إِنَّا (inna with -nā), إِنَّكَ.

<Block id="doer-object" title="Doer or object? The same letters">
  <Stage label="A vowel decides">
    <Tiles
      label="ظَلَمْنَا and ظَلَمَنَا"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ظَلَمْنَا', en: 'we wronged', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'ظَلَمَنَا', en: 'he wronged us', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The ending -nā is on both words. The letter in front of it is different.

- **ظَلَمْنَا**: the letter before -nā has a **sukūn**. The verb is complete with the ending: “we wronged”. Here -nā is the **doer**. This is the word in 7:23.
- **ظَلَمَنَا**: the letter before -nā has a **fatḥa**. The verb is complete without it: ظَلَمَ, “he wronged”. So -nā is the **object**: “he wronged us”.

This works for the past tense. The past tense has its own doer endings, such as -tu (“I”), -ta, -ti and -nā (“we”), and -ū (“they”). L3.05 teaches them. The doer ending comes **first**, then the object.

On a **present-tense** verb the doer is shown at the front (يَـ, تَـ, نَـ) or by an ending like -ūna. L3.06 teaches this. For now, notice that the word تَرْحَمْنَا in 7:23 starts with تَـ, so the -nā at its end is the object.

</Block>

A verb can carry **two** joined pronouns. The first is the doer, the second is the object.

- ضَرَبْنَاهُ: ضَرَبَ + **-nā** (we, the doer) + **-hu** (him, the object). “We hit him.”
- Al-Jadwal reads the Quran's رَزَقْنَٰهُمْ the same way: **-nā** is the doer, in the place of the nominative, and **-hum** is the object, in the place of the accusative. You will see it word by word below.

So one written word can hold a verb, a doer and an object. In English that takes three words.

The Pronoun column shows the separate pronoun for the doer, because the object -hu is joined.

<Note kind="tip">When you meet a long verb, take the endings off from the back. The last ending is the object. The one before it is the doer.</Note>

<Block id="mistake-order" title="Mistake 1: mixing up the doer and the object">
  <Stage label="One word, three pieces">

كَتَبْتُهُ is “I wrote it”. It has **two** joined pronouns. The first, -tu, is the doer. The second, -hu, is the object.

It is not “he wrote me” and it is not “it wrote”. Read the endings from the back: the last is the object. Do not take all the endings on a verb for objects. Some endings are doers.

This verse has the protecting nūn twice. In خَلَقَنِى it is joined to a past verb. In يَهْدِينِ it is joined to a present verb, and the yāʾ of “me” is dropped. Al-Jadwal says it is dropped to match the endings of the verses around it. The nūn does the same job in both.

Notice also the pair: **هُوَ**, a separate pronoun, is the mubtadaʾ. **ـنِي** is a joined pronoun, the object. They do two different jobs.

<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: read the endings">
  <Stage label="For each joined pronoun, say">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Person:</b> I, we, you or he, she, they?</li>
      <li><b>Joined to:</b> a noun, a preposition, a verb or inna?</li>
      <li><b>Job:</b> muḍāf ilayh, noun after a preposition, object, ism of inna or doer?</li>

    </ol>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="What does each ending mean? (a) كِتَابُهُ (b) كِتَابِي (c) كِتَابُنَا (d) كِتَابُكَ (e) كِتَابُهُمْ (f) كِتَابُهَا. These are made-up forms.">

- (a) كِتَابُهُ, “his book”: -hu is “his”.
- (b) كِتَابِي, “my book”: -ī is “my”, on a noun.
- (c) كِتَابُنَا, “our book”: -nā is “our”.
- (d) كِتَابُكَ, “your book”, said to one man: -ka.
- (e) كِتَابُهُمْ, “their book”: -hum, for men.
- (f) كِتَابُهَا, “her book”: -hā.

In each one the pronoun is the muḍāf ilayh, genitive in place.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Join the ending -hum (“their, them”) to each word. (a) كِتَاب, book (b) رَءَا, he saw (c) مِنْ, from (d) بَيْت, house (e) عَلَىٰ, on. These are made-up forms.">

- (a) كِتَابُهُمْ, “their book”: the pronoun is the muḍāf ilayh, genitive.
- (b) رَءَاهُمْ, “he saw them”: the pronoun is the object, accusative.
- (c) مِنْهُمْ, “from them”: the noun after the preposition, genitive.
- (d) بَيْتُهُمْ, “their house”: the muḍāf ilayh, genitive.
- (e) عَلَيْهِمْ, “on them”: عَلَىٰ turns its ى into يْ, and the hā takes a kasra after it. The noun after the preposition, genitive.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Find the joined pronouns and say their jobs: رَبَّنَآ إِنَّكَ جَامِعُ ٱلنَّاسِ (Al-Imran 3:9, start). Meaning: “Our Lord, indeed you gather the people.”">

There are two.

- **-nā** in رَبَّنَآ: joined to a noun. It is the muḍāf ilayh, “our”, genitive in place.
- **-ka** in إِنَّكَ: joined to inna. It is the ism of inna, “you”, accusative in place.

The word جَامِعُ has no joined pronoun. It is the khabar of inna.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="In هُوَ يُحِبُّهَا, “he loves her”, which pronoun is separate and which is joined? What job does each do? This is a made-up example.">

- **هُوَ** is the **separate** pronoun. It is the **mubtadaʾ**. The sentence is a nominal sentence, and the khabar is the verbal sentence يُحِبُّهَا (L2.01).
- **ـهَا** is the **joined** pronoun. It is joined to the verb يُحِبُّ, so it is the **object**, accusative in place.
- The doer of يُحِبُّ is a hidden “he”, the same person as هُوَ.

You can also say يُحِبُّهَا alone, “he loves her”. The doer is hidden, and the object is joined.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Split each word into the preposition and the pronoun. Say who the pronoun is. لَهُمْ فِيهَا فَٰكِهَةٌ (Ya-Sin 36:57, start). Meaning: “They have fruit in it.”">

- **لَهُمْ**: لَ (“for”) + هُمْ (“them”). The pronoun is genitive in place. The phrase is the khabar, put first: “for them”.
- **فِيهَا**: فِي (“in”) + هَا (“it”, feminine singular). The pronoun means the garden, ٱلْجَنَّة, from 36:55, which is feminine.
- **فَٰكِهَةٌ**, “fruit”, is the mubtadaʾ, put later (L2.01).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Doer or object? Say what -nā is in each. (a) ظَلَمْنَا (b) ظَلَمَنَا (c) تَرْحَمْنَا (d) رَزَقْنَا. These are teaching forms.">

- (a) ظَلَمْنَا: a sukūn before -nā. The doer: “we wronged”.
- (b) ظَلَمَنَا: a fatḥa before -nā. The object: “he wronged us”.
- (c) تَرْحَمْنَا: the verb starts with تَـ, a present-tense prefix for “you”, so -nā is the object: “you have mercy on us”.
- (d) رَزَقْنَا: a sukūn before -nā. The doer: “we provided”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Choose ـِي or ـنِي. (a) “my Lord” رَبّ__ (b) “he created me” خَلَقَ__ (c) “for me” لِ__ (d) “he saw me” رَءَا__">

- (a) رَبِّي: a noun takes ـِي.
- (b) خَلَقَنِي: a verb takes ـنِي.
- (c) لِي: this preposition keeps ـِي.
- (d) رَءَانِي: a verb takes ـنِي.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Say each in Arabic, with the vowels. (a) “for you” (one man) (b) “from him” (c) “with them” (men) (d) “I wrote it”. These are made-up forms.">

- (a) **لَكَ**: لِ becomes لَ before a joined pronoun, except for “me” (L2.07).
- (b) **مِنْهُ**: the sukūn on نْ comes before the hā, so -hu.
- (c) **بِهِمْ**: a kasra comes before the hā, so -him.
- (d) **كَتَبْتُهُ**: the verb, then the doer -tu, then the object -hu.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Pick hum or him. (a) لَ__ (b) بِ__ (c) عَلَيْ__ (d) رَبِّ__ (e) رَبُّ__ (f) مِنْ__">

- (a) لَهُمْ: a fatḥa before.
- (b) بِهِمْ: a kasra before.
- (c) عَلَيْهِمْ: a yāʾ with sukūn before.
- (d) رَبِّهِمْ: a kasra before.
- (e) رَبُّهُمْ: a ḍamma before.
- (f) مِنْهُمْ: a sukūn on the nūn before.

<Exercise n={10} q="Find the joined pronoun and its job: ٱلنَّارَ ٱلَّتِى وَقُودُهَا ٱلنَّاسُ (Al-Baqarah 2:24, part). Meaning: “The fire whose fuel is people.”">

**-hā** is joined to the noun وَقُودُ, “fuel”. It is the muḍāf ilayh, “its”, genitive in place. It means the fire, ٱلنَّار, which is feminine.

Al-Jadwal reads وَقُودُهَا as the mubtadaʾ, with -hā as its muḍāf ilayh. ٱلنَّاسُ is the khabar.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={11} q="What is the ending on the last word? وَٱلشَّمْسِ وَضُحَىٰهَا (Ash-Shams 91:1). Meaning: “By the sun and its morning light.”">

**-hā** is joined to the noun ضُحَىٰ, “morning light”. It is the muḍāf ilayh, “its”: the morning light of the sun. It is genitive in place. Because ضُحَىٰ ends in a long ā (ى), you cannot see its own ending, as you learned in L2.01.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={12} q="What job does -hu have, and what does it mean? وَلَمْ يَكُن لَّهُۥ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌۢ (Al-Ikhlas 112:4). Meaning: “And no one is equal to him.”">

**-hu** is joined to the preposition لِ: لَهُۥ, “to him”. It is the noun after the preposition, genitive in place. It refers to Allah.

Al-Jadwal says the phrase لَهُ belongs to كُفُوًا, “equal”: “no one is equal to Him”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={13} q="Name the joined pronouns in إِنَّآ أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ ٱلْكَوْثَرَ (Al-Kawthar 108:1). Meaning: “We have given you Al-Kawthar.”">

There are three. Two of them are **-nā** with different jobs, and one is **-ka**.

- **-nā** in إِنَّآ: joined to inna. It is the ism of inna, “we”, accusative in place.
- **-nā** in أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ: joined to the verb, first. It is the doer, “we”, nominative in place.
- **-ka** in أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ: joined to the verb, last. It is the first object, “you”, accusative in place.

The verb أَعْطَى takes two objects (L2.03). ٱلْكَوْثَرَ is the second one.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={14} q="What does -kumā mean, and whom does it speak to? فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ (Ar-Rahman 55:13). Meaning: “So which of your Lord's gifts do you two deny?”">

**-kumā** is the dual “you two”. It is joined to the noun رَبِّ, so it is the muḍāf ilayh: “your (two) Lord”. It is genitive in place.

The verb تُكَذِّبَانِ ends in -āni, a dual ending for the doer: “you two”. The pronoun and the verb agree.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={15} q="What are the joined pronouns? قَالَ رَبِّ ٱغْفِرْ لِى (Al-Aʿraf 7:151, start). Meaning: “He said: My Lord, forgive me.”">

- **رَبِّ**: the word for “my Lord” with the yāʾ of “my” left out. The kasra on the bāʾ is what is left of it. Al-Jadwal says the yāʾ is dropped for lightness. The word is being called, so it is accusative.
- **لِى**: لِ (“for”) + ـِي (“me”). It is the noun after the preposition, genitive in place. There is no nūn, because a preposition like لِ keeps the plain ـِي.

</Exercise>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="a word plus a pronoun ending equals one written word"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'كِتَابُ', small: 'a word' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'هُ', small: 'a joined pronoun' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'كِتَابُهُ', small: 'his book' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- An **attached pronoun** is joined to the end of another word. There are twelve: ـِي, ـنَا, ـكَ, ـكِ, ـكُمَا, ـكُمْ, ـكُنَّ, ـهُ, ـهَا, ـهُمَا, ـهُمْ, ـهُنَّ.
- The **k family** means “you”. The **h family** means “he, she, they”.
- The same endings join **nouns** (my book), **prepositions** (with him) and **verbs** (he hit him).
- On a noun the pronoun is the **muḍāf ilayh**, and the noun loses its al- and tanwīn. On a preposition it is the noun after it. Both are genitive in place.
- On a verb the pronoun is the **object**, accusative in place. After inna it is the **ism of inna**, accusative in place.
- Some endings on a past verb are the **doer**, nominative in place. With two endings, the doer comes first and the object last: ضَرَبْنَاهُ.
- “Me” is ـِي on a noun and ـنِي on a verb, with the protecting nūn.
- The pronouns هُ, هُمْ, هُمَا, هُنَّ become هِ, هِمْ, هِمَا, هِنَّ after a kasra or a yāʾ with sukūn.
