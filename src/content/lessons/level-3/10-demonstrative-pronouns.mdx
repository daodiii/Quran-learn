This is the start of a verse about the Quran. The first word, هَٰذَا, means “this”. It points at something close.

Look at what the three parts do.

- هَٰذَا is the **mubtadaʾ**. The sentence is about “this”.
- بَيَانٌ is the **khabar**. It ends in -un, like any khabar.
- لِّلنَّاسِ, “for people”, says who the statement is for. Al-Jadwal reads it as describing بَيَانٌ.

So a pointing word takes a job in the sentence like a noun does. You met this in L2.02, where a pointing word was listed among the definite mubtadaʾs.

<Block id="what" title="A pointing word is a noun" level={2}>
  <Stage label="From al-Ajurrumiyyah">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote" lang="ar" dir="rtl">
      <p>وَالِاسْمُ الْمُبْهَمُ نَحْوُ هَذَا وَهَذِهِ وَهَؤُلَاءِ</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">The third kind of definite noun, in the book’s list. Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>
  </Stage>

English has four of these words: *this*, *that*, *these*, *those*. The grammar books call them اِسْم إِشَارَة (*ism ishāra*). *Ishāra* means pointing. So a pointing word is a **noun** that points.

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* lists it as the third of five kinds of definite noun, under the name *al-ism al-mubham*. L1.06 gave the whole list.

Two things follow from this.

- A pointing word is **definite** without ٱلْ. A mubtadaʾ can be a pointing word, as in 3:138.
- It can do the jobs of a noun: mubtadaʾ, object, the noun after a little word. Step 3 shows each one.

Al-Jadwal takes the words apart.

- **Near:** in 7:73, 2:35 and 3:62 it says هَا is a little word of attention (حَرْف تَنْبِيه), and the pointing word is what follows: ذِهِ, ذَا.
- **Far:** in 2:2 and 6:102 it says ذَا is the pointing word, the **lām** (ل) is “for distance”, and the **kāf** (ك) is “for the one spoken to”.

That is why the near words start with هَـ and the far words end in ـكَ. In أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ al-Jadwal names only the kāf (2:157).

<Note kind="warn">The ك at the end of ذَٰلِكَ is a letter of address. It is not the pronoun **-ka**, “you”, that L3.02 teaches. L2.07 said the same about أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ.</Note>

Five near words and five far words: ten forms. The dual has two shapes, so the chart has fourteen rows. Rows with a colour are the six you will meet most (step 1).

- The near words start with هَـ. The far words end in ـكَ.
- Each set has one form for “one male”, one for “one female”, one for “two” and one for “three or more”.
- The plural has one form. The Corpus tags هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ and أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ as plural, with no gender.
- The dual changes with the job of the word: -āni for the nominative, -ayni for the accusative and genitive.

<Note kind="know">Quran Learn searched the Corpus for dual pointing words. They occur in four places only: هَٰذَانِ (22:19), هَٰذَٰنِ (20:63), هَٰتَيْنِ (28:27) and ذَٰنِكَ (28:32). The other dual forms in the chart are the textbook forms. Quran Learn did not find them among the pointing words of the Corpus.</Note>

The pointing word matches the thing it points at, as an adjective does (L2.09). The Corpus tags هَٰذَا as male and هَٰذِهِۦ as female, one each.

- 3:138: بَيَانٌ is male, so the pointing word is هَٰذَا.
- 7:73: نَاقَة is female, so the pointing word is هَٰذِهِۦ.

L1.08 introduced the dual: exactly two. A pointing word has a dual too.

- In 22:19 the mubtadaʾ is هَٰذَانِ. It ends in **-āni**, the nominative shape. Its khabar خَصْمَانِ matches it: two, nominative (L2.02).
- In 28:27, هَٰتَيْنِ ends in **-ayni**. Al-Jadwal says it is a genitive word, a “clarifier” (ʿaṭf bayān) of ٱبْنَتَىَّ, and calls it built on the yāʾ, in the place of the genitive. It is female, because “daughters” is.

So the dual pointing word shows its job by its shape. That is the one place in this lesson where the ending of a pointing word changes.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal calls the dual pointing words built too: on the alif in 20:63 and 28:32, on the yāʾ in 28:27. Al-Durr al-Maṣūn records another view in its notes on 20:63, إِنْ هَٰذَٰنِ لَسَٰحِرَٰنِ. It lists several readings. In Abū ʿAmr’s, the word is هَٰذَيْنِ, and it is the ism of a heavy إِنَّ, accusative with the yāʾ. In the reading of our text, al-Jadwal takes هَٰذَٰنِ as the mubtadaʾ, built on the alif.</Note>

In this verse Mūsā is told to put his hand into his collar, and it comes out white. Al-Durr al-Maṣūn says “those two” points at the staff and the hand. Both words are female in Arabic. But the pointing word is **male**: ذَٰنِكَ.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn explains it. The pointing word is male because its khabar, بُرْهَٰنَانِ, is male. It says a pointing word can be made female in the same way, to match a female khabar.

So the pointing word does not always copy the noun it points at. Here it matches the khabar.

For three or more there is one far word, أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ, and one near word, هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ. They have no separate male and female form.

In 2:157 the sentence has a sentence inside it, as in L2.01. أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ is the mubtadaʾ. Its khabar is the whole sentence عَلَيْهِمْ صَلَوَٰتٌ, where عَلَيْهِمْ comes first (khabar muqaddam).

In 2:31 the near word follows بِأَسْمَآءِ. Al-Jadwal says هَا is an attention word and أُولَاءِ is the second word of the idafah, in the place of the genitive. Step 3 comes back to this.

The kāf of a far word is a letter of address, so it changes with the people spoken to. In ذَٰلِكُمُ the ending is **-kum**, for a group of men. Al-Jadwal says the lām is “for distance” and the ending is “for the one spoken to”.

The ending can differ: ذَٰلِكَ has -ka, ذَٰلِكُمْ has -kum for a group of men, and ذَٰلِكُنَّ has -kunna for a group of women. Step 4 shows ذَٰلِكُنَّ (12:32).

The word is the same pointing word each time. In 6:102 it is the mubtadaʾ, ٱللَّهُ is its khabar, and رَبُّكُمْ is a second khabar. The -kum of رَبُّكُمْ is the pronoun “your”. The -kum inside ذَٰلِكُمُ is not.

<Step id="job" label="The job" title="What a pointing word does in the sentence">
<Block id="built" title="It keeps its shape">
  <Stage label="Built on a fixed ending">
    <Tiles
      label="ذَا sukūn, ذِهِ kasra, ذَانِ alif, تَيْنِ yāʾ"

Most pointing words keep one shape whatever the job. The grammar books say they are **built**, مَبْنِيّ (*mabnī*). You cannot read the job from the ending, so the books say where the word stands: **in the place of** the nominative, the accusative or the genitive. You met this with pronouns in L2.01 and L2.05.

Al-Jadwal names the fixed ending where it speaks of it. ذَا in 2:2 is built on the sukūn. ذِهِ in 2:35 is built on the kasra. The dual is built on the alif or on the yāʾ.

Here is what you will see in this step:

| Job | Verse | The pointing word is in the place of |
|---|---|---|
| mubtadaʾ | 2:157 | the nominative |
| ism inna | 3:62 | the accusative |
| object | 2:35 | the accusative |
| second word of an idafah | 2:31 | the genitive |
</Block>

The most common job is the mubtadaʾ, as in 3:138, 6:102 and 22:19. A pointing word has no ending to show it, so it stands “in the place of” the nominative.

In 2:157 the khabar is a whole sentence. Al-Jadwal says the sentence عَلَيْهِمْ صَلَوَٰتٌ is in the place of the nominative, as the khabar of أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ.

The khabar can be a sentence, a phrase or a place word (L2.01). A pointing word does not change that.
</Block>

After إِنَّ the first part is accusative (L2.10). A pointing word cannot show that, so it stands in the place of the accusative. Al-Jadwal says هَا is an attention word, and ذَا is “built, in the place of the accusative, the ism of inna”.

The khabar is ٱلْقَصَصُ, “the account”, with its adjective ٱلْحَقُّ. The word between them, هُوَ, is the separating pronoun you met in L2.10. Al-Jadwal also allows another reading of it, as a mubtadaʾ.

In 2:31 the pointing word is the second word of an idafah, “the names of these”. The second word of an idafah is genitive (L2.08). A pointing word keeps its shape, so it stands in the place of the genitive. Al-Jadwal says exactly this.

A pointing word after a preposition works the same way. L2.07 gave ذَٰلِكَ as an example. In L2.10 you saw فِى ذَٰلِكَ in 26:8.

<Block id="noun-after" title="The noun after a pointing word">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:2 (start)"
      groups={[

This verse is the first one after Al-Fatiha, and it starts with a pointing word. Look at what follows it: ٱلْكِتَٰبُ, “the Book”.

You have two nouns side by side, both definite and both in the -u form. In L2.09 you saw that the second one is not always an adjective. Al-Jadwal reads ٱلْكِتَٰبُ as a **badal** of ذَٰلِكَ, and also allows **ʿaṭf bayān**. Both mean the same thing for you: ٱلْكِتَٰبُ names what “that” points at.

Then the news comes after: لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ, “there is no doubt in it”, is the khabar, and هُدًى, “a guidance”, is a second khabar. So the verse says: “That Book: no doubt in it, a guidance for those who fear Allah.”

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal also allows ٱلْكِتَٰبُ to be the khabar of ذَٰلِكَ: “That is the Book”, with لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ describing the state. Both readings are in al-Jadwal. The online copy of al-Durr al-Maṣūn that we used has no notes on this verse.</Note>

The noun after a pointing word can have more than one job. Here are the verses so far:

| Verse | After the pointing word | Al-Jadwal reads it as |
|---|---|---|
| 3:138 | بَيَانٌ | khabar (it is indefinite) |
| 7:73 | نَاقَةُ ٱللَّهِ | khabar (an idafah) |

A way to start: look at what follows. In 2:253 the news, فَضَّلْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ, “We preferred some of them”, comes right after, so ٱلرُّسُلُ explains the pointing word. In 3:138 nothing else is the news, so بَيَانٌ is the khabar.

This is a first guide, not a law. Al-Jadwal allows more than one reading in 2:2 and 2:253, and al-Durr al-Maṣūn lists the same choices for 2:253: naʿt, ʿaṭf bayān or badal of تِلْكَ, with فَضَّلْنَا as the news.

<Step id="word-by-word" label="Mistakes, word by word" title="Four mistakes, and four verses word by word">
<Block id="mistake-case" title="Mistake 1: looking for the case on the pointing word">
  <Stage label="The ending does not show the job">
    <Tiles
      label="Same shape, three jobs"

        { ar: 'هَٰذَا', en: 'mubtadaʾ (3:138)' },

Learners look for -u, -a or -i on a pointing word, as they do on a noun. Most pointing words have none. هَٰذَا looks the same as a mubtadaʾ in 3:138 and as the ism of inna in 3:62.

Ask where the word stands: first in the sentence, after إِنَّ, after a verb, after a little word. That tells you the job. Only the dual changes shape (step 2).

The ـكُمْ at the end of ذَٰلِكُمُ looks like the pronoun “you all” you know from L3.02. It is not. Al-Jadwal calls it a letter of address. The word does not mean “your that”. It means “that”, said to a group.

The ـكُمْ on رَبُّكُمْ is the pronoun. It is joined to رَبّ and means “your”. The Arabic spelling is the same. The job is different.

هَٰذَا ٱلْكِتَابُ can mean “this book”, a phrase that is not finished. It can also mean “this is the book”, a full sentence. Two definite words side by side leave the choice open, as in L2.02. The words that follow, and the meaning, decide.

هَٰذَا كِتَابٌ is a full sentence: “This is a book.” The second word is indefinite, so it is the news about هَٰذَا. 3:138 is a verse of this kind. It is a correct sentence.

Verbs have moods, not cases.', irab: 'مضارع مجزوم وعلامة جزمه حذف النون، والألف ضمير متصل في محل رفع فاعل' },
        { at: 2, ar: 'هَا', role: 'a little word of attention', why: 'Little words have no case.', irab: 'ها حرف تنبيه' },
        { at: 2, ar: 'ذِهِ', role: 'the pointing word, “this”, near and female. Here the pointing word is an object, so it stands in the place of the accusative. Its badal ٱلشَّجَرَةَ shows the -a that the pointing word hides.

This is the clearest case of the way a pointing word and its noun work together. The noun brings the ending, and the pointing word brings the meaning “this”.
</Block>

<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: forms, jobs and the noun after">
  <Stage label="Four questions to ask of a pointing word">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Near or far?</b> Look for هَـ or for ـكَ.</li>
      <li><b>How many, and what gender?</b> One male, one female, two, or many.</li>
      <li><b>What job?</b> Mubtadaʾ, ism inna, object, or after a little word.</li>
      <li><b>What is the noun after?</b> A khabar, a badal, or nothing.</li>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Say what each word means, and whether it is near or far: (a) هَٰذَا (b) هَٰذِهِ (c) ذَٰلِكَ (d) تِلْكَ (e) هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ (f) أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ">

- (a) هَٰذَا: “this”, near, one male.
- (b) هَٰذِهِ: “this”, near, one female.
- (c) ذَٰلِكَ: “that”, far, one male.
- (d) تِلْكَ: “that”, far, one female.
- (e) هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ: “these”, near, many.
- (f) أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ: “those”, far, many.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Choose the pointing word that fits each noun. (a) ____ ٱلْمَسْجِدُ, “this mosque” (b) ____ ٱلْآيَةُ, “this verse” (c) ____ ٱلْيَوْمُ, “that day” (d) ____ ٱلْجَنَّةُ, “that garden” (e) ____ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ, “these believers”. These are made-up examples.">

- (a) هَٰذَا ٱلْمَسْجِدُ: a male noun, near.
- (b) هَٰذِهِ ٱلْآيَةُ: a female noun, near.
- (c) ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْيَوْمُ: a male noun, far.
- (d) تِلْكَ ٱلْجَنَّةُ: a female noun, far.
- (e) هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ: many, near.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Go through ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْكِتَٰبُ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ (Al-Baqarah 2:2, start). Meaning: “That Book: there is no doubt in it.” (a) Which word points? (b) Near or far, one or many, male or female? (c) What is its job and its case? (d) What job has ٱلْكِتَٰبُ?">

- (a) ذَٰلِكَ.
- (b) Far, one, male. The lām (ل) shows distance, and the kāf (ك) is for the one spoken to.
- (c) It is the **mubtadaʾ**. It stands in the place of the nominative. It is built on the sukūn, so you cannot see the case.
- (d) Al-Jadwal reads ٱلْكِتَٰبُ as a **badal** of ذَٰلِكَ (or ʿaṭf bayān). It is nominative, -u, like the word before it. The news about the pointing word is لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Say the number, gender and ending of each dual. (a) هَٰذَانِ خَصْمَانِ (Al-Hajj 22:19, part) (b) إِحْدَى ٱبْنَتَىَّ هَٰتَيْنِ (Al-Qasas 28:27, part)">

- (a) هَٰذَانِ: near, **dual**, male. The ending -āni is the nominative shape, so it is a mubtadaʾ. خَصْمَانِ is dual too and matches it.
- (b) هَٰتَيْنِ: near, **dual**, female. The ending -ayni is the accusative or genitive shape. Al-Jadwal says it is genitive, built on the yāʾ, and a clarifier (ʿaṭf bayān) of ٱبْنَتَىَّ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="What job has the pointing word? Say which case it stands in the place of. (a) إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَهُوَ ٱلْقَصَصُ ٱلْحَقُّ (Al-Imran 3:62, start) (b) بِأَسْمَآءِ هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ (Al-Baqarah 2:31, part) (c) أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ عَلَيْهِمْ صَلَوَٰتٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:157, start)">

- (a) هَٰذَا is the **ism inna**, in the place of the accusative.
- (b) هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ is the **second word of the idafah** “the names of these”, in the place of the genitive.
- (c) أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ is the **mubtadaʾ**, in the place of the nominative. Its khabar is the sentence عَلَيْهِمْ صَلَوَٰتٌ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Find the mistake and correct it. (a) هَٰذَا ٱلسُّورَةُ (b) هَٰذِهِ ٱلْكِتَابُ (c) هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ ٱلْكِتَابُ. These are made-up examples.">

- (a) سُورَة is female, so use هَٰذِهِ: هَٰذِهِ ٱلسُّورَةُ, “this surah”.
- (b) كِتَاب is male, so use هَٰذَا: هَٰذَا ٱلْكِتَابُ, “this book”.
- (c) هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ points at many. ٱلْكِتَابُ is one, so use هَٰذَا.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="In فَذَٰنِكَ بُرْهَٰنَانِ (Al-Qasas 28:32, part) the pointing word points at the hand and the staff, which are female words. Why is the pointing word male?">

It matches its khabar, بُرْهَٰنَانِ, which is male. Al-Durr al-Maṣūn says the pointing word is male because the khabar is male, and that it can be female in the same way when the khabar is female.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Sentence or phrase? Say what each could mean. (a) هَٰذَا رَجُلٌ (b) هَٰذَا ٱلرَّجُلُ. These are made-up examples.">

- (a) A **sentence**: “This is a man.” رَجُلٌ is indefinite, so it is the khabar.
- (b) **Either**: the phrase “this man”, or the sentence “This is the man.” Both words are definite, so the words that follow and the meaning decide.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Go through ذَٰلِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ رَبُّكُمْ (Al-Anʿam 6:102, start). (a) What is the mubtadaʾ, and what do its parts do? (b) What is the khabar? (c) What is the -kum of رَبُّكُمْ, and how is it different from the -kum in ذَٰلِكُمُ?">

- (a) ذَٰلِكُمُ is the mubtadaʾ, “that”. The lām shows distance. The ending is the letter of address for a group of men.
- (b) ٱللَّهُ is the khabar. رَبُّكُمْ is a second khabar.
- (c) The -kum of رَبُّكُمْ is the pronoun “your”, joined to رَبّ. The -kum in ذَٰلِكُمُ is a letter of address, not a pronoun.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={10} q="What is the pointing word and what is the “who” word? What does each do? فَذَٰلِكُنَّ ٱلَّذِى لُمْتُنَّنِى فِيهِ (Yusuf 12:32, part)">

ذَٰلِكُنَّ is the pointing word. It is the mubtadaʾ, far, one, male, and the ending -kunna speaks to a group of women. ٱلَّذِى is the “who” word. Al-Jadwal says it is the khabar, and لُمْتُنَّنِى فِيهِ is its clause. L3.11 teaches this kind of word.

</Exercise>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="pointing word plus the thing it points at makes a phrase"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'pointing word', small: 'اِسْم إِشَارَة' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'the thing pointed at', small: 'a noun after it' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'this book, that day', small: 'a phrase' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- A pointing word is a definite noun. English has four (*this, that, these, those*). Arabic has ten forms.
- Near words start with هَـ, an attention word. Far words end in ـكَ. The lām is for distance, and the kāf is for the one spoken to.
- It matches the noun in gender and number: one male, one female, two, many. A pointing word can also match its khabar (28:32).
- Most pointing words are built, with one shape. They stand in the place of the nominative, accusative or genitive. Only the dual changes shape: -āni, -ayni.
- The noun after a pointing word can be a badal, ʿaṭf bayān or the khabar. Read the sentence to decide.
- The kāf changes with the people spoken to: ذَٰلِكَ, ذَٰلِكُمْ, ذَٰلِكُنَّ. It is not the pronoun “you”.

**Next:** L3.11 teaches the “who” words, which also point to a noun but start a clause. If anything here felt new, review L1.07 and L1.08 (gender and number), L2.01 and L2.02 (the nominal sentence), and L2.09 (the badal).
