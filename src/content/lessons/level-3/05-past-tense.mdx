<Block id="name" title="The past verb">
  <Stage label="From al-Ajurrumiyyah">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote" lang="ar" dir="rtl">
      <p>اَلْأَفْعَالُ ثَلَاثَةٌ: مَاضٍ وَمُضَارِعٌ وَأَمْرٌ، نَحْوُ ضَرَبَ وَيَضْرِبُ وَاضْرِبْ</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>
  </Stage>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* is a short grammar book by Ibn Ājurrūm (died 723 AH). In its chapter on verbs it says there are three kinds: past, present and command. Its examples are ضَرَبَ, يَضْرِبُ and اضْرِبْ: “he hit”, “he hits” and “hit!”.

The past verb is called فِعْل مَاضٍ (*fiʿl māḍin*), “a verb that has passed”. It names an action that is finished. The Quran uses it to say what happened: قَالَ (he said), خَلَقَ (he created), كَانَ (he was).

The same chapter describes the past verb as “open at the end” (مَفْتُوحُ الْآخِرِ): the last letter has a fatḥa, as in ضَرَبَ. The next step shows what happens to that last letter when an ending is added.

<Note kind="know">The present verb is L3.06 and the command is L3.08. L3.09 shows the past verb with the doer left out (the passive).</Note>

<Check n={2} q="Which of the three verbs in the book is the past verb?" options={['ضَرَبَ', 'يَضْرِبُ', 'اضْرِبْ']} answer={0} why="ضَرَبَ, “he hit”, is finished. يَضْرِبُ is the present (L3.06) and اضْرِبْ is a command (L3.08)." />
</Block>

L2.03 showed that a verb can carry its doer as an ending: -tu (“I”), -ū (“they”) and -nā (“we”). In this verse the verb وَكَتَبْنَا ends in **-nā**. No separate word for “we” is written.

This lesson gives the whole set. The root here is ك ت ب, “writing” (L3.03). It stays the same in each form. The ending is what changes:

- كَتَبَ, “he wrote”
- كَتَبُوا, “they wrote”
- كَتَبْتَ, “you wrote”

So you can say who did the action without looking for a pronoun. The separate pronouns هُوَ, أَنْتَ and نَحْنُ are L3.01. A verse does not need them, because the ending already names the doer.

</Block>
</Step>

The verb is كَتَبَ (*kataba*), “he wrote”, root ك ت ب. It is a real Quran verb: you saw its -nā form in 7:145. The Quran does not use all 13 forms of each verb. The table shows the full pattern, and the verses in this lesson show real forms of other verbs.

The **ending** column shows the last root letter with its vowel, then the letters that are added.

- **He** has no added letters. It is the plain form, and this course uses it to name a verb.
- **She** adds ـَتْ.
- **The two** add an alif: ـَا for men, ـَتَا for women. The ت marks the feminine, as in “she”.
- **They (men)** add ـُوا. **They (women)** add ـْنَ.

The pronoun column shows the words from L3.01. The verb does not need them.

There are five “you” forms, then “I” and “we”. The form for “you two” is the same for two men, two women, or one of each, so the table has one row for it.

Count them: six third-person forms, five “you” forms and two forms for “I” and “we” make **13 forms**.

Six of the seven endings in this table start with the letter ت. The ت is the doer: “you” or “I”. The seventh ending is -nā, “we”, and it starts with ن.

<Note kind="know">Counted in the Quranic Arabic Corpus file by its person tags (Form I past verbs, passives left out, weak verbs included), the Quran has 3,600 verbs tagged “he” and 2,129 tagged “they (men)”. It has 36 tagged “they (women)” and 2 tagged “you two”. These are Quran Learn's counts, and some tags are unreliable. The full table is still worth knowing, because the endings do not change from verb to verb.</Note>

<Block id="sukun" title="The sukūn falls on the last root letter">
  <Stage label="كَتَبْتُ, “I wrote”, letter by letter">
    <Tiles
      label="ك ت ب and the ending تُ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'كَ', en: 'root letter 1', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'تَ', en: 'root letter 2', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'بْ', en: 'root letter 3, with a sukūn', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'تُ', en: 'the ending: “I”', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Compare كَتَبَ with كَتَبْتُ. In كَتَبَ the last root letter ب has a fatḥa. In كَتَبْتُ it has a **sukūn**, ـْ.

The ending تُ starts with a letter that has its own vowel. So the last root letter before it closes the syllable: كَتَبْ + تُ. The same happens before -ta, -ti, -tumā, -tum, -tunna, -nā and -na.

The sukūn is on the **last** root letter, the ب. It is not on the middle letter ت. Say it slowly: *ka-ta-b-tu*. The vowels of the first two root letters (a, a) stay the same in these forms.

<Note kind="warn">Look at the sukūn in the ending column of the tables. It sits on the last root letter, before the letters that are added.</Note>

A verb for “she” ends in ـَتْ: a fatḥa on the last root letter, then a ت with a sukūn. The grammar books call this ت **tāʾ al-taʾnīth**, “the tāʾ of feminine”. It also shows when the doer is a written feminine word: جَآءَتِ ٱلْمَرْأَةُ. L2.03 taught this match, and L1.07 introduced it.

This ت is an ordinary tā. It is **not** the tāʾ marbūṭa, ة, that you saw on nouns in L1.07. The ة belongs to nouns, like رَحْمَةٌ. A verb ends in a plain ت.

In جَآءَتِ the ت carries a kasra, so it can join the next word ٱلْمَرْأَةُ.

The verse is about Adam and his wife. The verb فَأَكَلَا ends in **-ā**. It means two doers (the dual of L1.08). The doer is the ending, “the two of them”.

The verb فَبَدَتْ ends in -at. Its doer is سَوْءَٰتُهُمَا, a feminine plural word, so the verb is feminine singular, as L2.03 taught.

The verb ءَامَنَّا has a shadda on the نَ. It comes from the **root**, not from the pattern.

The root is ء م ن. Its last letter is ن. The ending -nā starts with ن too. First the last root letter takes its sukūn, as in كَتَبْتُ: ءَامَنْ + نَا. Then the two nūns meet and are written as one with a shadda: ءَامَنَّا.

The same happens to any verb whose last root letter is ن. The endings are the same on the other forms of the verb. ءَامَنَ is Form IV (L3.13), and أَنزَلْتَ in the same verse is Form IV with the ending -ta.

<Block id="groups" title="Five groups to remember">
  <Stage label="Which letters tell you who?">
    <Tiles
      label="The five groups of endings"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ـَ ـَتْ', en: 'he, she', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ـَا ـَتَا', en: 'the two', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ـُوا ـْنَ', en: 'they', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ـْتَ … ـْتُ', en: 'you, I', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ـْنَا', en: 'we', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

You do not need to learn 13 endings one by one. They fall into five groups.

| Group | Endings | Who |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | -a, -at | he, she |
| 2 | -ā, -atā | the two (men, women) |
| 3 | -ū, -na | they (men), they (women) |
| 4 | -ta, -ti, -tumā, -tum, -tunna, -tu | you (any), I: the ending starts with ت |
| 5 | -nā | we |

Look at the end of the word. A ت that starts the ending means “you” or “I”. An alif after -a or -at means “the two”. A و with an alif means “they (men)”. ـْنَا means “we”, and ـْنَ means “they (women)”.

<Note kind="tip">A ت at the **end** of a past verb names the doer. A ت at the **start** of a verb is a different sign: the present tense (L3.06).</Note>
</Block>

The endings do not belong to كَتَبَ alone. Other past verbs take them too.

A Form I verb can have a fatḥa, a kasra or a ḍamma on its middle letter (L3.04). The endings stay the same:

| Middle vowel | He | I | They (men) |
|---|---|---|---|
| fatḥa | كَتَبَ | كَتَبْتُ | كَتَبُوا |
| kasra | سَمِعَ | سَمِعْتُ | سَمِعُوا |
| ḍamma | حَسُنَ | حَسُنْتُ | حَسُنُوا |

The same endings fit the other forms of the verb too (L3.12 to L3.18). The stem changes, and the ending does not: ءَامَنَ gives ءَامَنَّا, and أَنزَلَ gives أَنزَلْتَ.

<Note kind="know">Al-Kawthar (108:1, L2.12) has the verb أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ. It is أَعْطَى, “he gave” (Form IV, L3.13), with the ending **-nā**, “we”, and then the attached **-ka**, “you”, as its object (L3.02). Al-Jadwal reads the verb as built on a sukūn, as for any form with -nā.</Note>

Some past verbs look different from كَتَبَ. They take the **same endings**, but the stem changes shape. Two kinds are very common:

- **Hollow** verbs (أَجْوَف): the **middle** root letter is و or ي. قَالَ (root ق و ل) and كَانَ (root ك و ن) are hollow. Hollow verbs are 26.7% of the verbs in the Quran.
- **Defective** verbs (نَاقِص): the **last** root letter is و or ي. دَعَا (root د ع و) and رَمَىٰ (root ر م ي) are defective. Defective verbs are 18.0% of the verbs in the Quran.

Both percentages are Quran Learn's count from the Quranic Arabic Corpus. قَالَ and كَانَ are the 7th and 10th most common words of the Quran, by the same count.

| Kind | He | I | They (men) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular | كَتَبَ | كَتَبْتُ | كَتَبُوا |
| Hollow | قَالَ | قُلْتُ | قَالُوا |
| Hollow | كَانَ | كُنتُ | كَانُوا |
| Defective | دَعَا | دَعَوْتُ | دَعَوْا |
| Defective | رَمَىٰ | رَمَيْتُ | رَمَوْا |

Look at what you can see. The endings are the same, or nearly the same: -tu, -ta, -ū (in دَعَوْا it is a w sound after the a), and the hidden “he”. But the long ā of قَالَ is missing in قُلْتُ, and the ي of رَمَىٰ shows in رَمَيْتَ. You can read all of these verses with the endings you know.

The verses show it: وَإِذْ قَالَ ٱللَّهُ opens 5:116, and the same verse has قُلْتَ and قُلْتُهُۥ. In 71:5 Nuh says دَعَوْتُ قَوْمِى, “I called my people”.

In this lesson you **recognise** these verbs. L4.11 (weak verbs: the idea), L4.12 (hollow verbs) and L4.13 (defective verbs) teach why the stem changes and how to build the forms. L4.14 and L4.15 cover two more kinds.

<Note kind="know">The word نَاقِص is also used for كَانَ and its sisters (L2.11), where it means “incomplete”: they need a khabar. Here it names a verb whose last root letter is weak.</Note>

<Step id="irab" label="I'rab and mistakes" title="The iʿrāb line, four mistakes, and four verses word by word">
<Block id="irab-line" title="How a past verb is written in iʿrāb">
  <Stage label="Built on …">
    <Tiles
      label="Three sounds a past verb can be built on"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلْفَتْح', en: 'fatḥa: -a, -at, -ā', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ٱلضَّمّ', en: 'ḍamma: -ū', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ٱلسُّكُون', en: 'sukūn: -tu, -ta, -nā, -na', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A past verb has **no case**. Its last sound is fixed. The grammar books say it is **built**, مَبْنِيّ (*mabnī*). The words in L2.04 to L2.06 that change with case are called مُعْرَب (*muʿrab*).

So the iʿrāb line of a past verb names the sound it is built on: فعل ماض مبني على الفتح (or الضم, or السكون). The ending decides which one, and the line then names the ending's job.

| Ending | Built on | The ending is |
|---|---|---|
| none (he), -at (she) | الفتح | for “he” and “she”, a hidden doer: ضمير مستتر. In -at, the ت is the sign of feminine (تاء التأنيث) |
| -ā, -atā | الفتح | the alif is the doer: الألف فاعل |
| -ū | الضم | the waw is the doer: الواو فاعل |
| -ta, -ti, -tumā, -tum, -tunna, -tu | السكون | the tāʾ is the doer: التاء فاعل |
| -nā | السكون | nā is the doer: نا فاعل |
| -na | السكون | the nūn is the doer: النون فاعل |

Al-Jadwal words these lines in the verses of this lesson: نا فاعل in 7:145, النون فاعل in 2:234, الواو فاعل in 2:6, الألف فاعل in 20:121, and التاء فاعل in 8:17.

<Note kind="know">The doer endings are attached pronouns, so some books write “a pronoun in the place of the nominative” (ضمير في محل رفع فاعل). It is the same analysis in longer words.</Note>

</Block>

<Block id="mistake-sukun" title="Mistake 1: a vowel before -tu">
  <Stage label="Which one is a word?">
    <Tiles
      label="كَتَبْتُ is a word. كَتَبَتُ is not."
      items={[
        { ar: 'كَتَبْتُ', en: 'I wrote: correct', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'كَتَبَتُ', en: 'not a word', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

It is easy to keep the fatḥa of كَتَبَ and add تُ. That gives كَتَبَتُ. It looks like “I wrote”, but it is not a word.

Before -tu, -ta, -nā and the other consonant endings, the last root letter takes a sukūn: كَتَبْتُ. The sukūn is on the ب, the last root letter, and not on the middle letter.

The ending -at is different: it keeps the fatḥa, كَتَبَتْ. Read the ending, then fix the vowel.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-lookalike" title="Mistake 2: endings that look alike">
  <Stage label="Look at the last letters">
    <Tiles
      label="Three look-alikes"
      items={[
        { ar: 'كَتَبْنَ', en: 'they (women) wrote', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'كَتَبْنَا', en: 'we wrote', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'يَكْتُبْنَ', en: 'they (women) write: present', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Four pairs are easy to mix up.

- **-na and -nā.** One alif changes “they (women)” into “we”.
- **Past -na and present يَكْتُبْنَ.** The past verb has no prefix. The present starts with يَ (L3.06).
- **-ā and -atā.** كَتَبَا is two men, and كَتَبَتَا is two women. The ت marks the feminine, as it does for “she”.
- **-at and ة.** The ت at the end of كَتَبَتْ is an ordinary tā. It is not the tāʾ marbūṭa of L1.07.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-attached" title="Mistake 3: reading the end of the verb as one thing">
  <Stage label="Two jobs in one word">
    <Tiles
      label="كَتَبْتُهُ: root, doer, object"
      items={[
        { ar: 'كَتَبْ', en: 'root ك ت ب', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'تُ', en: 'the doer: “I”', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'هُ', en: 'the object: “it”', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A past verb can end in more than one piece. In كَتَبْتُهُ the **-tu** is the doer, “I”. The **-hu** is the object, “it” or “him”. The meaning is “I wrote it”.

The doer ending comes first, and the object ending comes after it. L3.02 teaches the object endings. L2.03 taught that a pronoun on a verb can be the doer or the object.

<Note kind="warn">Do not read -hu as “he”. It is the object. The doer is -tu.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-forms" title="Mistake 4: thinking the endings belong to Form I">
  <Stage label="One ending, two forms">
    <Tiles
      label="Form I and Form IV take the same -nā"

The 13 endings are not special to Form I. They fit the past verbs of Forms II to X too (L3.12 to L3.18). The stem changes: كَتَبَ is Form I and أَنزَلَ is Form IV. Both take -nā.

The verbs that change shape (قَالَ, كَانَ, رَمَىٰ) take the same endings as well. The stem looks different, and the endings do not.
</Block>

        { at: 3, ar: 'مَرَّتْ', role: 'a past-tense verb: “she passed”', case: 'a verb has no case; it is built', mark: 'a fatḥa on the last root letter', why: 'It has the same ending as حَمَلَتْ: -at, with a hidden “she”.', irab: 'فعل ماض مبني على الفتح مثل حملت، والتاء تاء التأنيث، والفاعل ضمير مستتر تقديره هي' },
        { at: 4, role: '“with it”: bi- and the pronoun hu. It belongs to the verb.', why: 'It says what she passed with.', irab: 'جار ومجرور متعلق بمرت' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Here the ending is **-at** and the doer is hidden: “she”. The verb is built on a fatḥa, because -at keeps the fatḥa of the stem.

Compare this verb with كَتَبَتْ in the table. The two work the same way.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal gives a second reading of حَمْلًا: an object meaning “the thing carried”. This lesson follows its first reading, the noun made from the verb.</Note>
</Block>

<Exercise n={3} q="Find the past verb in each, then say its root and who did it: (a) وَقَالُوا۟ سَمِعْنَا وَأَطَعْنَا (Al-Baqarah 2:285, part) (b) قَالُوٓا۟ ءَامَنَّا بِرَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ (Ash-Shuara 26:47) (c) خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مِن صَلْصَٰلٍ كَٱلْفَخَّارِ (Ar-Rahman 55:14)">

- (a) **سَمِعْنَا**: root س م ع (hearing), ending -nā, “we heard”. **أَطَعْنَا**: root ط و ع (obeying), ending -nā, “we obeyed”. It is a hollow verb (Form IV), and its stem changes. **قَالُوا**: root ق و ل, ending -ū, “they said”. It is also hollow.
- (b) **قَالُوا**: root ق و ل, ending -ū, “they said”. **ءَامَنَّا**: root ء م ن, ending -nā, “we believed”. The shadda comes from the root's last letter ن.
- (c) **خَلَقَ**: root خ ل ق (creating), no ending, “he created”. The doer is hidden.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Look at كَذَّبَتْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ ٱلْمُرْسَلِينَ (Ash-Shuara 26:105). Say the ending of the verb, the doer, and why the verb is feminine. The root is ك ذ ب.">

The ending is **-at**: the tāʾ of “she”. The verb is built on a fatḥa. The root is ك ذ ب, “lying, denying”. The shadda on the ذ shows Form II, which L3.12 teaches. The ending works the same way.

The doer is قَوْمُ نُوحٍ, “the people of Nuh”, a written word that comes after the verb. Al-Jadwal says the verb is feminine because the doer means a group or a nation, and not because of the shape of the word قَوْم.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="What does each ending tell you? (a) كَتَبُوا (b) كَتَبْنَا (c) كَتَبَتَا (d) كَتَبْتُمْ (e) كَتَبْنَ">

- (a) **-ū**: they (men) wrote.
- (b) **-nā**: we wrote.
- (c) **-atā**: the two (women) wrote.
- (d) **-tum**: you all (men) wrote.
- (e) **-na**: they (women) wrote.

Check (b) and (e). One alif is the difference: كَتَبْنَا is “we” and كَتَبْنَ is “they (women)”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Hunt. Open Al-Baqarah 2:1 to 2:10. Find three past verbs of Form I. For each, say the ending and who did it.">

Past verbs of Form I in these ten verses include:

- **رَزَقْنَٰهُمْ** (2:3): root ر ز ق, “we gave them”. The ending -nā is the doer. The -hum is the object.
- **كَفَرُوا۟** (2:6): root ك ف ر, “they disbelieved”. The ending -ū is the doer.
- **خَتَمَ** (2:7): root خ ت م, “he sealed”. The doer is a hidden “he”. Here it is Allah, written after the verb: خَتَمَ ٱللَّهُ.

Others are كَانُوا۟ (2:10, hollow) and the Form IV verbs ءَامَنَّا (2:8) and ءَأَنذَرْتَهُمْ (2:6).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Which of these is a regular verb: قُلْتُ, كَتَبْتُ, كُنتُ, رَمَيْتُ? Say what the ending means in each.">

**كَتَبْتُ** is regular: the three root letters ك ت ب all show and do not change.

The others change shape. قُلْتُ and كُنتُ are hollow (the middle root letter is و). رَمَيْتُ is defective (the last root letter is ي).

All four end in **-tu**, “I”. The ending is the same, even where the stem changes.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Write the iʿrāb line of the verb: (a) كَتَبَتْ (b) كَتَبُوا (c) كَتَبْنَا (d) كَتَبَا (e) كَتَبْتُمْ">

- (a) فعل ماض مبني على الفتح، والتاء تاء التأنيث، والفاعل ضمير مستتر تقديره هي
- (b) فعل ماض مبني على الضم، والواو فاعل
- (c) فعل ماض مبني على السكون، ونا فاعل
- (d) فعل ماض مبني على الفتح، والألف فاعل
- (e) فعل ماض مبني على السكون، والتاء فاعل، والميم لجمع الذكور

The ending decides the sound the verb is built on. The doer is the ending, except for “he” and “she”, where it is hidden.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={10} q="Is فَعَلْنَ “we did” or “they (women) did”? Look at فِيمَا فَعَلْنَ (Al-Baqarah 2:234, part).">

**They (women) did.** The ending is -na, with no alif. “We did” would be فَعَلْنَا. Al-Jadwal reads فَعَلْنَ like بَلَغْنَ in the same verse: a past verb built on a sukūn, with the nūn as the doer.

The women are the widows of the verse, so the meaning fits.

</Exercise>

  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="stem plus ending equals a past verb"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'stem', small: 'the root and its vowels' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'ending', small: 'tells who did it' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'past verb', small: 'فِعْل مَاضٍ' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- The past verb names a finished action. The plain form, كَتَبَ, means “he wrote”.
- The **ending** names the doer. There are **13 forms**, in five groups: he and she, the two, they, you and I (the ending starts with ت), and we.
- Before -ta, -ti, -tum, -tu, -nā and the other consonant endings, the sukūn falls on the **last root letter**: كَتَبْتُ.
- The ت of -at is the tāʾ of “she”, an ordinary tā. It is not the ة of nouns.
- Hollow verbs (قَالَ, كَانَ) and defective verbs (دَعَا, رَمَىٰ) change shape but take the same endings. L4.11 to L4.13 teach the rules.
- The iʿrāb line names the sound the verb is built on: فتح (he, she, the two), ضم (-ū), سكون (the consonant endings). The doer is the ending, or a hidden pronoun.
