L1.01 gave you the letters and the three short vowels: *a*, *i* and *u*. Real Quran text has more marks than that. This one short verse has four more kinds.

Read it from right to left. Every word has a mark that is new to you. By the end of this lesson you will say the whole verse correctly and know why.

The four kinds in this verse:

1. **Long vowels** (يَدَآ, أَبِى)
2. **Sukun** (the small circle on the last ت of تَبَّتْ)
3. **Shadda** (the sign above the ب in تَبَّتْ and وَتَبَّ)
4. **Tanwīn** (the doubled kasra under the ب of لَهَبٍ)

A fifth kind, the **glide**, is not in this verse. You meet it in Al-Kawthar.

A long vowel is a short vowel held for about **two beats** instead of one. Arabic makes it by putting a **stretching letter** after the short vowel. The letter has no vowel of its own. Its only job is to make the sound longer.

| Short vowel | Becomes | Stretching letter after it | Word in this verse |
|---|---|---|---|
| a (fatḥa) | ā | ا alif | مَا (*mā*) |
| i (kasra) | ī | ي yā | جِيدِهَا (*jīdihā*, Al-Masad 111:5) |
| u (ḍamma) | ū | و wāw | تَعْبُدُونَ (*taʿbudūna*, the و after the second ḍamma) |

In لَآ the *l* has a fatḥa and the alif stretches it: *lā*. In تَعْبُدُونَ the د has a ḍamma and the و stretches it: *ta-ʿbu-dū-na*.

<Note kind="tip">Say a short *a* and a long *ā* out loud, one after the other. The long one takes as long as two short ones. Hold it. Do not speed up at the end of a word.</Note>

</Block>

The letters و and ي do two jobs. Look at the letter **before** and at the letter **itself**:

- A و or ي with its **own vowel mark** (وَ, يَ) is an ordinary letter: *w* or *y*.
- A و or ي with **no mark** after a **matching** short vowel stretches it: ḍamma + و gives *ū*, kasra + ي gives *ī*.

In وَتَبَّ (*wa-tabba*) the و has a fatḥa on it. It is the sound *w*. Nothing is being stretched.

An alif never carries a vowel of its own, so an alif after a fatḥa always stretches it.

</Block>

In the Quran, many long ā sounds are **not** written with a full alif. A **small alif** (ٰ) sits above the letter instead. It is a long ā.

- ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ: the small alif above the ك makes *kā*. You say *al-kā-fi-rū-na*.
- يَٰٓأَيُّهَا: the small alif above the ي makes *yā*.

| Word | Where | How you say it |
|---|---|---|
| ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ | Al-Fatiha 1:3 | *ar-raḥ-mā-ni* |
| ذَٰلِكَ | Al-Baqarah 2:2 | *dhā-li-ka* |
| أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ | Al-Kawthar 108:1 | *aʿṭay-nā-ka* |

A word that ends in ى can also have a small alif, as in أَغْنَىٰ (Al-Masad 111:2). It is *aghnā*: the fatḥa before it and the small alif make the long ā.

<Note kind="warn">A final ى is **not always** the same sound. After a fatḥa with a small alif above it (أَغْنَىٰ), it is ā. After a kasra with nothing above it (أَبِى in Al-Masad 111:1), it is ī: *abī*. This text writes the final ī of many words as ى.</Note>

Some alifs carry a small wavy sign (ٓ). You see it in يَدَآ (111:1), لَآ (109:2), إِنَّآ (108:1) and جَآءَ (110:1). The sign tells you to **hold the ā longer**. In the first stage of reading, read it as a clear long ā. How long to hold it is taught in recitation (tajwīd).

The sign is **not** a hamza. Look at جَآءَ: the hamza is a separate letter written after the alif (ء). In إِنَّآ and يَدَآ there is no hamza at all. The next word starts with one, but this word ends in ā.

The same idea works with **small letters**. A small **wāw** (ۥ) after a ḍamma stands for a long ū. A small **yā** (ۦ) after a kasra stands for a long ī.

In مَالُهُۥ (*mā-lu-hū*) the small wāw sits on the pronoun هُ, “his”. The word ends in a long ū, not in a short *u*.

The small yā is rarer. You see it in إِۦلَٰفِهِمْ (Quraysh 106:2, start), where the small ۦ stands for the long ī of *īlāf*. A few lines earlier, 106:1 writes the same word with a full ي: لِإِيلَٰفِ.

<Note kind="know">Reciters have rules for when the small wāw and small yā are held long and when they are not, for example when you stop at the end of a verse. For now, learn what the small letter means: a long vowel.</Note>

Sometimes a letter has **no vowel** after it, like the *k* in “ask” or the *b* in “cab”. The Quran marks this with a small circle (ْ) above the letter. It is called **sukun**, “stillness”.

Say the letter and go straight on. Do not add an *a*, *i* or *u*.

- أَلَمْ ends in a م with sukun: *alam*, not *alama*.
- In يَجْعَلْ the ج has sukun and so does the last ل: *yaj-ʿal*. The sukun on the ج closes the first part of the word. It does not make a new one.

<Note kind="tip">A word never **starts** with a sukun letter. If a word seems to start with one, the Quran text puts a silent ٱ in front (ٱنْحَرْ in 108:2). You will see why in L1.03.</Note>

</Block>

Sukun also helps make two **glides**. A glide is a vowel that moves from one sound to another. It is not a long vowel.

| Marks | Sound | Word |
|---|---|---|
| fatḥa + يْ (yā with sukun) | *ay*, like “eye” said quickly | أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ (*aʿṭaynāka*), كَيْدَهُمْ (*kaydahum*, 105:2) |
| fatḥa + وْ (wāw with sukun) | *aw*, like “ow” in “cow” | ٱلْكَوْثَرَ (*al-kawthara*) |

Compare the long vowels. A long **ī** is a kasra and a ي with **no** sukun. An **ay** is a fatḥa and a ي **with** sukun. A long vowel makes one sound longer. A glide moves between two sounds.

In ٱلْكَوْثَرَ, the ك has a fatḥa and the و has sukun: *kaw*, not *kū*. A long *ū* would need a ḍamma before the و.

Look at the end of فَلْيَعْبُدُوا۟. The last letter is an alif with a small round sign (۟) above it. This is **not** a sukun. It means: **this letter is written but not said**.

The verb ends in the long ū of the و. The alif is only there in the spelling. You say *fal-yaʿ-bu-dū* and stop.

So the Quran has two circles:

- **ْ** (sukun, on a letter you say): say the letter, no vowel.
- **۟** (on a letter you do not say): skip the letter.

<Note kind="know">A similar sign (۠) is on the alif of أَنَا (Al-Kafirun 109:4). It also marks an alif that is not said when you read on.</Note>

A small shape like a tiny *w* above a letter (ّ) is **shadda**, “strengthening”. The letter counts **twice**: once with no vowel, once with its vowel. This is like the double *t* in “butter”.

A letter with shadda always has a vowel too. The shadda doubles the letter. The vowel tells you the sound after it.

| Marks | Sound | Word |
|---|---|---|
| shadda + fatḥa | *-bba* | وَتَبَّ (*wa-tab-ba*, 111:1) |
| shadda + kasra | *-bbi* | لِرَبِّكَ (*li-rab-bi-ka*, 108:2) |
| shadda + ḍamma | *-bbu* | رَبُّكَ (*rab-bu-ka*, Al-Fil 105:1) |

Notice فَصَلِّ: the ل has shadda and a kasra, so it is *ṣal-li*, two *l* sounds. When the vowel is a kasra, the kasra is written below the letter and the shadda above it.

<Note kind="know">A shadda can change a word. عَلِمَ (*ʿalima*) means “he knew”. عَلَّمَ (*ʿallama*, Ar-Rahman 55:2) means “he taught”. The only difference is the doubled ل.</Note>

</Block>

Now you can read the two verbs of Al-Masad 111:1.

| Word | Letter by letter | You say |
|---|---|---|
| تَبَّتْ | ت with fatḥa, ب with shadda and fatḥa, ت with sukun | *ta-b-ba-t*, which is *tab-bat* |
| وَتَبَّ | و with fatḥa, ت with fatḥa, ب with shadda and fatḥa | *wa-ta-b-ba*, which is *wa-tab-ba* |

Both words have a doubled ب. The first ends with a sukun on ت, so the last sound is *t*. The second ends with a vowel, so the last sound is *a*.

Shadda and sukun do opposite jobs. Shadda adds a sound. Sukun takes a vowel away.

**Tanwīn** (تَنْوِين) adds an **-n** sound to the end of a word. The name comes from the letter *nūn*. It is written by **doubling the vowel mark**. There is no letter ن on the page.

| Name | Mark | Sound | Word |
|---|---|---|---|
| tanwīn ḍamma | ـٌ | *-un* | حَبْلٌ (*ḥablun*, Al-Masad 111:5) |
| tanwīn fatḥa | ـً | *-an* | نَارًا (*nāran*, 111:3) |
| tanwīn kasra | ـٍ | *-in* | لَهَبٍ (*lahabin*, 111:3) |

Some books call them *ḍammatān*, *fatḥatān* and *kasratān*, “two ḍammas”, “two fatḥas” and “two kasras”.

Tanwīn comes only at the **end** of a word. It is a double sign. A normal short vowel is a single sign. Tell them apart: ـُ is *-u*, but ـٌ is *-un*.

<Block id="tanween-alif" title="The alif after -an">
  <Stage label="Three words ending in -an">
    <Tiles
      label="نَارًا, غَرْقًا and طَيْرًا"
      items={[
        { ar: 'نَارًا', en: 'nāran: a fire (Al-Masad 111:3)' },
        { ar: 'غَرْقًا', en: 'gharqan: hard pulling (An-Naziʿat 79:1)' },
        { ar: 'طَيْرًا', en: 'ṭayran: birds (Al-Fil 105:3)' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Tanwīn fatḥa (-an) is usually written with an **alif** after the letter. The double fatḥa sits on the letter **before** the alif.

The alif here only holds a place for the sign. You do **not** stretch it. Say *nāran*, with the long ā from the first alif and a short *-an* at the end, not *nārā*.

Tanwīn ḍamma and tanwīn kasra need no extra letter.

<Note kind="know">A word that ends in ة (tāʾ marbūṭa) writes its -an on the ة itself, with no alif. L2.05 shows this.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="tanween-al" title="Tanwīn and ٱل do not mix">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      reference="Al-Masad 111:4"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'وَٱمْرَأَتُهُۥ', en: 'and his wife', say: 'wamraʾatuhū' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'حَمَّالَةَ', en: 'carrier of', say: 'ḥammālata' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْحَطَبِ', en: 'the firewood', say: 'al-ḥaṭabi' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And his wife, the carrier of the firewood.']}
    />
  </Stage>

Put the ending of لَهَبٍ next to the ending of ٱلْحَطَبِ. Both end in a kasra sound, but only the first has tanwīn:

- لَهَبٍ: **-in**, tanwīn. A word with tanwīn is indefinite: “a flame”.
- ٱلْحَطَبِ: **-i**, one kasra. The word has ٱل (“the”), so it never takes tanwīn.

So tanwīn tells you the word has no ٱل. Learn this now: you will use it all through Level 2 (L2.04 to L2.06).

This verse also shows two marks from earlier. حَمَّالَةَ has a shadda on the م and a long ā: *ḥam-mā-la-ta*. وَٱمْرَأَتُهُۥ ends in a small wāw: *-hū*.

<Note kind="know">A few names take tanwīn too, and they are still definite. Quraysh in 106:1 is one: لِإِيلَٰفِ قُرَيْشٍ. So “tanwīn means indefinite” is a good first rule for ordinary nouns, but not a law.</Note>

<Block id="small-meem" title="The small mīm on top of a tanwīn">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      reference="Al-Imran 3:163 (end)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'وَٱللَّهُ', en: 'and Allah', say: 'wallāhu' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'بَصِيرٌۢ', en: 'sees', say: 'baṣīrum' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'بِمَا', en: 'what', say: 'bi-mā', type: 'p' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'يَعْمَلُونَ', en: 'they do', say: 'yaʿmalūna', type: 'v' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And Allah sees what they do.']}
    />
  </Stage>

Look at بَصِيرٌۢ. It has a ḍamma, but the second ḍamma is replaced by a **small mīm** (ۢ).

The sign tells you how to say the tanwīn here. When a tanwīn is followed by the letter **ب**, the -n sound changes to an **-m** sound. So you say *baṣīrum bi-mā*, not *baṣīrun bi-mā*.

The Quran text writes this for you. You see the small mīm on top of the tanwīn (ۢ) or below it (ۭ), and you say -m.

<Note kind="know">Tanwīn changes before some other letters too. These rules belong to recitation (tajwīd). For now, learn the one change you can see on the page: the small mīm means -m.</Note>

<Block id="tanween-case" title="Tanwīn is also a case ending">
  <Stage label="Three tanwīns, three cases">
    <Tiles
      label="حَبْلٌ, نَارًا and لَهَبٍ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'حَبْلٌ', en: 'ḥablun: a rope, nominative (-un)' },
        { ar: 'نَارًا', en: 'nāran: a fire, accusative (-an)' },
        { ar: 'لَهَبٍ', en: 'lahabin: flames, genitive (-in)' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Arabic nouns change their endings to show their job in the sentence (L1.10 introduces this). Tanwīn is where you see it most. Each of the three signs goes with one case:

- **-un** (tanwīn ḍamma): nominative, رَفْع. حَبْلٌ is the mubtadaʾ (L2.01) in فِى جِيدِهَا حَبْلٌ (111:5), “on her neck is a rope”.
- **-an** (tanwīn fatḥa): accusative, نَصْب. نَارًا is the fire he will enter (111:3).
- **-in** (tanwīn kasra): genitive, جَرّ. لَهَبٍ comes after ذَاتَ, “having” (111:3).

Why each word has its case is the work of Level 2. L2.04 teaches the nominative, L2.05 the accusative and L2.06 the genitive. Here you only need to read each sign correctly.

<Check n={13} q="Which sound goes with the nominative tanwīn?" options={['-un', '-an', '-in']} answer={0} why="Nominative is the -u family, and with tanwīn it is -un." />

<Note kind="remember">Tanwīn is a doubled vowel sign at the end of a word. It says the sound -n. It also shows the case: -un, -an or -in.</Note>
</Block>

<Step id="mistakes-verses" label="Whole verses" title="Common mistakes, then four verses">
<Block id="mistakes-1" title="Mistakes with long vowels and small signs">
  <Stage label="Right and wrong">
    <Tiles
      label="ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ said right and wrong"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ', en: 'ar-raḥ-mā-ni', type: 'n' },
        { ghost: 'ar-raḥ-ma-ni', note: 'wrong: the small alif is skipped' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- **Reading the small alif as nothing.** ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ has a small alif above the م. Say *ar-raḥ-mā-ni*, not *ar-raḥ-ma-ni*. The small alif is easy to miss because it is so small.

- **Treating wāw and yā as always long.** A و or ي with its own vowel is a letter (وَتَبَّ). Check the mark on the letter before and on the letter itself.

- **Making long vowels too short.** The length changes the meaning. قَتَلَ (*qatala*) is “he killed” and قَاتَلَ (*qātala*) is “he fought”. The only difference is a long ā. These two words are not from a verse. They only show the point.

- **Reading every final ى as ā.** After a fatḥa with a small alif above it, ى is ā (أَغْنَىٰ). After a kasra with nothing above it, it is ī (أَبِى).

- **Reading the wavy sign as a hamza.** In إِنَّآ and يَدَآ the sign only makes the ā longer. A hamza would be a separate letter, as in جَآءَ.
</Block>

<Block id="mistakes-2" title="Mistakes with sukun, shadda and tanwīn">
  <Stage label="Right and wrong">
    <Tiles
      label="تَبَّتْ said right and wrong"
      items={[
        { ar: 'تَبَّتْ', en: 'tab-bat', type: 'v' },
        { ghost: 'tabat', note: 'wrong: one b' },
        { ghost: 'tabbata', note: 'wrong: a vowel after the sukun' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- **Saying a shadda letter once.** Say *tab-bat*, not *tabat*. Think of “latter” and “later”.

- **Adding a vowel where there is a sukun.** The small circle means no vowel. Say *tab-bat*, not *tab-bata*, and *alam*, not *alama*.

- **Confusing the two circles.** Sukun (ْ) is on a letter you say. The small round sign (۟) is on a letter you do not say, like the last alif of فَلْيَعْبُدُوا۟.

- **Mixing up tanwīn and a plain vowel.** Tanwīn is a **double** sign (ـٌ ـً ـٍ) and adds -n. A plain vowel is a single sign (ـُ ـَ ـِ). Tanwīn comes only at the end of a word.

- **Putting tanwīn on a word with ٱل.** The two do not go together. ٱلْحَطَبِ (“the firewood”) ends in one kasra. Never say it with -in.
</Block>

<Exercise n={1} q="In إِنَّآ (Al-Kawthar 108:1), what makes the long ā at the end?">

The **alif with the wavy sign** (آ). There is no hamza in this word. The wavy sign only asks you to hold the ā.

- إِ: a hamza at the start, with a kasra: *i*
- نَّ: ن with shadda and fatḥa: *nna*
- آ: the long ā

Say *in-nā*, with the ā held.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="What is the difference between بَيْ and بِي? These are made-up examples, not from a verse.">

- **بَيْ** has a fatḥa on the ب and a ي with sukun. It is the glide *ay*: one sound moving to another.
- **بِي** has a kasra on the ب and a ي with no mark. It is the long vowel *ī*: one sound held.

Look at the vowel on the ب and at the mark on the ي. Fatḥa + ي with sukun is a glide. Kasra + ي with no mark is a long ī.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Name every mark in تَبَّتْ (Al-Masad 111:1) and say what each does.">

Three marks:

1. **Fatḥa** on the first ت: *ta*.
2. **Shadda + fatḥa** on the ب: the ب counts twice, then *a*. That is *bba*.
3. **Sukun** on the last ت: no vowel, just *t*.

Together: *tab-bat*. The shadda adds a sound and the sukun takes a vowel away.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Read فَصَلِّ لِرَبِّكَ وَٱنْحَرْ (Al-Kawthar 108:2) and name every mark.">

*fa-ṣal-li li-rab-bi-ka wa-nḥar*

- فَ, صَ: fatḥa: *fa*, *ṣa*
- لِّ: shadda + kasra: *lli* (so *ṣal-li*)
- لِ: kasra: *li*
- رَ: fatḥa: *ra*
- بِّ: shadda + kasra: *bbi*
- كَ: fatḥa: *ka*
- وَ: fatḥa. The و is the letter *w*, not a long vowel.
- ٱ: silent after وَ.
- نْ: sukun: *n* with no vowel.
- حَ: fatḥa: *ḥa*
- رْ: sukun: *r* with no vowel.

Marks found: fatḥa, kasra, shadda (twice) and sukun (twice). No long vowels and no tanwīn.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Name the tanwīn in لَهَبٍ (Al-Masad 111:1) and say what it tells you.">

It is **tanwīn kasra** (ـٍ): two kasras that make *-in*. Say *la-ha-bin*.

It tells you two things: the word has **no ٱل**, and it is genitive, as it comes after أَبِى (“father of”). A word with ٱل, like ٱلْحَطَبِ (111:4), takes a single kasra and no tanwīn.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Name every mark in ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ (Al-Fatiha 1:3).">

*ar-raḥ-mā-ni*

- ٱ: the opening alif. Its sound is the *a* you hear when the word starts a phrase.
- ل: no mark, because the ل is not said on its own here. Its sound joins the ر.
- رَّ: shadda + fatḥa. The ر is doubled: *rra*.
- حْ: sukun: *ḥ* with no vowel.
- مَٰ: fatḥa + **small alif**: a long ā, *mā*.
- نِ: kasra: *ni*.

Marks found: shadda, sukun, small alif, and short vowels. L1.06 explains why the ل disappears into the ر.

</Exercise>

*mā-lu-hū*

- مَا: fatḥa + alif: long ā.
- لُ: ḍamma: *lu*.
- هُۥ: ḍamma + **small wāw**: long ū. The small wāw makes the pronoun “his” end in *-hū*.

<Exercise n={9} q="How do you say فَلْيَعْبُدُوا۟ (Quraysh 106:3), and what does the last sign mean?">

*fal-yaʿ-bu-dū*

- ل and ع have sukun: no vowel after them.
- دُو: ḍamma + و with no mark: long ū.
- ا۟: an alif with a small round sign. It is written but **not said**.

The word ends on the long ū.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={10} q="How do you say بَصِيرٌۢ بِمَا (Al-Imran 3:163), and why is it not baṣīrun?">

*ba-ṣī-rum bi-mā*

The small mīm (ۢ) replaces the second ḍamma. It tells you that the tanwīn is said **-m** before the ب of بِمَا. The ī in بَصِيرٌ is a long vowel: kasra + ي.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={11} q="Name the tanwīn in each word and say its sound: (a) نَارًا (Al-Masad 111:3) (b) لَهَبٍ (Al-Masad 111:3) (c) حَبْلٌ (Al-Masad 111:5).">

- (a) نَارًا: **tanwīn fatḥa**, *-an*. Written with an alif as a seat. *nāran*.
- (b) لَهَبٍ: **tanwīn kasra**, *-in*. *lahabin*.
- (c) حَبْلٌ: **tanwīn ḍamma**, *-un*. The ب has a sukun, so the ل holds the tanwīn: *ḥab-lun*.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={12} q="Say جَآءَ نَصْرُ (An-Nasr 110:1) part by part and name the marks.">

*jā-a naṣ-ru*

- جَآ: fatḥa + alif with the wavy sign: a long ā, held.
- ءَ: a hamza with fatḥa, written as a separate letter: *a*.
- نَ: fatḥa: *na*.
- صْ: sukun: *ṣ* with no vowel.
- رُ: ḍamma: *ru*.

</Exercise>

- A **long vowel** is a short vowel held for two beats: fatḥa + ا, kasra + ي, ḍamma + و. A **small alif** (ٰ), a **small wāw** (ۥ) and a **small yā** (ۦ) can stand for a long vowel. The wavy sign (ٓ) asks for a longer hold.
- **Glides:** fatḥa + يْ is *ay*. Fatḥa + وْ is *aw*.
- **Sukun** (ْ): the letter has no vowel. A small round sign (۟) means the letter is not said at all.
- **Shadda** (ّ): the letter counts twice. It always has a vowel too.
- **Tanwīn** (ـٌ ـً ـٍ): -un, -an, -in. It comes only at the end of a word without ٱل. A small mīm (ۢ) means -m before ب.
- Tanwīn also shows the case: L2.04, L2.05 and L2.06 teach them.
