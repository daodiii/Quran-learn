Read it from right to left. The speaker is Allah. The first word means “he wrote”, and here it means “he decreed”.

Two of these words carry an idea you will follow through the whole lesson: كَتَبَ (writing) and ٱلرَّحْمَةَ (mercy). Each belongs to a **family** of words that share the same core letters.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal says كَتَبَ here also carries the sense of “made binding”. So Allah has made mercy a duty on Himself.</Note>

Look at the letters in each word. In every one you can find **ك**, then **ت**, then **ب**, in that order. The vowels change. Other letters come and go. The three letters stay.

Those three letters are the **root**, جَذْر (*jadhr*). Here the root is **ك ت ب**, and its core idea is *writing*.

| Word | Meaning | Kind | Seen in | Root (Corpus) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| كَتَبَ | he wrote, he decreed | verb | Al-Anʿam 6:12 | ك ت ب |
| كِتَٰبٌ | a book | noun | Al-Mutaffifin 83:9 | ك ت ب |
| كَاتِبٌ | a writer | noun | Al-Baqarah 2:282 | ك ت ب |
| مَكْتُوبًا | written | noun | Al-Aʿraf 7:157 | ك ت ب |

A verb and nouns can come from one root. In L1.04 you sorted words into noun, verb and little word. Roots cut across that sorting: the verb and the nouns above are one family.

The **root** gives the idea: writing. The **pattern**, وَزْن (*wazn*), gives the shape of the word: its vowels and any extra letters. The shape tells you the job of the word.

The grammar books write a pattern with three stand-in letters, **ف ع ل**. They stand for the first, second and third letter of the root.

- ف is the first root letter: ك.
- ع is the second root letter: ت.
- ل is the third root letter: ب.

Pour ك ت ب into فَاعِل and you get كَاتِب: ك, a long ā, ت, a kasra, ب. Al-Jadwal gives this pattern for كَاتِب in Al-Baqarah 2:282.

<Note kind="know">Do not try to learn many patterns now. L3.03 teaches how to find a root and name a pattern. Here you only look.</Note>

</Block>

      items={[

Pour the same root into different patterns and you get different jobs. Each pattern below is the one al-Jadwal names in the ṣarf notes for that verse.

| Word | Pattern | What al-Jadwal says | Seen in |
|---|---|---|---|
| كَاتِبٌ | فَاعِل | a doer word (اِسْم فَاعِل): the one who writes | Al-Baqarah 2:282 |
| كِتَٰبًا | فِعَال | an action name (مَصْدَر): the act of writing, or of counting | An-Naba 78:29 |
| مَكْتُوبًا | مَفْعُول | a done-to word (اِسْم مَفْعُول): what was written | Al-Aʿraf 7:157 |

So from one idea you can say *the one who writes*, *the writing* and *the thing written*.

<Note kind="warn">كِتَٰبًا in 78:29 is “writing”, an action name. It is not the same as “a book”. For ٱلْكِتَٰب in Al-Baqarah 2:2, al-Jadwal says only that it is a fixed noun taken from the action name. It gives no pattern there. Never name a pattern unless a source gives it.</Note>

Look at the second word of each verse. They are four different roots, but one pattern.

| Word | Root (Corpus) | Pattern (al-Jadwal) | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ٱلْمَعْمُورِ | ع م ر | مَفْعُول | visited, lived in |
| ٱلْمَرْفُوعِ | ر ف ع | مَفْعُول | raised |
| ٱلْمَسْجُورِ | س ج ر | مَفْعُول | filled |
| مَّرْقُومٌ | ر ق م | مَفْعُول | marked |

Al-Jadwal calls each one a done-to word on the pattern مَفْعُول. Learn this one shape and you can read *what has been* ___*ed* for any root. The م at the front and the و in the middle belong to the pattern, not the root.

You say these two words in every Bismillah (1:1, L1.03). They come from the same root, **ر ح م**, the idea of mercy. The Corpus gives both this root.

- ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ is on the pattern **فَعْلَان**.
- ٱلرَّحِيمُ is on the pattern **فَعِيل**.

On 1:1, al-Jadwal calls ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ an intensive form (a word of “very much”). It calls ٱلرَّحِيمِ an intensive form or a quality word. In its notes on style it says ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ is the stronger of the two.

The pattern فَعِيل is common. Al-Jadwal also names it for ٱلْحَكِيمُ (root ح ك م) in Al-Baqarah 2:32.

<Step id="limits" label="Limits and numbers" title="What to watch for, and how big the system is">
<Block id="mistake-little" title="Mistake 1: looking for a root in every word">
  <Stage label="Words with no root">
    <Tiles
      label="هُوَ has no root, but ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ share ر ح م"
      items={[
        { ar: 'هُوَ', en: 'he: no root', type: 'n' },
        { ar: 'ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ', en: 'Most Merciful', type: 'n' },
        { ar: 'ٱلرَّحِيمُ', en: 'Ever Merciful', type: 'n' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Not every word has a root. This is the end of Al-Baqarah 2:163: هُوَ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ. The pronoun هُوَ has no root. Little words like عَلَىٰ and بِ have none either.

In the Corpus file, none of the 7,679 words whose main part is a preposition has a root. None of the 3,301 words whose main part is a pronoun has one. In all, 27,462 of the Quran's 77,429 words (35%) carry no root tag.

<Check n={9} q="Which word has no root?" options={['هُوَ', 'كَاتِبٌ', 'ٱلرَّحِيمُ']} answer={0} why="A pronoun has no root. كَاتِبٌ has ك ت ب, and ٱلرَّحِيمُ has ر ح م." />
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-whole" title="Mistake 2: thinking the root is the whole meaning">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="the root ك ت ب gives كَاتِب the writer and مَكْتُوب the written"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ك ت ب', en: 'one root', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'كَاتِب', en: 'writer: the one who does', type: 'n' },
        { ar: 'مَكْتُوب', en: 'written: what is done to', type: 'n' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The root gives the idea. The pattern gives the job. A writer and a thing written come from the same root, yet they are opposite: one does, one receives.

So a root helps you guess, but it does not replace the word. Always check the pattern, and then the verse.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-letters" title="Mistake 3: counting pattern letters as root letters">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="in ٱلْمَرْفُوعِ the root is ر ف ع; ٱل and م and و are not root letters"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ر', en: 'first root letter', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ف', en: 'second root letter', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ع', en: 'third root letter', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Take ٱلْمَرْفُوعِ (52:5). The letters ٱل are the article (L1.06). The م at the front and the و in the middle are part of the pattern مَفْعُول. The root is only **ر ف ع**.

<Note kind="tip">Strip the article, the prefixes like وَ and بِ, and any pronoun ending first. Then look at what is left for the three letters.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-hide" title="Mistake 4: expecting all three letters to show">
  <Stage label="A letter that hides">
    <Tiles
      label="the noun ٱسْم shows only two root letters; the plural ٱلْأَسْمَآء brings the third back"
      items={[
        { ar: 'س م و', en: 'root', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ٱسْم', en: 'a name', type: 'n' },
        { ar: 'ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ', en: 'the names', type: 'n' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The noun ٱسْم (name) shows only **س** and **م**. Its root is **س م و**. Al-Jadwal says the last root letter was dropped, and a helping alif took its place at the front. It shows the third letter by the plural أَسْمَآء (Al-Baqarah 2:31).

Some roots lose or change a letter. L3.03 and L4.11 to L4.15 teach this. For now, remember that the Corpus lists the full root even when the word shows less.

<Note kind="know">The Corpus gives the same root س م و to ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ (the heavens, Al-Anʿam 6:12). This course does not explain the link in meaning.</Note>

<Check n={10} q="What is the root of ٱسْم, according to the Corpus and al-Jadwal?" options={['س م و', 'ا س م', 'س م']} answer={0} why="The root is س م و. The third letter is dropped in the singular and returns in the plural." />
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-one" title="Mistake 5: trusting that similar words share a root">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="أَحَدٌ has the root أ ح د and وَٰحِدٌ has the root و ح د"
      items={[
        { ar: 'وَٰحِدٌ', en: 'one: root و ح د', type: 'n' },
        { ar: 'أَحَدٌ', en: 'one: root أ ح د', type: 'n' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Both words mean “one”. They look alike. But the Corpus gives أَحَدٌ (Al-Ikhlas 112:1) the root **أ ح د** and وَٰحِدٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:163) the root **و ح د**. In the Corpus they are two roots.

<Note kind="know">Scholars differ on this pair. *Al-Durr al-Maṣūn* records the common view that the hamza of أَحَد comes from a wāw, which would give it the root و ح د too, and also the view that the hamza is original. This course follows the Corpus tag here. L1.11 comes back to it.</Note>

<Note kind="warn">Never decide a root by feel. Check the Corpus tag, or ask a teacher.</Note>
</Block>

We counted the Quranic Arabic Corpus file ourselves.

|---|---|

So the usual root has three letters. And roughly half of all words in the Quran come from just the 200 most common roots. That is why roots are the best tool for vocabulary.

<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: find the root">
  <Stage label="To find the root">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Strip</b> ٱل, وَ, بِ and any pronoun ending.</li>
      <li><b>Strip</b> the pattern letters and vowels.</li>

Work out each answer first. Then open it.

<Exercise n={1} q="These three words share a root: ٱلْحَمْدُ (Al-Fatiha 1:2), حَمِيدٌ (Hud 11:73) and مَّحْمُودًا (Al-Isra 17:79). What are the three root letters?">

The root is **ح م د**. The Corpus gives all three this root.

- ٱلْحَمْدُ: strip ٱل. The letters left are ح م د.
- حَمِيدٌ: ح م د with a long ī between م and د.
- مَّحْمُودًا: strip the م at the front and the و in the middle (pattern letters). ح م د is left. Al-Jadwal calls this word a done-to word on the pattern مَفْعُول.

The idea is *praise*. The three words mean praise, praiseworthy, and praised.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="The root ع ل م means “knowing”. In ٱلْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَّعْلُومَٰتٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:197) the second part is مَّعْلُومَٰتٌ. It is on the pattern مَفْعُول. What does it mean?">

The pattern مَفْعُول means “what has been done to”. With ع ل م it gives **known**. So مَّعْلُومَٰتٌ is “known” (plural, feminine): *the Hajj is in known months*.

Al-Jadwal confirms: a done-to word from the verb ʿalima, on the pattern مَفْعُول.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Give the root of each word in قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ (Al-Ikhlas 112:1), or say it has none.">

- قُلْ (say): root **ق و ل**. The middle letter و does not show in the word.
- هُوَ (he): a pronoun, **no root**.
- ٱللَّهُ: root **أ ل ه** in the Corpus.
- أَحَدٌ (one): root **أ ح د** in the Corpus. (Many scholars give it و ح د; see the note in Mistake 5.)

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Three of these four words share a root. Which one does not? ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ and ٱلرَّحِيمِ (Al-Fatiha 1:1), ٱلْأَرْحَامَ (An-Nisa 4:1), and ٱلْعَلِيمُ (Al-Baqarah 2:32).">

ٱلْعَلِيمُ does not. Its root is **ع ل م**. The other three have **ر ح م** in the Corpus.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Name the root and the pattern of ٱلْمَرْفُوعِ in وَٱلسَّقْفِ ٱلْمَرْفُوعِ (At-Tur 52:5), and say what it means.">

Root: **ر ف ع** (raising). Pattern: **مَفْعُول**, a done-to word, as al-Jadwal says. Meaning: “the raised”. The ٱل is the article. So the phrase is “the roof, the raised one”.

</Exercise>
</Block>

      <li><b>Doer</b> (فَاعِل): كَاتِبٌ</li>
      <li><b>Action</b> (فِعَال): كِتَٰبًا</li>
      <li><b>Done-to</b> (مَفْعُول): مَكْتُوبًا</li>

<Exercise n={6} q="Which of these words in كَتَبَ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ ٱلرَّحْمَةَ (Al-Anʿam 6:12) has a root, and which has none? كَتَبَ, عَلَىٰ, نَفْسِ, ٱلرَّحْمَةَ.">

- كَتَبَ: root **ك ت ب**.
- عَلَىٰ: a preposition, **no root**.
- نَفْسِ: root **ن ف س**.
- ٱلرَّحْمَةَ: root **ر ح م**.

The pronoun هِ on the end of نَفْسِهِ also has none.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="ٱسْم (name) shows only two of its root letters. Which letters show, which one is missing, and where does the missing one come back?">

The letters **س** and **م** show. The root is **س م و**, so **و** is missing. Al-Jadwal says it was dropped from the end, and a helping alif was added at the front. The و comes back in the plural أَسْمَآء (Al-Baqarah 2:31), which is the proof al-Jadwal gives.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Write the pattern of كَاتِبٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:282) with the stand-in letters, then pour the root ع ل م into the same pattern. What word do you get, and what would it mean?">

The pattern is **فَاعِل**. Pour ع ل م into it: ع takes the place of ف, ل takes ع, and م takes ل. You get **عَالِم**, “one who knows”.

This word is in the Quran: عَٰلِمُ ٱلْغَيْبِ in Ar-Raʿd 13:9, with the root ع ل م in the Corpus. Its shape matches فَاعِل. Al-Jadwal does not name its pattern in that verse, so this is our reading of the shape, not a quotation.

</Exercise>

  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="root plus pattern equals a word"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'root', small: 'the idea: ك ت ب' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'pattern', small: 'the shape: فَاعِل' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'word', small: 'كَاتِب, a writer' },

- Many Arabic words are built from a **root** (usually three letters) and a **pattern**.
- One root gives a family: ك ت ب gives كَتَبَ, كَاتِبٌ, كِتَٰبًا and مَكْتُوبًا.
- One pattern works on many roots: مَفْعُول gives مَكْتُوب, مَرْفُوع, مَعْمُور and مَسْجُور.
- Little words and pronouns have no root.
- A root can hide a letter (ٱسْم), and two words that look alike can have different roots in the Corpus (أَحَد and وَاحِد, where scholars differ).
- The root gives the idea. The pattern gives the job.
