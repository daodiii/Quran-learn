This is the start of a verse about Allah. Read it from right to left. Look only at the **end** of each word.

- ٱللَّهُ ends in a ḍamma, **-u**.
- لَطِيفٌ ends in two ḍammas, **-un**. A small ۢ is written after it. It is a reading sign and does not change the ending.
- بِعِبَادِهِ has a kasra, **-i**, before the pronoun “his”.

These endings are not random. Each one shows the **job** of its word in the sentence. This lesson is a first look at how.

In English, **word order** tells you who does what. “The boy hit the ball” and “The ball hit the boy” use the same words. Only the order is different, and so is the meaning.

Arabic can use **endings** for the same job. In both lines above, ٱلطَّالِبُ ends in **-u**, so it is the doer, the **fāʿil**, فَاعِل. ٱلْكِتَابَ ends in **-a**, so it is what the action falls on, the **mafʿūl bihi**, مَفْعُول بِهِ. You can move the words and the meaning stays.

<Note kind="know">English keeps a little of this in its pronouns: *he* and *him*, *I* and *me*, *they* and *them*. The ending changes with the job. Arabic does it for nearly all nouns.</Note>

  <Stage label="The three cases">
    <Tiles

      items={[
        { ar: 'رَفْع', en: 'nominative · -u' },

        { ar: 'جَرّ', en: 'genitive · -i' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Each ending is called a **case**. Arabic nouns have three, and the whole system is called **iʿrāb**, إِعْرَاب. Think of each case as a job badge:

- **Nominative**, رَفْع (*rafʿ*): “I am the doer, the starting point, or the news.” Its mark is **-u**.
- **Accusative**, نَصْب (*naṣb*): “I am what the action falls on.” Its mark is **-a**.
- **Genitive**, جَرّ (*jarr*): “I am pulled in by a little word in front of me, or by a word I belong to.” Its mark is **-i**.

The case belongs to the **job**, not to the word. The same word can wear any of the three badges.

<Note kind="remember">A noun’s ending tells you its job. The job decides the ending.</Note>
</Block>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* is a short grammar book by Ibn Ājurrūm. It says:

- “Iʿrāb is the changing of the **ends** of words, because of the different things that come in front of them, seen or understood.” The words “or understood” are about marks you cannot see. Step 3 shows some.
- Nouns get **rafʿ, naṣb and khafḍ**. They get no jazm.
- Verbs get **rafʿ, naṣb and jazm**. They get no khafḍ.

So case endings belong to **nouns**. Verbs have endings of their own. You will meet them in Level 3.

<Note kind="know">The book calls the third case *khafḍ*, خَفْض. Al-Jadwal, the i’rab book this course follows, calls it *jarr*. It is the same case. This course says jarr.</Note>

These are the vowels from L1.01 and the tanween from L1.02. A case ending is one of these marks doing a job.

| Case | Name | With al- (definite) | Without al- (indefinite) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | رَفْع (*rafʿ*) | -u (ḍamma) | -un (two ḍammas) |
| Accusative | نَصْب (*naṣb*) | -a (fatḥa) | -an (two fatḥas) |
| Genitive | جَرّ (*jarr*) | -i (kasra) | -in (two kasras) |

A word with *al-* takes one mark. A word without *al-* takes two, the tanween. This is the rule for the most common nouns. Step 3 shows words that do not follow it.

<Note kind="tip">Only the last vowel of the noun counts. The vowels inside a word belong to the word, not to its case. The kasra is the only one written **under** the letter, so it is easy to spot.</Note>

The **nominative** is the case of three jobs. Its marks are **-u** and **-un**.

1. **The starting point** of a nominal sentence, the mubtadaʾ. Here it is ٱللَّهُ, with -u.
2. **The news** about it, the khabar. Here it is لَطِيفٌ, with -un, because it has no *al-*.
3. **The doer** of a verb, the fāʿil. You saw it in ٱلطَّالِبُ, “the student”, in the made-up sentence.

Both parts of a nominal sentence are nominative, as L2.01 teaches. [L2.04, The Nominative Case](/learn/level-2/04-nominative-case/), gives every job and every mark.

The phrase بِعِبَادِهِ is not nominative. It belongs to لَطِيفٌ and takes the genitive. You will see it two blocks on.

</Block>

The **accusative** is the case of the receiver. Its marks are **-a** and **-an**.

- Fear **what**? ٱللَّهَ, “Allah”. It ends in **-a**.
- Say **what**? قَوْلًا, “a word”. It has no *al-*, so it ends in **-an**.

These are objects, **mafʿūl bihi**. The action of the verb falls on them.

سَدِيدًا, “right”, describes قَوْلًا and copies its ending: **-an**. A word that describes a noun takes the noun’s case. L2.09 teaches this.

The accusative has other jobs too: the first part of a sentence after *inna* (“indeed”), the news after *kāna* (“was”), and words for a place or a time. [L2.05, The Receiver Case](/learn/level-2/05-accusative-case/), teaches them.

<Note kind="know">-an is usually written with a silent alif after it: قَوْلًا. The alif is not a separate sound.</Note>

The **genitive** is the case of a word that is “pulled in” to another. Its marks are **-i** and **-in**. There are two main reasons.

1. **A little word in front.** A preposition, such as بِ “with, in”, فِي “in”, مِنْ “from” or إِلَىٰ “to”, pulls the next noun into the genitive. In بِسْمِ, the noun ٱسْم, “name”, ends in **-i** because of بِ.
2. **A word it belongs to.** ٱللَّهِ means “of Allah”. It is the second word of a pair, “the name **of** Allah”. The grammar books call this pair an idafah, and the second word *muḍāf ilayh*. It is genitive, so it ends in **-i**.

The two words that follow, ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ and ٱلرَّحِيمِ, describe ٱللَّهِ. Describing words copy the case of the word they describe, so they end in -i too. That is why the whole phrase ends in one sound.

[L2.06, The Genitive Case](/learn/level-2/06-genitive-case/), teaches all of this. L2.07 teaches the prepositions and L2.08 the idafah.

The same verse ends with a second sentence. Its starting point is هُوَ, “he”.

Look at هُوَ. It has no -u, no -a and no -i. It has **one shape**, whatever its job. Pronouns like هُوَ do not change.

It still has a job. It is the mubtadaʾ, so the grammar books say it is “in the place of” the nominative (*fī maḥall rafʿ*). The words that follow, ٱلْقَوِىُّ and ٱلْعَزِيزُ, are the khabar and a second khabar. Both end in **-u**.

So “every noun takes one of three endings” is not the whole story. A word without a visible ending can still have a job. L3.08 and L3.09 teach the pronouns.

<Note kind="warn">No visible ending does not mean no job. Do not invent an ending that is not there. Ask what the word is doing in the sentence.</Note>

The plain pattern of -u, -a and -i is the one to learn first. Some words show the same three cases in other ways. Level 2 teaches each one.

| Kind of word | Nominative | Accusative | Genitive | Taught in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A single noun | -u, -un | -a, -an | -i, -in | this lesson |
| Two of something (dual) | -āni | -ayni | -ayni | L2.04 to L2.06 |
| A plural of men, with an ending (sound plural) | -ūna | -īna | -īna | L2.04 to L2.06 |
| A word that ends in a long ā | hidden | hidden | hidden | L2.01, L2.04 |
| A name like فِرْعَوْنَ (a diptote) | -u | -a | -a | L2.06 |
| A pronoun like هُوَ | no ending | no ending | no ending | L3.08, L3.09 |

A word that ends in a long ā, like مَوْلَىٰ, cannot carry a vowel mark on that ā. Its case is still there. The grammar books say it is “understood”, not seen. That is the “or understood” in *al-Ajurrumiyyah*.

<Note kind="remember">Seeing the case is easy for the plain pattern. For the other kinds, you learn the mark once, and then you know it.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-verb" title="Mistake 1: reading a verb’s -a as a case">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Alaq 96:2"

        { role: 'fiil', words: [{ ar: 'خَلَقَ', en: 'created', say: 'khalaqa', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'maful', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ', en: 'the human being', say: 'al-insana' }] },
        { role: 'tied', hint: 'belongs to خَلَقَ', words: [{ ar: 'مِنْ', en: 'from', type: 'p' }, { ar: 'عَلَقٍ', en: 'a clinging clot', say: 'alaqin' }] },
      ]}
      en={['He created the human being from a clinging clot.']}
    />
  </Stage>

خَلَقَ ends in **-a**, and so does ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ. Only the second one is a case.

خَلَقَ is a past-tense verb. The basic past-tense verb ends in -a, whatever the sentence. That -a is part of the verb. It says nothing about a job. A present-tense verb has its own endings too (-u, -a or a sukūn), and they follow their own rules in Level 3.

So the noun cases are for **nouns**. Here:

- ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ is accusative (**-a**): it is what was created.
- مِنْ is a little word. It has no case, and it pulls the next noun in.
- عَلَقٍ is genitive (**-in**): it comes right after مِنْ, and it has no *al-*.

<Note kind="know">A present-tense verb can end in -u, as يَرْزُقُ does in 42:19. The grammar books also call that *rafʿ*. It is a verb ending, not the noun case. L3.04 teaches it.</Note>

<Check n={9} q="In خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ, which word is accusative?" options={['خَلَقَ', 'ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ']} answer={1} why="ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ is a noun and the object. The -a on خَلَقَ is the fixed ending of a past-tense verb." />
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-pronoun" title="Mistake 2: looking for the case in the wrong place">
  <Stage label="One word, two parts">
    <Tiles
      label="عِبَادِ then هِ: the noun carries the case, then the pronoun"
      items={[
        { ar: 'عِبَادِ', en: 'servants of · the case is here' },
        { ar: 'هِ', en: 'his · a pronoun, one shape' },
      ]}

In بِعِبَادِهِ the last vowel you hear is the kasra on the ه. It is not the case ending.

The word is two parts: the noun عِبَاد, “servants”, and the pronoun هِ, “his”. The pronoun never changes. The **case sits on the noun, just before it**: عِبَادِ is genitive, with a kasra on the د, because of بِ.

When a pronoun is attached to the end of a noun, look at the vowel **before** the pronoun.

A rule of thumb says “the subject takes -u”. It works most of the time, and then it breaks.

Compare the two verses. In the first, ٱللَّهُ is the starting point and ends in **-u**. In the second, the little word إِنَّ (“indeed”) comes first. Now the same starting point, ٱللَّهَ, ends in **-a**: it is **accusative**. The news, لَطِيفٌ, stays **-un**.

So *inna* makes the first part accusative and leaves the news nominative. It does not change the news. L2.05 and L2.10 teach this.

The ending follows the **job and what comes before it**, not an English word like “subject”.

      <li><b>Case:</b> nominative, accusative or genitive?</li>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Name the case of each form of one word. This is a made-up example, not a verse. (1) ٱلْكِتَابُ (2) ٱلْكِتَابَ (3) ٱلْكِتَابِ. Hint: look at the last vowel.">

1. **ٱلْكِتَابُ**, *al-kitābu*: **nominative**, because it ends in -u. It could be the starting point, the news or the doer.
2. **ٱلْكِتَابَ**, *al-kitāba*: **accusative**, because it ends in -a. It could be the object.
3. **ٱلْكِتَابِ**, *al-kitābi*: **genitive**, because it ends in -i. It could follow a preposition, or be the second word of an idafah.

The word “the book” is the same each time. Only the job, and so the ending, changes.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Match each job to its case. Jobs: (1) the starting point of a nominal sentence (mubtadaʾ) (2) the object of a verb (3) the noun right after a preposition (4) the news (khabar). Cases: A. accusative, B. nominative, C. genitive.">

1. The starting point: **B, nominative** (-u).
2. The object of a verb: **A, accusative** (-a).
3. The noun after a preposition: **C, genitive** (-i).
4. The news: **B, nominative** (-u).

One exception to keep in mind: after إِنَّ the starting point goes to **accusative**, as you saw in Mistake 3. L2.05 and L2.10 teach it.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Give the case of each word, and the reason. بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ (Al-Fatiha 1:1, part). Meaning: “In the name of Allah, the All-Merciful.” Then say why all the endings are alike.">

All three nouns are **genitive**, with a kasra.

1. **ٱسْمِ** (in بِسْمِ): genitive, because it comes right after the little word بِ.
2. **ٱللَّهِ**: genitive, because it is the second word of the idafah, “the name **of** Allah”.
3. **ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ**: genitive, because it describes ٱللَّهِ and copies its case.

The endings are alike because three different reasons all lead to the genitive. This is why the Bismillah has so many “i” sounds at the end of its words.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Compare ٱللَّهُ لَطِيفٌۢ (Ash-Shura 42:19) with إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَطِيفٌ خَبِيرٌ (Luqman 31:16, end). Which endings changed, which did not, and why?">

- **ٱللَّهُ → ٱللَّهَ**: it changed from **-u to -a**. After إِنَّ the first part of the sentence is **accusative**.
- **لَطِيفٌ**: it did **not** change. It is still **-un**, nominative. The news after إِنَّ stays nominative.
- **خَبِيرٌ** is a second piece of news, so it is **-un** too.

So إِنَّ does not make the second word accusative. It makes the **first** word accusative. If the second word were accusative here, for example لَطِيفًا, it would be wrong in this sentence. L2.10 teaches the six little words that work like إِنَّ.

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Block id="practice-2" title="Practice: marks, sorting and meaning">
<Exercise n={5} q="Three words end in a vowel mark in خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مِنْ عَلَقٍ (Al-Alaq 96:2). Which of them are case endings, and which case?">

- **خَلَقَ**: the -a is the fixed ending of a past-tense verb. It is **not** a case.
- **ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ**: **accusative**, -a. It is what was created.
- **مِنْ**: a little word. It has no case.
- **عَلَقٍ**: **genitive**, -in. It follows مِنْ, and it has no *al-*.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Sort these by case, and say the mark. (a) ٱللَّهُ (Ash-Shura 42:19) (b) قَوْلًا (Al-Ahzab 33:70) (c) عَلَقٍ (Al-Alaq 96:2) (d) ٱلرَّحِيمِ (Al-Fatiha 1:1) (e) هُوَ (Ash-Shura 42:19)">

- (a) **Nominative**, -u: the starting point.
- (b) **Accusative**, -an: the object. It has no *al-*.
- (c) **Genitive**, -in: it follows مِنْ. It has no *al-*.
- (d) **Genitive**, -i: it describes ٱللَّهِ and copies its case.
- (e) **No visible ending.** A pronoun has one shape. Here it is the starting point, so it counts as nominative.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Endings decide the meaning. This is a made-up example. In قَرَأَ ٱلطَّالِبُ ٱلْكِتَابَ, who read? Now swap the endings: قَرَأَ ٱلطَّالِبَ ٱلْكِتَابُ. What does it mean?">

In the first sentence ٱلطَّالِبُ ends in -u, so he is the doer: **“The student read the book.”**

In the second the endings are swapped. ٱلطَّالِبَ has -a, so he is what was read, and ٱلْكِتَابُ has -u, so it is the doer: **“The book read the student.”** The words stayed where they were. The meaning changed, because the endings changed.

This is why case endings matter: they say who does what. The Quran is vowelled so that you can see them.

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="a noun plus an ending shows its job"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'a noun', small: 'the same word' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'an ending', small: '-u, -a or -i' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'its job', small: 'who does what' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- Arabic nouns change their **last vowel** to show their job. This is called **iʿrāb**.
- There are three cases. **Nominative**, رَفْع: **-u** or **-un**. **Accusative**, نَصْب: **-a** or **-an**. **Genitive**, جَرّ: **-i** or **-in**.
- Nominative is the case of the starting point, the news and the doer. Accusative is the case of the object. Genitive follows a little word, or belongs to the word before it.
- The case belongs to the job. The same word can take any of the three.
- Some words do not follow the plain pattern. A pronoun like هُوَ never changes, and still has a job. A word that ends in a long ā hides its mark. A few kinds use other marks.
- Case is for nouns. The -a of a basic past-tense verb is part of the verb, not a case. And after إِنَّ, the first part is accusative.

**You can now:**

1. Read the last vowel of a noun and name the case.
2. Say what job each case usually shows.
3. Spot a pronoun, a verb or a little word that does not take a case.
4. Go through a short verse word by word.
