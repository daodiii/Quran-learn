This verse is about witnesses for a written agreement. Read it from right to left. It has five nouns, and each one counts something.

- **رَجُلٌ**: one man.
- **رَجُلَيْنِ**: two men.
- **رِجَالِكُمْ**: your men, three or more.
- **شَهِيدَيْنِ**: two witnesses.
- **وَٱمْرَأَتَانِ**: two women.

In English, the word *two* is a separate word. In Arabic the number sits **inside the ending** of the noun. رَجُلَيْنِ is one word, and it already means “two men”.

English has two numbers. A noun is one (*book*) or more than one (*books*). To say *two*, English adds a word: *two books*.

Arabic has **three** numbers:

1. **Singular**, مُفْرَد (*mufrad*): one.
2. **Dual**, مُثَنَّى (*muthannā*): exactly two.
3. **Plural**, جَمْع (*jamʿ*): three or more.

The dual is the new one. A dual noun needs no word for “two”. Its ending says it.

<Note kind="warn">In L2.01 you met *mufrad* as “single”, meaning “not a sentence”. In this lesson, and in the grammar books’ lists of noun shapes, *mufrad* means “one, not two and not many”. Read the word in its place.</Note>

</Block>

  <Stage>

      ]}
      en={['Say: I seek safety with the Lord of the daybreak.']}
    />

The **singular** is the basic form of a noun. It is the form you meet first, and the one the other numbers are built from. رَبِّ (“Lord”) and ٱلْفَلَقِ (“the daybreak”) are both singular here.

| Singular | Meaning | Gender |
|---|---|---|
| كِتَابٌ | a book | masculine |
| رَجُلٌ | a man | masculine |
| مُسْلِمٌ | a Muslim man | masculine |
| مُسْلِمَةٌ | a Muslim woman | feminine |

A feminine noun often ends in ـَة (L1.07). You will see what happens to that ـَة in the dual and the plural.

Every noun has a gender **and** a number.
</Block>
</Step>

<Step id="dual" label="The dual" title="Exactly two: the dual">
<Block id="dual-make" title="How to make a dual">
  <Stage label="Single to dual">
    <Tiles
      label="كِتَابٌ, كِتَابَانِ, كِتَابَيْنِ: a book, two books"
      items={[
        { ar: 'كِتَابٌ', en: 'a book' },
        { ar: 'كِتَابَانِ', en: 'two books: -āni' },
        { ar: 'كِتَابَيْنِ', en: 'two books: -ayni' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Take the single word, then:

1. Take off the tanwīn (the doubled mark, -un).
2. Add the dual ending.

There are **two** dual endings. Which one you use depends on the word’s job in the sentence. This is its **case**, a subject you meet properly in L1.10 and in L2.04 to L2.06.

- **ـَانِ** (-āni): the nominative, for example a word that starts a sentence.
- **ـَيْنِ** (-ayni): the other two cases, the accusative and the genitive.

| Single | Dual with -āni | Dual with -ayni |
|---|---|---|
| كِتَابٌ (a book) | كِتَابَانِ | كِتَابَيْنِ |
| رَجُلٌ (a man) | رَجُلَانِ | رَجُلَيْنِ |
| مُسْلِمٌ (a Muslim man) | مُسْلِمَانِ | مُسْلِمَيْنِ |

The next three blocks show each of these endings in the Quran.

<Note kind="remember">Dual: take off the tanwīn, then add **-āni** or **-ayni**. The alif or the yāʾ before the nūn says “two”.</Note>
</Block>

جَنَّتَانِ means “two gardens”. The ending -āni is the dual. Here it is the nominative: the sentence is about the gardens. Its first part, “for whoever fears…”, only says who gets them (L2.01 teaches this shape).

Look at the word before the ending. The single word is جَنَّةٌ (a garden). It ends in the feminine ـَة, and in the dual that ـَة becomes **ـَت**: جَنَّ**ت**َانِ. The next block comes back to this.

Both words end in **-ayni**.

- رَجُلَيْنِ is the accusative: after the verb *kāna* (“to be”), its news word is accusative (L2.11). Al-Jadwal says: “accusative, and its mark is the yāʾ”.
- ٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ means “the two parents”. It comes after the little word *bi-* (“to, with”), so it is genitive (L2.06).

So -ayni is one ending for two cases. Only the sentence tells you which one it is. L2.05 and L2.06 teach how.

</Block>

The single word is ٱمْرَأَةٌ, “a woman”. Look at the ending ـَة. In the dual it turns into **ـَت**, and then the dual ending is added: ٱمْرَأَ**ت**َانِ.

| Single | Dual with -āni | Dual with -ayni |
|---|---|---|
| ٱمْرَأَةٌ (a woman) | ٱمْرَأَتَانِ | ٱمْرَأَتَيْنِ |
| جَنَّةٌ (a garden) | جَنَّتَانِ | جَنَّتَيْنِ |
| مُسْلِمَةٌ (a Muslim woman) | مُسْلِمَتَانِ | مُسْلِمَتَيْنِ |

So this verse counts with endings: **رَجُلٌ** is one man, and **ٱمْرَأَتَانِ** is two women.

The dual is not only for nouns.

- **A pronoun.** فِيهِمَا is *fī* (“in”) and **-humā** (“both of them”). The verse is about two gardens, so “both of them” means those two. L3.01 and L3.02 teach pronouns.
- **A verb.** يَسْجُدَانِ means “the two of them bow down”. The ending -āni is on a verb here. The plant and the tree are two things, so the verb is dual too. Level 3 teaches the verb.

<Note kind="know">Many readers know ٱلنَّجْمُ as “the star”. Al-Jadwal explains it in this verse as a plant with no stem, which is why the English says “plant”. Either way the verse names two things, so the verb is dual.</Note>

<Step id="plural" label="Plurals" title="Three or more: the plurals">
<Block id="plural-kinds" title="Three kinds of plural">
  <Stage label="Three plurals">
    <Tiles
      label="ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ, ٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ, قُلُوب: believers, believing women, hearts"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ', en: 'sound masculine' },
        { ar: 'ٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ', en: 'sound feminine' },
        { ar: 'قُلُوب', en: 'broken' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Arabic makes a plural in one of two ways.

- **Sound plural**, جَمْع سَالِم. The word stays whole, and an **ending** is added. There are two sound plurals: one for masculine words and one for feminine words.
- **Broken plural**, جَمْع تَكْسِير. The word is not kept whole. It **changes inside**.

That gives three kinds of plural altogether. Their case endings are different, and the next blocks go through them one by one. You can tell them apart by looking at the end of the word.
</Block>

<Block id="smp" title="The sound masculine plural: -ūna and -īna">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      reference="Al-Muminun 23:1"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'قَدْ', en: 'indeed', say: 'qad', type: 'p' }, { ar: 'أَفْلَحَ', en: 'have succeeded', say: 'aflaha', type: 'v' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ', en: 'the believers', say: 'al-muminuna' }] },
      ]}
      en={['The believers have truly succeeded.']}
    />
  </Stage>

The **sound masculine plural**, جَمْع مُذَكَّر سَالِم, is for groups of male people. The single word مُؤْمِنٌ stays whole, and the ending is added.

- **ـُونَ** (-ūna): the nominative. ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ did the succeeding.
- **ـِينَ** (-īna): the accusative and the genitive.

| Single | Plural with -ūna | Plural with -īna |
|---|---|---|
| مُسْلِمٌ (a Muslim man) | مُسْلِمُونَ | مُسْلِمِينَ |
| مُؤْمِنٌ (a believer) | مُؤْمِنُونَ | مُؤْمِنِينَ |
| مُعَلِّمٌ (a teacher) | مُعَلِّمُونَ | مُعَلِّمِينَ |

Compare the endings with the dual. A dual has an alif or a yāʾ **and a nūn** (-āni, -ayni). A sound masculine plural has a wāw or a yāʾ **and a nūn** (-ūna, -īna).

<Note kind="tip">Look at the letter before the nūn. A long **ā** (alif) is a nominative dual. A long **ū** (wāw) is a nominative masculine plural.</Note>

<Check n={6} q="Which ending is a nominative sound masculine plural?" options={['-āni', '-ūna', '-ātu']} answer={1} why="-ūna is the nominative. -āni is the nominative dual, and -ātu belongs to the feminine plural." />
</Block>

<Block id="sfp" title="The sound feminine plural: -āt">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      reference="Al-Ahzab 33:35 (start)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'إِنَّ', en: 'indeed', say: 'inna', type: 'p' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْمُسْلِمِينَ', en: 'Muslim men', say: 'al-muslimina' }, { ar: 'وَٱلْمُسْلِمَٰتِ', en: 'and Muslim women', say: 'wal-muslimati' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'وَٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ', en: 'and believing men', say: 'wal-muminina' }, { ar: 'وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ', en: 'and believing women', say: 'wal-muminati' }] },
      ]}
      en={['Indeed, Muslim men and Muslim women, believing men and believing women…']}
    />
  </Stage>

The verse names men and women in pairs. Each pair is a sound masculine plural (-īna) and a sound feminine plural (-āt).

The **sound feminine plural**, جَمْع مُؤَنَّث سَالِم, is made by taking off the ـَة of the single feminine word and adding **ـَات**.

| Single | Sound feminine plural |
|---|---|
| مُسْلِمَةٌ (a Muslim woman) | مُسْلِمَاتٌ |
| مُؤْمِنَةٌ (a believing woman) | مُؤْمِنَاتٌ |
| جَنَّةٌ (a garden) | جَنَّاتٌ |
| صَلَاةٌ (a prayer) | صَلَوَاتٌ |

The last row changes a little more. The alif of صَلَاةٌ turns into **وَا**: صَلَوَاتٌ. You can see it in Al-Muminun 23:9, in the word صَلَوَٰتِهِمْ (“their prayers”). So “take off ـَة and add ـَات” is the main rule, and some words also change inside.

A sound feminine plural shows its case with a **vowel**, not a letter. Both ٱلْمُسْلِمَٰتِ and ٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ here end in a kasra, because the verse puts them after إِنَّ, and L2.05 teaches the accusative.

</Block>

A **broken plural** is not made by adding an ending. The single word is **broken** and put together again in a new shape. English does something like this with *man, men* and *foot, feet*.

| Single | Broken plural | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| كِتَابٌ (book) | كُتُبٌ | new vowels, the alif goes |
| رَجُلٌ (man) | رِجَالٌ | new vowels, an alif is added |
| بَيْتٌ (house) | بُيُوتٌ | new vowels, a wāw is added |
| قَلْبٌ (heart) | قُلُوبٌ | new vowels, a wāw is added |

You cannot work out a broken plural from the single word. Learn each one **with its single word**. A shape such as فُعُول (as in قُلُوب and بُيُوت) is called a pattern. Level 3 teaches patterns.

In 55:70 the two kinds of plural stand side by side. خَيْرَٰتٌ ends in -ātun, a sound feminine plural. حِسَانٌ is a broken plural, and al-Jadwal says so.

<Note kind="know">Some plurals come from a different word. The plural of ٱمْرَأَةٌ (“a woman”) is نِسَاءٌ. It is another word, not a changed form. You see it in An-Nisa 4:7.</Note>

<Block id="ending-preview" title="A look ahead: the endings and the cases">
  <Stage label="From al-Ajurrumiyyah">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote lx-quote-list" lang="ar" dir="rtl">
      <p>فأما التثنية فترفع بالألف وتنصب وتخفض بالياء</p>
      <p>وأما جمع المذكر السالم فيرفع بالواو وينصب ويخفض بالياء</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Written here without vowel marks.</p>
  </Stage>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah*, the first grammar book many students learn, says it in two lines: “The dual is raised with the alif, and put in the accusative and the genitive with the yāʾ. The sound masculine plural is raised with the wāw, and put in the accusative and the genitive with the yāʾ.” (To be “raised” is to be nominative.)

Here is the whole picture. You do not need to learn it yet. Levels 1 and 2 return to it again and again.

| Kind of noun | Nominative | Accusative | Genitive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single noun, broken plural | -u or -un | -a or -an | -i or -in |
| Dual | -āni | -ayni | -ayni |
| Sound masculine plural | -ūna | -īna | -īna |
| Sound feminine plural | -ātu or -ātun | -āti or -ātin | -āti or -ātin |

- L1.10 introduces the three cases.
- L2.04 teaches the nominative column, L2.05 the accusative, and L2.06 the genitive.
- The dual and the masculine plural lose their final **nūn** when they are the first word of an idafah (“X of Y”, L2.08).
</Block>

<Step id="mistakes" label="Mistakes, word by word" title="Five mistakes, and four verses word by word">
<Block id="mistake-verb" title="Mistake 1: reading every -ūna as a plural noun">
  <Stage label="Two words that end in -ūna">
    <Tiles
      label="تَعْبُدُونَ is a verb and مُعْرِضُونَ is a noun"
      items={[
        { ar: 'تَعْبُدُونَ', en: 'you worship', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'مُعْرِضُونَ', en: 'turning away', type: 'n' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Both words end in -ūna. Only one of them is a plural noun.

- مُعْرِضُونَ (Al-Baqarah 2:83) is a sound masculine plural. It comes from the single word مُعْرِضٌ.
- تَعْبُدُونَ (Al-Baqarah 2:83) is a **verb**: “you worship”. Its -ūna means “you, many people”. It is not a noun ending.

Test the word. A noun can take *al-*: ٱلْمُعْرِضُونَ works. A verb cannot. You met this test in L1.04. Level 3 teaches the verb endings.

<Block id="mistake-an" title="Mistake 2: reading every -ān as a dual">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      reference="Ar-Rahman 55:68"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'فِيهِمَا', en: 'in both of them', say: 'fihima', type: 'p' }] },

      en={['In both of them are fruit, palm trees and pomegranates.']}
    />

وَرُمَّانٌ ends in ـَانٌ. It looks like a dual, but it is a **single** word.

- A dual ends in **-āni**, with a **kasra** under the nūn: جَنَّتَانِ.
- رُمَّانٌ ends in **-ānun**, with a tanwīn on the nūn. The nūn belongs to the word itself.

The Corpus tags رُمَّانٌ as singular. Two other words in this verse are singular as well: فَٰكِهَةٌ and نَخْلٌ. They look like one thing, but they name a whole kind of thing, like *fruit* in English.

<Block id="mistake-ta" title="Mistake 3: making every feminine word end in -āt">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence

      ]}
      en={['…except in fortified villages.']}
    />
  </Stage>

The single word is قَرْيَةٌ, “a village”. It ends in ـَة, so a learner expects قَرْيَاتٌ. The Quran uses **قُرًى**, a broken plural.

A feminine noun can have a sound feminine plural (جَنَّةٌ, جَنَّاتٌ) or a broken plural (قَرْيَةٌ, قُرًى). You learn which one with each word, as you do in English with *ox, oxen*.

Look at مُّحَصَّنَةٍ (“fortified”). It describes “villages”, a plural of things, and it is a **single feminine** word. This is the rule from the last step, and L2.09 teaches it.

<Note kind="warn">The rule “take off ـَة, add ـَات” makes a sound plural. It does not tell you that a word has one.</Note>
</Block>

  <Stage>

Both nouns end in -ayni. The cases are different.

- شَهِيدَيْنِ is what the verb “call” falls on. It is accusative. Al-Jadwal: “accusative, and its mark is the yāʾ”.
- ٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ comes after *bi-*. It is genitive.

The same is true of -īna. A dual or a sound masculine plural has one ending for two cases. Ask what the word is doing in the sentence. L2.05 and L2.06 teach how.

<Block id="mistake-dual" title="Mistake 5: thinking two is always a dual">
  <Stage>

You learned that exactly two takes a dual. Usually it does, as in رَجُلَانِ and جَنَّتَانِ. But look at this verse. It speaks to **two** people. It says قُلُوبُكُمَا, “the hearts of you two”, and قُلُوب is a **plural**.

The word **-kumā** (“of you two”) carries the “two”. The Corpus tags قُلُوب as a plural here. This course does not teach the grammar behind this form. Remember only this: the dual is the normal way to say two, and a verse can say it another way.

<Exercise n={1} q="Single, dual or plural? (a) جَنَّتَانِ (Ar-Rahman 55:46) (b) ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ (Al-Muminun 23:1) (c) قُلُوبُهُمْ (Al-Hashr 59:14) (d) ٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ (Al-Ahzab 33:35) (e) رَجُلٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:282) (f) ٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ (Al-Baqarah 2:83)">

- (a) **Dual**: -āni, “two gardens”.
- (b) **Plural**: -ūna, a sound masculine plural, “the believers”.
- (c) **Plural**: a broken plural, “their hearts”. The single word is قَلْبٌ.
- (d) **Plural**: -āt, a sound feminine plural, “believing women”.
- (e) **Single**: no number ending, “one man”.
- (f) **Dual**: -ayni, “the two parents”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Make the dual with -āni: (1) يَوْمٌ (a day) (2) مُسْلِمَةٌ (a Muslim woman) (3) مُعَلِّمٌ (a teacher) (4) جَنَّةٌ (a garden). Then make كِتَابٌ (a book) into a dual with -ayni. These are made-up examples.">

Take off the tanwīn, then add the ending. A feminine ـَة becomes ـَت first.

1. يَوْمٌ → **يَوْمَانِ**, “two days”.
2. مُسْلِمَةٌ → **مُسْلِمَتَانِ**, “two Muslim women”. The ـَة became ـَت.
3. مُعَلِّمٌ → **مُعَلِّمَانِ**, “two teachers”.
4. جَنَّةٌ → **جَنَّتَانِ**, “two gardens”. You saw this word in 55:46.
5. كِتَابٌ → **كِتَابَيْنِ**, “two books” (-ayni).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Sound masculine plural, sound feminine plural or broken plural? (a) ٱلصَّٰدِقِينَ (Al-Ahzab 33:35) (b) ٱلصَّٰدِقَٰتِ (Al-Ahzab 33:35) (c) أَرْبَابٌ (Yusuf 12:39) (d) حِسَانٌ (Ar-Rahman 55:70) (e) خَيْرَٰتٌ (Ar-Rahman 55:70)">

- (a) **Sound masculine plural**: -īna, “the truthful men”.
- (b) **Sound feminine plural**: -āt, “the truthful women”.
- (c) **Broken plural**: the inside of رَبٌّ changed, “lords”.
- (d) **Broken plural**: al-Jadwal says so, “beautiful ones”.
- (e) **Sound feminine plural**: -ātun, “good ones”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Make the plural named in brackets: (1) مُسْلِمٌ (a sound masculine plural, with -ūna) (2) مُسْلِمَةٌ (a sound feminine plural) (3) كِتَابٌ (a broken plural) (4) صَلَاةٌ (a sound feminine plural). These are made-up examples.">

1. مُسْلِمٌ → **مُسْلِمُونَ**, “Muslim men”.
2. مُسْلِمَةٌ → **مُسْلِمَاتٌ**, “Muslim women”. The ـَة is taken off and ـَات is added.
3. كِتَابٌ → **كُتُبٌ**, “books”. The vowels change and the alif goes. You cannot guess this from the single word.
4. صَلَاةٌ → **صَلَوَاتٌ**, “prayers”. The ـَة goes, ـَات is added, and the alif turns into وَا. You can see it in Al-Muminun 23:9 (صَلَوَٰتِهِمْ).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Which cases can each word be? (a) جَنَّتَانِ (Ar-Rahman 55:46) (b) شَهِيدَيْنِ (Al-Baqarah 2:282) (c) ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ (Al-Muminun 23:1) (d) ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ (Al-Ahzab 33:35)">

- (a) **Nominative only**: -āni is the nominative dual.
- (b) **Accusative or genitive**: -ayni. In 2:282 it is accusative.
- (c) **Nominative only**: -ūna is the nominative sound masculine plural.
- (d) **Accusative or genitive**: -īna. In 33:35 it is accusative, after إِنَّ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Choose the describing word that matches. (a) ٱلْكِتَابَانِ ___ (the two books are big) (b) ٱلْكُتُبُ ___ (the books are big) (c) ٱلْمُعَلِّمُونَ ___ (the teachers are good). Use كَبِيرَانِ, كَبِيرَةٌ and صَالِحُونَ. These are made-up examples.">

- (a) ٱلْكِتَابَانِ **كَبِيرَانِ**: a dual noun gets a dual describing word.
- (b) ٱلْكُتُبُ **كَبِيرَةٌ**: books are things, so the describing word is feminine single.
- (c) ٱلْمُعَلِّمُونَ **صَالِحُونَ**: teachers are people, so the describing word is a sound masculine plural.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Look at فِى قُرًى مُّحَصَّنَةٍ (Al-Hashr 59:14). Is مُّحَصَّنَةٍ a plural word? Meaning: “in fortified villages”.">

No. قُرًى is a plural (a broken plural of قَرْيَةٌ), but مُّحَصَّنَةٍ is **feminine single**. Villages are things, so their describing word is feminine single. L2.09 teaches this rule in full.

        { ar: 'رِجَالٌ', en: 'many men' },

- Arabic has **three numbers**: single (one), dual (exactly two) and plural (three or more).
- The dual: take off the tanwīn, add **-āni** (nominative) or **-ayni** (accusative and genitive). A feminine ـَة becomes ـَت.
- Sound masculine plural: **-ūna** or **-īna**. Sound feminine plural: **-āt**. Broken plural: the word changes inside, and you learn each one.
- Look at the end of the word to find the kind, but check the word type too. A verb can end in -ūna, and a single word can end in -ān.
- The same ending can serve two cases (-ayni, -īna). The sentence tells you which.
- A word that describes a plural of things is usually feminine single.
