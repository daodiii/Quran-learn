This is a whole verse from Surah Ash-Sharh. It has four words and 13 letters. You can already see three things.

- The line starts on the **right**. Arabic is read from right to left.
- The letters of a word are joined, like handwriting.
- Small marks sit above the letters. They tell you how to say each letter.

The colours on the tiles show word types. You learn those in L1.04, so ignore them for now.

By the end of this lesson you can read every letter and every mark in this verse: *alam nashraḥ laka ṣadraka*.

The word صَدْرَكَ (*ṣadraka*, “your chest”) has four letters. The first letter is the one on the right. Then you move left: ص, then د, then ر, then ك. The sound is *ṣa-d-ra-ka*.

Arabic has no capital letters. It also has no separate printed and handwritten styles. Printed Arabic is joined, the way English handwriting is.

<Note kind="warn">Do not read Arabic from left to right. You would read every word backwards.</Note>

The Arabic alphabet has **28 letters**. Its name is *ḥurūf al-hijāʾ*, حُرُوفُ ٱلْهِجَاءِ. Each letter has a name and a sound. Step 2 teaches them in seven small families, so you do not have to learn 28 shapes at once.

Most of the letters are consonants. Three of them, alif, wāw and yāʾ, also write long vowels. L1.02 teaches that. Some lists count a 29th sign, the hamza (ء). You meet it in step 2.

<Note kind="know">The word *ḥarf* (حَرْف) means “letter”. It also means “little word”, a kind of word you meet in L1.04. It is one word with two uses. *Ḥurūf* is its plural.</Note>

A letter changes its shape depending on where it stands in a run of joined letters. Most letters have four forms.

| Form | Where it stands | What it joins to |
|---|---|---|
| Alone | by itself | nothing |
| Start | first in a joined run | the next letter (on its left) |
| Middle | inside the run | the letter before and the letter after |
| End | last in the run | the letter before (on its right) |

The long line ـ in the tiles is only a joining line. It shows where the letter holds on to its neighbour. It is not part of the letter.

In most letters the body stays the same. Only the ends change, to join up with the neighbours.

</Block>

Six letters join to the letter **before** them, but never to the letter **after** them: ا د ذ ر ز و.

So these six have only two shapes. The alone form and the start form look the same. The middle form and the end form look the same. The letter that comes after one of them begins a new run of joined letters.

A gap after one of these six is **not a space between words**. The word goes on. Words are separated by a wider space.

Counting shapes: 28 letters with four forms would be 112 shapes. Six letters have only two, so there are 12 fewer, and you learn 100.

Four of the six have no dots: ا د ر و. The other two, ذ and ز, have one dot above.

The last three letters have no family. Alif and wāw are two of the six letters that do not join forward.

| Letter | Sound | Alone | Start | Middle | End |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alif | a tall straight stroke. It writes a long ā (L1.02), or it carries a hamza | ا | ا | ـا | ـا |
| Hāʾ | h, as in *hat* | ه | هـ | ـهـ | ـه |
| Wāw | w, as in *we* (it also writes a long ū) | و | و | ـو | ـو |

The **hamza**, هَمْزَة, is a stop in the throat, like the pause in the middle of “uh-oh”. It is a sign. It is written on a “seat”: an alif, a wāw, a yāʾ, or on the line by itself.

The seat you meet most often is the alif. The hamza goes **above** the alif (أ) when its vowel is a fatḥa or a ḍamma. It goes **below** the alif (إ) when its vowel is a kasra.

You have already seen a hamza on an alif: the first letter of أَلَمْ in Ash-Sharh 94:1. You meet the hamza under an alif in step 4, in the word إِذْ.

</Block>

Some letters look alike. Only the dots tell them apart. Some sound alike to an English ear. The ear has to learn the difference.

**Look-alikes.** The dots are never decoration. Slow down at first and count the dots on every letter.

| Same body | How the dots tell them apart |
|---|---|
| ب ت ث ن ي | one dot below, two above, three above, one above, two below |
| ج ح خ | one dot below, none, one above |
| د ذ | none, one above |
| ر ز | none, one above |
| س ش | none, three above |
| ص ض | none, one above |
| ط ظ | none, one above |
| ع غ | none, one above |
| ف ق | one dot above, two dots above |

**Sound-alikes.** English spells both sounds of ث and ذ as *th*. Several other pairs sound alike to a new listener.

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| ث and ذ | th in *think*, and th in *this* |
| س and ص | light s, and heavy s |
| ت and ط | light t, and heavy t |
| د and ض | light d, and heavy d |
| ذ and ظ | light dh, and heavy dh |
| ه and ح | a light h, and a strong breathy ḥ |
| ك and ق | k at the front of the mouth, and q far back in the throat |

You now know which shape to expect. Here is how to find it in a word.

1. Cut the word after every letter that does not join forward (ا د ذ ر ز و).
2. Each piece is a joined run.
3. In a run, the first letter takes the **start** form, the last letter takes the **end** form, and the letters between take the **middle** form.
4. A run of one letter takes the **alone** form.

In صَدْرَكَ the letters ص and د form the first run. The د does not join forward, so the run stops there. ر is a run by itself, and so is ك. The ك is the last letter of the word, but it takes the alone form because the ر before it does not join to it.

</Block>

        { ar: 'حْ', en: 'alone', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Take the word نَشْرَحْ (*nashraḥ*, “We open”). The letter ر does not join forward, so the word has two runs: نَشْرَ and حْ.

| Letter | Form | Mark | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| ن | start | fatḥa | na |
| ش | middle | sukūn | sh |
| ر | end | fatḥa | ra |
| ح | alone | sukūn | ḥ |

The small circle on ش and ح is a **sukūn**. It means that the letter has no vowel after it. You say the letter and go straight on. L1.02 teaches it in full.

The first word of the verse, أَلَمْ (*alam*), also has two runs: أَ (an alif, which does not join forward) and لَمْ (lām at the start, mīm at the end).

<Block id="gap" title="A gap inside a word is not a space">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:2 (start)"

Look at ٱلْكِتَٰبُ (*al-kitābu*, “the Book”). The long ā after ت is written as a **small alif** above the letter: ٰ. It is a mark, not a letter. So the ت stays joined to the ب after it.

Modern spelling writes a full alif instead: كِتَاب. A full alif does not join forward, so there the run would be cut after it. The Quran text uses the small alif for many long ā sounds, so the word stays in one piece.

In ذَٰلِكَ the long ā is also a small alif above ذ. Modern spelling does not write this ā at all (ذلك). Both words show that Quran spelling can differ from the spelling you see in newspapers and books.

The first letter of ٱلْكِتَٰبُ is a special alif, **ٱ**. It is an alif, so it does not join forward. The block on ٱللَّهُ below returns to it.

<Block id="wasla" title="The alif at the start of ٱللَّهُ">
  <Stage>

The word ٱللَّهُ (*Allāhu*) shows four forms in one word.

| Letter | Form | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ٱ | alone | alif never joins forward |
| ل | start | it is the first letter of the run after the alif |
| ل | middle | it joins both sides |
| ه | end | it ends the run |

The two lām letters are different forms: the first is a start form and the second is a middle form. The mark ـّ on the second lām is a **shadda**. It means the lām is said twice. L1.02 teaches the shadda.

The alif at the start of the word has a small sign on top: **ٱ**. This is the *alif waṣla*, “the joining alif”. You will learn when it is said and when it is silent in L1.02 and L1.06 (the word *al-*). For now, read it as a plain alif.

Quran spelling does not write the long ā of *Allāh*. You say it, but it has no mark.
</Block>

<Block id="lam-alif" title="Lām and alif make one shape">

When lām is followed by alif, the two letters join in a special shape: **لا**. They are still two letters, lām and then alif. You see this shape in لَا (*lā*, “no”) many times.

The alif cannot join forward, so the lām-alif shape always ends a run.

In فِيهِ (*fīhi*, “in it”) the yāʾ is in the middle of the word, and you can see its two dots below. Compare this with the next block.
</Block>

Look at the last letter of فِى (*fī*, “in”) and عَلَىٰ (*ʿalā*, “on”). It is ى, a yāʾ **without its two dots**.

In the Quran text on this site, 6,046 words end in ى, and not one ends in a dotted ي. The same shape ى ends words that sound like *ī* (فِى) and words that sound like *ā* (عَلَىٰ). You learn the sound of each word with the word. L1.02 teaches the long vowels.

The yāʾ in the middle of a word keeps its dots, as in فِيهِ.

<Step id="vowels" label="Short vowels" title="The three short vowels">
<Block id="why" title="Why a letter needs a mark">
  <Stage label="Same letters, three words">
    <Tiles
      label="كَتَبَ, كُتِبَ, كُتُبٌ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'كَتَبَ', en: 'he wrote', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'كُتِبَ', en: 'it was written', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'كُتُبٌ', en: 'books' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The three words above are built from the same three letters: ك, ت and ب. Only the marks differ. The marks change the sound and the meaning.

English puts its vowels in the word as letters: *c-a-t*. Arabic puts a short vowel as a **mark** on the letter before it. These marks are called the *ḥarakāt* (حَرَكَات). There are three: fatḥa, kasra and ḍamma.

In the Quran text the marks are always written. In books and newspapers they are usually left out, and the reader supplies them from knowing the language. You are learning to read the Quran, so you learn the marks first.

Nearly every letter that is said carries a mark: a short vowel, or the sukūn. A few letters carry none. They make a long vowel or stay silent. L1.02 explains them.

The mark at the end of كُتُبٌ is a double ḍamma, called *tanwīn*. It makes the ending *-un*. L1.02 teaches it.

</Block>

<Block id="fatha" title="Fatḥa: the sound a">
  <Stage label="Fatḥa on three letters">
    <Tiles

    <Sentence

        { words: [{ ar: 'لَكَ', en: 'for you', say: 'laka', type: 'p' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'صَدْرَكَ', en: 'your chest', say: 'sadraka' }] },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The **fatḥa**, فَتْحَة, is a small slanted stroke **above** a letter. It gives the short sound **a**, as in *cat*.

The verse from the start of the lesson is a good example. It has 13 letters, and 9 of them carry a fatḥa: أَ لَ نَ رَ لَ كَ صَ رَ كَ. The other four carry a sukūn. This verse has no kasra and no ḍamma.

Read it aloud, letter by letter: *a-lam na-sh-ra-ḥ la-ka ṣad-ra-ka*.

</Block>

<Block id="kasra" title="Kasra: the sound i">
  <Stage label="Kasra on three letters">
    <Tiles
      label="بِ تِ ثِ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'بِ', en: 'bi', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'تِ', en: 'ti', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ثِ', en: 'thi', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The **kasra**, كَسْرَة, is a small slanted stroke **below** a letter. It gives the short sound **i**, as in *sit*.

It is the only one of the three marks that sits below the letter. If you see a small stroke under a letter, it is always a kasra.

You can see two kasras in بِسْمِ (*bismi*, “in the name of”), the first word of the Quran: بِ is *bi* and مِ is *mi*. The سْ between them has a sukūn.
</Block>

<Block id="damma" title="Ḍamma: the sound u">
  <Stage label="Ḍamma on three letters">
    <Tiles
      label="بُ تُ ثُ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'بُ', en: 'bu', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'تُ', en: 'tu', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ثُ', en: 'thu', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The **ḍamma**, ضَمَّة, is a small curl **above** a letter. It gives the short sound **u**, as in *put*.

Look at the two marks above the line. The fatḥa is a slanted stroke. The ḍamma is a curl, like a small و. The kasra is below the line.

You meet the ḍamma in قُلْ (*qul*, “say”). It starts 135 verses of the Quran, for example Al-Kafirun 109:1. The ق carries a ḍamma, so it is *qu*. The ل carries a sukūn, so the word is *qul*.

Compare the same letter with each mark: قَ is *qa*, قِ is *qi* and قُ is *qu*.

</Block>

<Step id="practice" label="Mistakes and practice" title="Common mistakes and practice">
<Block id="mistake-dots" title="Mistake 1: skipping the dots">
  <Stage label="One body, three letters">
    <Tiles
      label="jīm, ḥāʾ, khāʾ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ج', en: 'jīm: dot below', type: 'x' },

Letters such as ب ت ث or ج ح خ share a body and differ only by their dots. Beginners often read fast and skip the dots. It is a common reason for reading a word wrongly.

Count the dots on every letter, and look at where they are. Speed comes later.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-gap" title="Mistake 2: reading a gap as a space">
  <Stage label="Three runs, one word">
    <Tiles
      label="صَدْرَكَ is one word in three runs"
      items={[
        { ar: 'صَدْ', en: 'run 1', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'رَ', en: 'run 2', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'كَ', en: 'run 3', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

After ا د ذ ر ز و the next letter starts a new run, so there is a small gap. A beginner may think a new word has started. It has not. صَدْرَكَ is one word, even though it has three runs.

The space between words is wider than the gap inside a word. Do not split the word at the gap. Look for the wider space between words.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-marks" title="Mistake 3: ignoring the vowel marks">
  <Stage label="Same letters, different words">
    <Tiles
      label="كَتَبَ, كُتِبَ, كُتُبٌ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'كَتَبَ', en: 'kataba: he wrote', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'كُتِبَ', en: 'kutiba: it was written', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'كُتُبٌ', en: 'kutubun: books' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The same three consonants make three different words, depending on the marks. If you ignore the marks, you cannot tell them apart.

Every mark is part of the spelling of the Quran. Read each one.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-kasra" title="Mistake 4: mixing up kasra and yāʾ">
  <Stage label="A mark and a letter">
    <Tiles
      label="بِ is bi, and بِي is bī"
      items={[
        { ar: 'بِ', en: 'bi: kasra, a short i', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'بِي', en: 'bī: kasra plus yāʾ, a long ī', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The kasra is a small stroke **below** a letter. The yāʾ is a full letter with two dots. Both give an *i* sound. The kasra makes it short, and a yāʾ after a kasra makes it long.

A mark is on a letter. A letter stands in the line. L1.02 teaches the long vowels.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-th" title="Mistake 5: one English spelling for two sounds">
  <Stage label="Two sounds, one English spelling">
    <Tiles
      label="ث and ذ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ث', en: 'th, as in think', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ذ', en: 'dh, as in this', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

English spells the sounds of ث and ذ the same way, *th*. They are two different letters. Say ث with the *th* of *think* and ذ with the *th* of *this*. This course writes them *th* and *dh*.

The same care is needed for the light and heavy pairs: س and ص, ت and ط, د and ض, ذ and ظ. A heavy letter is a different letter, not a louder one.
</Block>

Try each exercise first. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Which form of the letter mīm (م) is in the word بِسْمِ (Al-Fatiha 1:1, start), and how do you know?">

**The end form** (ـم).

The mīm is the last letter of the word. The sīn before it joins forward, so the mīm is the end of the run. It has a joining line on its right and none on its left.

If mīm stood in the middle of a run, it would take the middle form ـمـ.
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="How many dots does thāʾ (ث) have, and how does it differ from bāʾ (ب) and tāʾ (ت)?">

**Three dots above.**

All three letters have the same body. They differ only in the dots:

- Bāʾ (ب): one dot **below**.
- Tāʾ (ت): two dots **above**.
- Thāʾ (ث): three dots **above**.

ث sounds like the *th* in *think*. Do not mix it up with ذ (dhāl), which sounds like the *th* in *this*.
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Cut صَدْرَكَ (Ash-Sharh 94:1, end) into joined runs, and name the form of each letter.">

The د and the ر do not join forward. The runs are:

- صَدْ: ص has the **start** form, د has the **end** form.
- رَ: ر is **alone**.
- كَ: ك is **alone**, because the ر before it does not join to it.

That is three runs in one word.
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Which of these letters stop the join with the next letter? ب ر س ذ ل و ع">

**ر ذ و.**

They are three of the six letters ا د ذ ر ز و. The letters ب س ل ع join forward.
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="The second letter of أَحَدٌ (Al-Ikhlas 112:1, end) has no dots. Is it ج, ح or خ? Name the form.">

**ح (ḥāʾ).** ج has a dot below and خ has a dot above.

In أَحَدٌ the ح comes after the alif, which does not join forward. So the ح starts a new run, with the **start** form: حَ. The د that follows ends the run with the **end** form.
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Read the word نَشْرَحْ (Ash-Sharh 94:1, part) letter by letter. Give each letter, its form and its mark.">

<Exercise n={7} q="What mark is on the qāf (ق) of قُلْ (Al-Ikhlas 112:1, start), and what sound does it make?">

**A ḍamma**, the small curl above the letter. It makes the sound **u**. So ق with a ḍamma is *qu*.

The lām that follows has a sukūn, so you say *l* with no vowel. The word is *qul*, “say”.

With a fatḥa the letter would be قَ (*qa*), and with a kasra قِ (*qi*). The mark changes the sound.

<Exercise n={8} q="Read the word هُوَ (Al-Ikhlas 112:1, part) aloud. Why is it hu-wa and not ha-wa?">

The ه carries a **ḍamma**, not a fatḥa. The letter gives the *h*. The mark tells you the vowel after it:

- هُ is *hu*, with a ḍamma.
- هَ is *ha*, with a fatḥa.
- هِ is *hi*, with a kasra.

The wāw has a fatḥa, so it is *wa*. Together the word is *huwa*, “he”.
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Analyse the word بِسْمِ (Al-Fatiha 1:1, start): give each letter's form and its mark.">

|---|---|---|---|

Together: *bismi*, “in the name of”.

- قِ: qāf with a **kasra**: *qi*.
- مْ: mīm with a **sukūn**: *m*.

Together: *aqim*. The word has the alif carrying a hamza, then a start-form qāf, then an end-form mīm. The alif does not join forward, so the ق begins a new run.

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="letters plus short vowel marks equal a word you can read"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'letters', small: '28 of them' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'short vowels', small: '3 marks' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'a word you can read', small: 'كَلِمَة' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- Arabic is read from **right to left**. The first letter of a word is on the right.
- The alphabet has **28 letters**. Most have four shapes: alone, start, middle and end.
- Six letters (ا د ذ ر ز و) join to the letter before but not to the one after. They have two shapes.
- A word is cut into joined runs after those six letters. The gap is not a space.
- Dots tell look-alike letters apart. Count them and check above or below.
- Three short vowels: **fatḥa** ـَ (a, above), **kasra** ـِ (i, below), **ḍamma** ـُ (u, above). The sukūn ـْ means no vowel.
- In the Quran text the marks are always written.

**Next:** L1.02 teaches the rest of the reading marks: long vowels, the sukūn, the shadda (doubling), tanwīn and the small alif. L1.03 reads the whole Bismillah with them. Before you move on, check that you can name any letter in the families above and name the three marks on sight.
