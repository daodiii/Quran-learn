These are the first two verses of Surah Ash-Shams. Read them from right to left.

Each verse has a word that starts with ٱل:

- ٱلشَّمْسِ means “the sun”.
- ٱلْقَمَرِ means “the moon”.

Now say them aloud: *wash-shamsi* and *wal-qamari*. In the second word you hear an **l**. In the first word you do not. The same two letters, ٱل, are said in two ways. This lesson shows why.

The little word وَ at the start of each verse means “by” here, as in an oath. L2.07 teaches it.

English has two small words in front of a noun: *a* and *the*. “A house” means any house. “The house” means one house that we both know.

Arabic does the same job in a different way.

- There is **no word for “a”**. A noun without ٱلْ is “a …”. It usually ends in tanwīn: بَيْتٌ, “a house”.
- **ٱلْ means “the”**. It is not a separate word. It is joined to the front of the noun, with no space: ٱلْبَيْتُ, “the house”.

The grammar books call a noun with “the” **definite**, مَعْرِفَة (*maʿrifa*). A noun with “a” is **indefinite**, نَكِرَة (*nakira*).

<Note kind="remember">ٱلْ goes on the front of a noun and makes it “the …”. Without it, the noun is “a …”.</Note>
</Block>

In L1.02 you met **tanwīn**: the extra *n* at the end of a noun, written as a doubled vowel mark (ـٌ is *-un*). It is the mark of “a …”.

When you add ٱلْ, the tanwīn goes.

| Without ٱلْ | With ٱلْ | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| رَجُلٌ *rajulun* | ٱلرَّجُلُ *ar-rajulu* | The tanwīn is gone. The ending is now *-u*. |
| يَوْمٌ *yawmun* | ٱلْيَوْمُ *al-yawmu* | The tanwīn is gone. The ending is now *-u*. |

The case ending stays. Both words are still nominative, ending in *-u*. Only the *n* is lost. A noun cannot be “a …” and “the …” at the same time, so a word never has both ٱلْ and tanwīn.

Notice that ٱلرَّجُلُ is said *ar-rajulu*, not *al-rajulu*, while ٱلْيَوْمُ is said *al-yawmu*. Step 2 explains this.

This verse has one definite noun and one indefinite noun side by side.

- ٱلْعُسْرِ starts with ٱلْ: “**the** hardship”.
- يُسْرًا ends in tanwīn (*-an*, ـً) and has no ٱلْ: “**an** ease”.

The verse before it, 94:5, says exactly the same words. That gives a small test of what ٱلْ does.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal passes on what the commentators say about the two verses. Arabic usually treats a repeated definite noun as the same thing, and a repeated indefinite noun as possibly a new thing. So ٱلْعُسْرِ, repeated, is the same hardship, while يُسْرًا, repeated, can be a new ease. This is the reason for the saying that one hardship does not overcome two eases. Al-Jadwal also records another view, so treat this as the commentators’ reading.</Note>

<Block id="written" title="The lam is written, but not always said">

ٱلْ is two letters: an alif and a lam (ل). The lam is **written** (the one case where it looks different is a noun that starts with ل, below). But you say it only some of the time.

- ٱلْقَمَرُ is said *al-qamaru*. You hear the **l**.
- ٱلشَّمْسُ is said *ash-shamsu*. You do not hear the **l**. The ش is said twice instead, as if the lam melted into it.

What decides? The **first letter of the noun**.

- Fourteen letters leave the lam alone. They are the **moon letters**, حُرُوف قَمَرِيَّة (*ḥurūf qamariyya*). The name comes from ٱلْقَمَرُ, “the moon”, which starts with one of them (ق).
- Fourteen letters swallow the lam. They are the **sun letters**, حُرُوف شَمْسِيَّة (*ḥurūf shamsiyya*). The name comes from ٱلشَّمْسُ, “the sun”, which starts with one of them (ش).

There are fourteen of each, so neither group is bigger. Together they cover the whole alphabet.

<Note kind="remember">The spelling of ٱلْ never changes. Only the sound changes, and the first letter of the noun decides it.</Note>

<Block id="clue" title="Reading the clue in the marks">
  <Stage label="What the marks tell you">
    <Tiles
      label="ٱلْقَمَرُ and ٱلشَّمْسُ side by side"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلْقَمَرُ', en: 'sukūn on the lam: say it' },

In a text with all the marks, such as the Quran, the marks tell you which way to read.

- **Moon letter:** the lam of ٱلْ has a sukūn. Say it.
- **Sun letter:** the lam of ٱلْ has no mark, and the next letter has a shadda. Skip the lam and say the next letter twice.

Look back at ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ and ٱلْغَيْبِ in the last block. You can now read both without being told.

Books and newspapers often leave the marks out. Then the clue is gone and you must know the fourteen letters. That is why the two tables above are worth learning.
</Block>

<Block id="special" title="Two letters that need a second look">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="ٱلَّيْلِ and ٱلْأَرْضِ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلَّيْلِ', en: 'allayli: ل is a sun letter' },
        { ar: 'ٱلْأَرْضِ', en: 'al-ʾarḍi: hamza is a moon letter' },
      ]}

**The letter ل.** The lam is itself a sun letter. The noun لَيْل (*layl*, night) starts with a lam, so the lam of ٱلْ melts into it: *allayli*, with a doubled l. Look at the spelling. The text writes only **one** ل before the ي, and it carries a shadda. Two lams are said, and one lam with a shadda is written.

**The hamza.** In ٱلْأَرْضِ the letter after ٱلْ is a hamza written on an alif. It is a moon letter, so the lam has a sukūn and you say it: *al-ʾarḍi*.
</Block>

  <Stage>

When a sun letter swallows the lam, some learners drop the lam from the spelling too. Do not. The lam is **written**. It is only left unsaid. (Before another ل the two lams are written as one ل with a shadda, as in ٱلَّيْلِ.)

The spelling of ٱلْ is the same before all 28 letters. Only your mouth changes.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-shadda" title="Mistake 2: saying the lam before a sun letter">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="wrong and right"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلْشَّمْسُ', en: 'wrong: a sukūn on the lam and a shadda together' },
        { ar: 'ٱلشَّمْسُ', en: 'right: no mark on the lam' },
      ]}
    />
    <p class="lx-quote-note">The first form is made up, to show the mistake.</p>

The shadda on a sun letter is not decoration. In a text with all the marks it is always there, and it tells you the lam is swallowed. If you see a shadda on the first letter, do not say *al-*.

Two more points about the same mistake.

- **Transliteration.** This course writes the sound you hear: *ash-shamsu*, *ar-raḥmānu*. Some books write *al-shamsu* in English letters, but the Arabic spelling is the same either way.
- **Marks left out.** If a text has no marks, the shadda is missing too. That does not make it optional. You must know that ش is a sun letter.
</Block>

<Step id="writing" label="Writing it" title="How ٱلْ joins the word before it">
<Block id="wasla" title="The joining alif ٱ">
  <Stage label="Starting and joining">
    <Tiles
      label="ٱلْقَمَرُ alone, وَٱلْقَمَرِ joined, ٱلْأَرْضِ alone, وَٱلْأَرْضِ joined"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلْقَمَرُ', en: 'start here: al-qamaru' },
        { ar: 'وَٱلْقَمَرِ', en: 'joined: wal-qamari' },
        { ar: 'ٱلْأَرْضِ', en: 'start here: al-ʾarḍi' },
        { ar: 'وَٱلْأَرْضِ', en: 'joined: wal-ʾarḍi' },
      ]}
    />

The Quran does not write ٱلْ with a plain alif. It writes **ٱ**: an alif with a small mark on top. This alif is called **hamzat al-waṣl**, هَمْزَة الْوَصْل. *Waṣl* means “joining”.

The rule:

- If you **start** your speech at the word, say the alif: *al-qamaru*.
- If the word is **joined** to a word before it, skip the alif: *wa* + *l-qamari* is said *wal-qamari*.

The same happens before a sun letter. In 91:1, وَٱلشَّمْسِ is said *wash-shamsi*: the alif is skipped and the lam is swallowed.

In ٱلْأَرْضِ there are two alifs, and they act differently. The ٱ is a joining alif, and it is skipped. The hamza on the second alif (أ) is a **cutting hamza**, هَمْزَة قَطْع. You always say it, as a short stop before the *a*. So you say *wal-ʾarḍi*.

<Note kind="know">The joining alif ٱ also starts some verbs and nouns. In 54:1, ٱقْتَرَبَتِ begins with one, and so does ٱسْم, “name”. They follow the same rule: say it when you start there, skip it when you join.</Note>

Some little words are one letter and join the next word: **لِ** (to, for), **بِ** (with, in, by) and **كَ** (like). L2.07 teaches them in full.

When the next word starts with ٱلْ, the spelling changes a little. This matches what L2.07 says.

- **لِ + ٱلْ** loses the alif of ٱلْ in writing. لِ + ٱللَّهِ becomes **لِلَّهِ**, said *lillahi*. In the same way, لِ + ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ becomes لِلْمُتَّقِينَ.
- **بِ and كَ** keep it. You still see the ٱ in **بِٱلْحَقِّ** and in كَٱلْفَخَّارِ, but you do not say it: *bil-ḥaqqi*, *kal-fakhkhāri*.

The noun after one of these little words ends in *-i*. You see it in ٱللَّهِ and ٱلْحَقِّ. L1.10 taught that ending, and L2.07 explains it.

Look at 5:120. The first word, لِلَّهِ, is the little word لِ plus the name ٱللَّهِ. The alif of ٱللَّهِ is not written. The last word, وَٱلْأَرْضِ, has a joining alif after وَ, which you skip.

<Block id="mistake-every" title="Mistake 4: thinking every ٱل is the article">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="two words that start with ٱل"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلَّذِينَ', en: 'those who' },
        { ar: 'ٱللَّهِ', en: 'Allah' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Some words start with ٱل, but the Quranic Arabic Corpus does not split off an article from them.

- ٱلَّذِينَ means “those who”. It is one word, a kind of pronoun. L3.11 teaches these.
- ٱللَّهِ is the name of Allah. The Corpus treats it as one name.

So when you count or sort ٱل words into sun and moon, count the nouns that take the article, like ٱلشَّمْسِ and ٱلْقَمَرِ. Do not count these two.

Also remember what you learned in L1.04: in the Quran, ٱلْ is joined to nouns and to describing words. It is never joined to a verb.
</Block>

<Block id="five" title="The five kinds of definite noun">
  <Stage label="From al-Ajurrumiyyah">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote lx-quote-list" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>
  </Stage>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* is a short grammar book by Ibn Ājurrūm. In its chapter on the describing word (naʿt) it lists **five kinds of definite noun**, مَعْرِفَة:

| Kind | In the book | In the Quran | Taught in |
|---|---|---|---|
| A pronoun | أَنَا، أَنْتَ | هُوَ | L3.01 |
| A name | زَيْدٌ، مَكَّةَ | ٱللَّهُ | the lessons on case |
| A pointing word | هَذَا، هَذِهِ، هَؤُلَاءِ | ذَٰلِكَ | L3.10 |
| A noun with ٱلْ (**this lesson**) | الرَّجُلُ، الْغُلَامُ | ٱلْكِتَٰبِ | L1.06 |
| A noun joined to one of these four | any such noun | رَسُولِهِ | L2.08 |

The fourth kind is this lesson: the noun with the alif and the lam (*al-alif wal-lām*, the book’s own name for ٱلْ). The book also gives a quick test for the opposite, the indefinite noun, نَكِرَة.

<blockquote class="lx-quote" lang="ar" dir="rtl">
  <p>وَتَقْرِيبُهُ كُلُّ مَا صَلَحَ دُخُولُ الْأَلِفِ وَاللَّامِ عَلَيْهِ نَحْوُ الرَّجُلِ وَالْفَرَسِ</p>
</blockquote>
<p class="lx-quote-note"><i>Al-Ajurrumiyyah</i>. Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>

In plain English: “A simple test is this: any noun that can take ٱلْ in front of it is indefinite.” رَجُلٌ, “a man”, can take it (ٱلرَّجُلُ), so it is indefinite.

A noun can be definite **without** ٱلْ. A name like ٱللَّهُ is definite already. L2.08 shows how a noun can become definite by being joined to another.

  <Stage label="One word, six forms">

In L1.10 you learned the three case endings: nominative (*rafʿ*, -u), accusative (*naṣb*, -a) and genitive (*jarr*, -i). An indefinite noun has these with the extra *n*, which is tanwīn.

| Case | Indefinite (tanwīn) | Definite (ٱلْ) |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | يَوْمٌ *-un* | ٱلْيَوْمُ *-u* |
| Accusative | يَوْمًا *-an* | ٱلْيَوْمَ *-a* |
| Genitive | يَوْمٍ *-in* | ٱلْيَوْمِ *-i* |

Look at the verses you have seen. In 94:6, يُسْرًا is *-an* (an ease). In 103:2, below, خُسْرٍ is *-in* (a loss). In 5:120, قَدِيرٌۢ is *-un* (all-powerful).

<Note kind="warn">No tanwīn does not always mean definite. A name like مَكَّةَ is definite without ٱلْ and without tanwīn. And the first word of a possessive phrase loses its tanwīn too. L2.08 teaches that. Use ٱلْ as the sure sign for now.</Note>
</Block>

  <Stage>

ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ has ٱلْ, so it is definite. خُسْرٍ has tanwīn (*-in*), so it is indefinite.

But look at the meaning. ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ does not point to one person we both know. It means **human beings as a kind**. Al-Jadwal says exactly this about this verse: ٱلْ here covers the whole kind.

So ٱلْ does two jobs:

- It can point to **one known thing**: ٱلْحَقِّ, “the truth”, or ٱلْعُسْرِ, “the hardship” of 94:6.
- It can cover **a whole kind**: ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ, “human beings”.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal gives each job a name. The first, pointing to a known thing, is *ʿahdiyya*, عَهْدِيَّة. The second, covering the whole kind, is *jinsiyya*, جِنْسِيَّة. You do not need to learn these names yet.</Note>

  <Stage label="Sentence or phrase?">
    <Tiles

Whether a noun has ٱلْ decides how Arabic reads two words side by side.

- **Definite + indefinite** usually makes a sentence: ٱلْكِتَابُ جَدِيدٌ, “The book is new.” L2.01 teaches this.
- **Definite + definite** usually makes a phrase: ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلْجَدِيدُ, “the new book”. An adjective copies the ٱلْ of its noun. L2.09 teaches this.

You do not need these rules yet. They are the reason to watch for ٱلْ now.
</Block>

<Exercise n={1} q="Sun letter or moon letter, and how do you say each? (a) ٱلنَّاسِ (Al-Baqarah 2:8) (b) ٱلْغَيْبِ (Al-Imran 3:44) (c) ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ (Al-Baqarah 2:3). Meanings: “the people”, “the unseen”, “the prayer”.">

- (a) ٱلنَّاسِ: **sun letter** ن, with a shadda. Said *an-nāsi*.
- (b) ٱلْغَيْبِ: **moon letter** غ, with a sukūn on the lam. Said *al-ghaybi*.
- (c) ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ: **sun letter** ص, with a shadda. Said *aṣ-ṣalāta*.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Why does ٱلشَّمْسِ (Ash-Shams 91:1) have a shadda on the ش, and no sukūn on the lam? Meaning: “the sun”.">

ش is a **sun letter**, so the lam of ٱلْ is swallowed. The shadda on ش is the proof: the ش is said twice, once for itself and once for the lam.

The lam has no sukūn because it is not said. Compare ٱلْقَمَرِ in 91:2: ق is a moon letter, so the lam has a sukūn and you say it.

After وَ you also skip the joining alif. The full sound is *wash-shamsi*.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Make each noun definite and say it. These are made-up examples: (a) بَابٌ (a door) (b) نَهَارٌ (daytime) (c) شَجَرٌ (a tree).">

- (a) ٱلْبَابُ, *al-bābu*, “the door”. ب is a moon letter.
- (b) ٱلنَّهَارُ, *an-nahāru*, “the daytime”. ن is a sun letter, so it gets a shadda.
- (c) ٱلشَّجَرُ, *ash-shajaru*, “the tree”. ش is a sun letter, so it gets a shadda.

In all three the tanwīn is gone and the ending is *-u*.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="What does ٱ do in وَٱلْأَرْضِ (Al-Maidah 5:120), and what does the أ do? Meaning: “and the earth”.">

The ٱ is the **joining alif**. It comes after وَ, so you skip it. The أ is a **cutting hamza**. You always say it, as a short stop before the *a*.

The hamza is also a moon letter, so the lam is said. The whole word is *wal-ʾarḍi*.

</Exercise>
</Block>

      <li><b>Has tanwīn?</b> Then it is indefinite.</li>
      <li><b>After لِ?</b> The alif of ٱلْ is not written.</li>

<Exercise n={5} q="What happens to the tanwīn when you add ٱلْ? Show the change. These are made-up pairs: (a) رَجُلٌ (b) مَسْجِدٌ (c) يَوْمٌ.">

The tanwīn **disappears**. The rest of the ending stays, so *-un* becomes *-u*.

- (a) رَجُلٌ, *rajulun*, becomes ٱلرَّجُلُ, *ar-rajulu*. “A man” becomes “the man”. ر is a sun letter.
- (b) مَسْجِدٌ, *masjidun*, becomes ٱلْمَسْجِدُ, *al-masjidu*. “A mosque” becomes “the mosque”. م is a moon letter.
- (c) يَوْمٌ, *yawmun*, becomes ٱلْيَوْمُ, *al-yawmu*. “A day” becomes “the day”. ي is a moon letter.

A noun never has both ٱلْ and tanwīn.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Find the definite nouns in this part of a verse: وَلَا تُسْـَٔلُ عَنْ أَصْحَٰبِ ٱلْجَحِيمِ (Al-Baqarah 2:119, end). Meaning: “and you will not be asked about the people of the Blazing Fire.”">

ٱلْجَحِيمِ is **definite**: it has ٱلْ. ج is a moon letter, so you say *al-jaḥīmi*.

أَصْحَٰبِ, “people of”, has no ٱلْ and no tanwīn. It is the first word of a possessive phrase, so it takes its definiteness from ٱلْجَحِيمِ (L2.08). Do not count it as a word with ٱلْ.

وَلَا تُسْـَٔلُ is a verb, so it has no ٱلْ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Definite or indefinite? (a) ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ (Al-Maidah 5:120) (b) شَىْءٍ (Al-Maidah 5:120) (c) قَدِيرٌۢ (Al-Maidah 5:120) (d) ٱلْأَرْضِ (Al-Maidah 5:120).">

- (a) ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ: **definite**. It has ٱلْ.
- (b) شَىْءٍ: **indefinite**. It ends in tanwīn (*-in*).
- (c) قَدِيرٌۢ: **indefinite**. It ends in tanwīn (*-un*). The small م above the tanwīn is a reading sign for how the tanwīn is said before ب.
- (d) ٱلْأَرْضِ: **definite**. It has ٱلْ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Join the little word to the noun and write the result. (a) لِ + ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ (b) بِ + ٱلْيَوْمِ (c) كَ + ٱلْفَخَّارِ. The results are in Al-Aʿraf 7:128, Al-Baqarah 2:8 and Ar-Rahman 55:14.">

- (a) لِلْمُتَّقِينَ, “for the God-fearing”. After لِ the alif of ٱلْ is **not written**. Said *lil-muttaqīna*.
- (b) بِٱلْيَوْمِ, “in the day”. After بِ the ٱ is **written** and not said. Said *bil-yawmi*.
- (c) كَٱلْفَخَّارِ, “like pottery”. After كَ the ٱ is **written** and not said. Said *kal-fakhkhāri*.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Each form has a mistake. Say what it is and write the right form. These are made-up wrong forms: (a) ٱلْبَيْتٌ (b) ٱشَّمْسُ (c) ٱلْشَّمْسُ.">

- (a) ٱلْبَيْتٌ has ٱلْ **and** tanwīn. Drop the tanwīn: ٱلْبَيْتُ.
- (b) ٱشَّمْسُ has **no lam**. The lam is always written: ٱلشَّمْسُ.
- (c) ٱلْشَّمْسُ gives the lam a **sukūn** and also a shadda on ش. Before a sun letter the lam has no mark: ٱلشَّمْسُ.

</Exercise>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="al plus a noun gives the definite noun"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'ٱلْ', small: 'the' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'بَيْتٌ', small: 'a house' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'ٱلْبَيْتُ', small: 'the house' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- **ٱلْ** means “the”. It is joined to the front of a noun and makes it definite. Without it, the noun is “a …”.
- A noun with ٱلْ has **no tanwīn**: *-un* becomes *-u*, *-an* becomes *-a*, *-in* becomes *-i*.
- The lam of ٱلْ is written (before another ل, as one ل with a shadda). After the **14 moon letters** you say it: *al-qamaru*. After the **14 sun letters** you swallow it and say the letter twice: *ash-shamsu*.
- In a text with marks: a **sukūn** on the lam means “say it”. A **shadda** on the next letter means “skip it”.
- The ٱ is the **joining alif** (hamzat al-waṣl). Say it when you start there. Skip it when the word is joined: *wal-qamari*, *wash-shamsi*.
- After **لِ** the alif of ٱلْ is not written (**لِلَّهِ**). After **بِ** and **كَ** it is written but not said (**بِٱلْحَقِّ**).
- Not every ٱل is the article. ٱلَّذِينَ and ٱللَّهِ are single words in the Corpus.
- *Al-Ajurrumiyyah* lists five kinds of definite noun. The noun with ٱلْ is the fourth.
