This verse is about shares of an inheritance. It names two men and two women. Read it from right to left.

- رَجُلٌ is “a man”. ٱمْرَأَةٌ is “a woman”.
- أَخٌ is “a brother”. أُخْتٌ is “a sister”.

Look at the last two words. أَخٌ and أُخْتٌ start with the same letters. The word for the sister has an extra letter at the end: ت.

Arabic gives every noun a gender: **masculine** or **feminine**. In these four words the gender matches the meaning. The next block shows that it is not always so.

        { words: [{ ar: 'تَلَىٰهَا', en: 'it follows her', say: 'talaha', type: 'v' }] },
      ]}
      en={['By the moon, when it follows her.']}
    />

In English, only people and animals have a gender: *he*, *she*. A table, the sun or an idea is just *it*.

Arabic is different. **Every** noun has a gender, also things, places, times and ideas. These two verses show it.

- ٱلشَّمْسِ, “the sun”, is **feminine**. The ending هَا on وَضُحَىٰهَا means “her”. The Arabic speaks of the sun as “she”.
- ٱلْقَمَرِ, “the moon”, is **masculine**. The next verse says the moon follows “her”, the sun.

The Quranic Arabic Corpus tags ٱلشَّمْسِ as feminine and ٱلْقَمَرِ as masculine, and it tags the ending هَا in both verses as “her”.

<Note kind="remember">Gender is a label on the word. The sun is not a woman. The word ٱلشَّمْسُ is feminine.</Note>

</Block>

The grammar books call the two genders **مُذَكَّر** (*mudhakkar*, masculine) and **مُؤَنَّث** (*muʾannath*, feminine).

- **Masculine** is the plain gender. A masculine noun usually has no sign. Examples: رَجُلٌ (a man), كِتَٰبٌ (a book), بَيْتٌ (a house).
- **Feminine** is the marked gender. A feminine noun usually has a sign at the end. Examples: رَحْمَةٌ (mercy), جَنَّةٌ (a garden, Paradise).

English has a few pairs like this: *actor* and *actress*, *prince* and *princess*. The feminine word gets an ending. Arabic works in a similar way, but for every noun.

<Note kind="know">The grammar books talk about the gender of a **word**, not of the thing. ٱلشَّمْسُ has a gender, but the sun has no sex. This is why the course says “grammatical gender”.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="marbuta" title="The tāʾ marbūṭa: the main sign">
  <Stage>

        { ar: 'ٱلصَّلَوٰةُ', en: 'the prayer' },

The most common sign of a feminine word is the letter **ة**. It is called the **tāʾ marbūṭa**, تَاء مَرْبُوطَة, “tied tāʾ”. In 21:107 it ends رَحْمَةً, “a mercy”.

The test is short: **a noun that ends in ة is almost always feminine.** The Corpus tags رَحْمَةٌ, جَنَّةٌ and ٱلصَّلَوٰةُ as feminine.

The Quran writes ٱلصَّلَوٰةُ with a small alif above the wāw. You will not see the spelling صَلَاة in the Quran text.

“Almost always” is on purpose. Step 3 shows the few words that break the test.
</Block>

<Block id="sound" title="How the tāʾ marbūṭa looks and sounds">
  <Stage label="One word, three ways">
    <Tiles
      label="رَحْمَةٌ said in three ways"
      items={[
        { ar: 'رَحْمَةٌ', en: 'raḥmatun' },
        { ar: 'رَحْمَةُ', en: 'raḥmatu' },
        { ar: 'رَحْمَة', en: 'raḥmah (stopping)' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

**Look.** ة looks like a small round ه with two dots on top. It joins to the letter before it: ـة.

**Sound.** It changes with its place in the sentence.

- When the word carries an ending, the ة is said **-t**: رَحْمَةٌ is *raḥmatun*, رَحْمَةُ is *raḥmatu*.
- When you stop on the word, the ة is said as a soft **-h**, or it is silent: *raḥmah*.

Because the ة is a **t** before an ending, the nominative is -atun or -atu and the accusative is -atan. L2.05 shows the spelling of رَحْمَةً: there is no alif after the tanwīn when the word ends in ة.

<Note kind="tip">Say the word with its ending, not only the stopping form. You hear the -t, and then you remember it is a tāʾ.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="ordinary-ta" title="Do not mix it up with an ordinary tāʾ">
  <Stage label="ة and ت">
    <Tiles
      label="Two words ending in an ordinary ت, and one in ة"
      items={[
        { ar: 'أُخْتٌ', en: 'a sister (feminine)' },
        { ar: 'بَيْتٌ', en: 'a house (masculine)' },
        { ar: 'رَحْمَةٌ', en: 'mercy (feminine)' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The letter **ت** is an ordinary tāʾ. It has no round shape. The **ة** looks like a small ه with two dots. When you stop on the word, the ت stays **t** (*ukht*, *bayt*), and the ة turns into **h**.

An ordinary ت says nothing about gender.

- أُخْتٌ ends in ت and is feminine, because it means a sister. The Corpus tags it feminine.
- بَيْتٌ ends in ت and is masculine. The Corpus tags it masculine.

<Note kind="warn">Only the **ة** (tāʾ marbūṭa) is a feminine sign. Check the shape of the letter before you decide.</Note>

</Block>

<Block id="pairs" title="From masculine to feminine: add ـَة">
  <Stage label="Pairs of describing words">
    <Tiles
      label="Two pairs: masculine and feminine"
      items={[
        { ar: 'مُؤْمِنٌ', en: 'believing (m.)' },

A word that describes a noun, an **adjective**, makes its feminine by adding **ـَة**: مُؤْمِنٌ becomes مُؤْمِنَةٌ. The Quran has both forms of مُؤْمِن in 4:92, and ظَالِمَةٌ in 22:45.

For nouns that name a person, the feminine is often a different word, as you saw: رَجُلٌ and ٱمْرَأَةٌ, أَخٌ and أُخْتٌ.

L2.09 teaches how an adjective follows its noun. Here you only need the idea: the feminine adds a sign to the masculine.

</Block>

There are two more signs of a feminine word. Both are a long **ā** at the end.

- **A long ā by itself**, أَلِف مَقْصُورَة (*alif maqṣūra*). It is written ىٰ in ٱلْأُولَىٰ (93:4) and with a plain alif in ٱلدُّنْيَا (87:16). The spelling differs, but it is the same ending: a long ā.
- **A long ā and a hamza**, أَلِف مَمْدُودَة (*alif mamdūda*): ـَاء. In 3:118, ٱلْبَغْضَآءُ ends this way.

The Corpus tags all three words as feminine.

In 93:4 there are two signs in one verse: ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةُ has the ة, and ٱلْأُولَىٰ has the long ā.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal says of ٱلْبَغْضَآءُ that its pattern is فَعْلَاء, the same pattern as السَّرَّاء and الضَّرَّاء. In 3:118 it is the doer of a feminine verb. You will see that in Step 5.</Note>

A word with a sign is **usually** feminine. A word with no sign is **usually** masculine. “Usually” matters, because the next step is about the exceptions.

Some words are feminine and have no sign. You have to learn them.

- **أَرْضٌ** (earth): 2:30, in the next step.
- **شَمْسٌ** (sun): 91:1, which you saw in Step 1.
- **نَارٌ** (fire): 2:24, in Step 5.
- **أُمٌّ** (mother): feminine because of its meaning.
- **نَفْسٌ**, **يَدٌ** and **عَيْنٌ**: a self, a hand and an eye.

The Corpus tags every word in this list as feminine. A hand and an eye are body parts that come in pairs, and both are feminine here. That is a hint for memory, not a rule.

<Block id="two-surprises" title="Two surprises in one phrase">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:30 (part)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'إِنِّى', en: 'indeed I', say: 'inni', type: 'p' }] },

This phrase has two words that surprise you.

- **ٱلْأَرْضِ**, “the earth”, has no sign and is **feminine**. The Corpus tags it feminine.
- **خَلِيفَةً**, “a successor”, ends in ة and is **masculine**. The Corpus tags every use of it in the Quran as masculine.

Why does a masculine word end in ة? Al-Jadwal says the pattern of خَلِيفَة is فَعِيلَة, with the same meaning as فَاعِل (“one who takes over”), and the ة was added to make the meaning stronger.

<Note kind="warn">So the tāʾ marbūṭa test is “almost always”, not “always”. A ة points to feminine. The word decides.</Note>

Look at the two nouns in the verse. Both end in **-āʾ**.

- ٱلسَّمَآءِ, “the sky”, is **feminine**.

So the ending ـَاء helps, but it does not prove anything. The same is true of the long ā. مَوْلَىٰ, “protector”, as in 3:150, ends in ىٰ, and it is masculine. هُدًى, “guidance”, is masculine too. The Corpus gives the tags for all four.

A sign is a hint. It is strong, but it can be wrong.

ٱلنَّارَ, “the Fire”, has no sign. How do you know it is feminine?

The word next to it tells you. ٱلَّتِى means “which”, and it is the feminine form. The masculine form is ٱلَّذِى. The word “which” has to match the noun before it.

This is the third way to know. When a noun has no sign, look at the words around it. The next step shows what they are.

<Block id="adjective" title="An adjective follows the noun">
  <Stage label="Same gender">
    <Sentence
      reference="Al-Haqqah 69:22"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'فِى', en: 'in', say: 'fi', type: 'p' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'جَنَّةٍ', en: 'a garden (f.)', say: 'jannatin' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'عَالِيَةٍ', en: 'high (f.)', say: 'aliyatin' }] },

    <Sentence

The first reason to know the gender is the **adjective**. It must match its noun.

- جَنَّةٍ is feminine, so its adjective is feminine too: عَالِيَةٍ, with ـَة.
- مَآءً is masculine, so its adjective is masculine: طَهُورًا, with no ـَة.

The Corpus tags both adjectives the same way as their nouns. L2.09 teaches this matching in full: gender, number, case and definiteness.

The second reason is the **verb**. A verb that comes before its doer matches the doer in gender.

- In 110:1 the doer is نَصْرُ, “help”, which is masculine. The verb جَآءَ has no ت. The Corpus tags نَصْرُ and ٱلْفَتْحُ as masculine.
- In 3:118 the doer is ٱلْبَغْضَآءُ, which is feminine. The verb بَدَتِ ends in **ت**.

Compare two made-up sentences: جَآءَ ٱلرَّجُلُ, “the man came”, and جَآءَتِ ٱلْمَرْأَةُ, “the woman came”. The feminine verb adds ت. L2.03 teaches verbs with a doer, and L3.03 teaches the past tense.

Two more words follow the gender.

- **The khabar** (“the news”, the second part of a sentence like these) matches the **mubtadaʾ** (“the starting point”). A feminine mubtadaʾ gives a khabar with ـَة. L2.02 teaches this.
- **Pronouns** (words like *he*, *she*, *her*). هُوَ is “he” and هِيَ is “she”, for masculine and feminine nouns. Attached endings do the same: هَا is “her” in ضُحَىٰهَا (91:1) and in وَقُودُهَا (2:24). L3.08 and L3.09 teach pronouns.

<Note kind="remember">Gender is fixed for each noun. You do not decide it. You learn it once, and the words around the noun show it every time.</Note>

<Block id="mistake-english" title="Mistake 1: using English to decide">
  <Stage label="Sun and moon">
    <Tiles
      label="ٱلشَّمْسُ feminine, ٱلْقَمَرُ masculine"
      items={[

In English the sun is “it”. In some languages the sun is masculine. A learner often carries that over.

In Arabic the sun is feminine and the moon is masculine. 91:1 and 91:2 say so with the endings هَا. Do not use your own language or the meaning to decide. Trust the Arabic word.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-nosign" title="Mistake 2: “no sign means masculine”">
  <Stage label="Feminine, no sign">
    <Tiles
      label="Four feminine words with no ة"

A word with no feminine sign is usually masculine, but not always. أَرْضٌ, شَمْسٌ, نَارٌ and أُخْتٌ have no ة and are feminine.

Before you call a word masculine, ask two questions. Is it one of the words you have learned in Step 3? Do the words around it (the adjective, the verb) show feminine?
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-ta" title="Mistake 3: mixing up ة and ت">
  <Stage label="Two different letters">
    <Tiles
      label="أُخْتٌ and بَيْتٌ end in ت, رَحْمَةٌ ends in ة"
      items={[
        { ar: 'بَيْتٌ', en: 'house' },
        { ar: 'أُخْتٌ', en: 'sister' },
        { ar: 'رَحْمَةٌ', en: 'mercy' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Both letters can look alike in a fast reading. They are not the same.

- **ة** is a sign of a feminine word. It becomes *h* when you stop.
- **ت** is an ordinary letter. It stays *t*. بَيْتٌ is masculine, and أُخْتٌ is feminine for another reason: its meaning.

If you see ت at the end, do not call the word feminine because of it.

<Check n={11} q="بَيْتٌ ends in ت. Is it feminine?" options={['Yes, it ends in a feminine sign', 'No. The ت is an ordinary tāʾ, and the word is masculine.']} answer={1} why="Only ة is a sign. The Corpus tags بَيْتٌ masculine." />
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-looks" title="Mistake 4: “it has the sign, so it must be feminine”">
  <Stage label="Sign, but masculine">
    <Tiles
      label="Three masculine words with a feminine-looking ending"
      items={[
        { ar: 'خَلِيفَةً', en: 'a successor (ة)' },
        { ar: 'مَآءً', en: 'water (-āʾ)' },
        { ar: 'مَوْلَىٰ', en: 'protector (long ā)' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The reverse mistake is as common. A feminine-looking ending does not make a word feminine.

- خَلِيفَةً ends in ة and is masculine (2:30).
- مَآءً ends in ـَاء and is masculine (25:48).
- مَوْلَىٰ ends in a long ā and is masculine (3:150).

The old rule “a word with a sign is almost always feminine” still helps. It is a first guess, and the words around the noun check it.

1. رَحْمَةٌ, “mercy”: **feminine**. It has ة.

4. رَجُلٌ, “a man”: **masculine**. No sign.
5. جَنَّةٌ, “a garden”: **feminine**. It has ة.
6. بَيْتٌ, “a house”: **masculine**. Its ت is an ordinary tāʾ, not a ة.

The Corpus tags all six this way.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Which of these end in a tāʾ marbūṭa (ة)? (1) مَدِينَةٌ (2) بَيْتٌ (3) أُخْتٌ (4) ٱلْفَتْحُ (5) رَحْمَةٌ. Challenge: are all of the words with a ة feminine?">

- (1) مَدِينَةٌ, “a city”: **yes**, ة. Feminine.
- (2) بَيْتٌ: **no**, an ordinary ت. Masculine.
- (3) أُخْتٌ: **no**, an ordinary ت. But it is feminine, because it means a sister.
- (4) ٱلْفَتْحُ, “the victory”: **no**. Masculine.
- (5) رَحْمَةٌ: **yes**. Feminine.

**Challenge:** the words with a ة here, (1) and (5), are feminine. Remember that not every word with a ة is: خَلِيفَةٌ is masculine. And not every feminine word has a ة: أُخْتٌ does not.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="These four words are feminine and have no ة. What does each mean? (1) أُمٌّ (2) شَمْسٌ (3) أَرْضٌ (4) نَارٌ. Which verse in this lesson shows (2), (3) and (4)?">

1. أُمٌّ: **mother**. It is feminine because of its meaning.
2. شَمْسٌ: **sun**. Ash-Shams 91:1.
3. أَرْضٌ: **earth, land**. Al-Baqarah 2:30.

They have to be learned. Learn each with the verse that shows it.

<Exercise n={5} q="Say the gender of every noun in إِنِّى جَاعِلٌ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ خَلِيفَةً (Al-Baqarah 2:30, part). Which one surprises you?">

- **جَاعِلٌ**, “one who puts”: **masculine**. No sign.
- **ٱلْأَرْضِ**, “the earth”: **feminine**, with no sign. This one is a surprise, and you learn it.

<Exercise n={6} q="Both ٱلسَّمَآءِ and مَآءً end in -āʾ in وَأَنزَلْنَا مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءً طَهُورًا (Al-Furqan 25:48, part). Which one is feminine? What in the verse backs it up for the other one?">

ٱلسَّمَآءِ, “the sky”, is **feminine**. مَآءً, “water”, is **masculine**.

The adjective after مَآءً is طَهُورًا, “pure”, with no ـَة. It matches a masculine noun. The ending -āʾ alone could not tell you.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Name the feminine sign in each: (1) رَحْمَةً (2) ٱلْأُولَىٰ (3) ٱلْبَغْضَآءُ (4) ٱلدُّنْيَا.">

1. رَحْمَةً (21:107): the **tāʾ marbūṭa**, ة.
2. ٱلْأُولَىٰ (93:4): the **alif maqṣūra**, a long ā written ىٰ.
3. ٱلْبَغْضَآءُ (3:118): the **alif mamdūda**, a long ā and a hamza.
4. ٱلدُّنْيَا (87:16): the **alif maqṣūra**, a long ā written with an alif.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Choose the right word. (a) ___ ٱلشَّمْسُ: طَلَعَ or طَلَعَتْ? (b) ___ ٱلْقَمَرُ: طَلَعَ or طَلَعَتْ? (c) ٱلْأَرْضُ ___: وَاسِعٌ or وَاسِعَةٌ? These are made-up examples. Hint: think about the gender of each noun.">

- (a) **طَلَعَتِ** ٱلشَّمْسُ, “the sun rose”. The sun is feminine, so the verb takes ت.
- (b) **طَلَعَ** ٱلْقَمَرُ, “the moon rose”. The moon is masculine: no ت.
- (c) ٱلْأَرْضُ **وَاسِعَةٌ**, “the earth is wide”. ٱلْأَرْضُ is feminine with no sign, so the khabar has ـَةٌ.

In (a) and (b) the verb comes first, so it is next to its doer. L2.03 teaches the verbal sentence.

- Every Arabic noun is **masculine** (مُذَكَّر) or **feminine** (مُؤَنَّث). Things have a gender too.
- The main feminine sign is the **tāʾ marbūṭa**, ة. Two more signs are a long ā (**alif maqṣūra**) and a long ā with a hamza (**alif mamdūda**). An ordinary ت is not a sign.
- Some feminine words have no sign: أَرْض, شَمْس, نَار, نَفْس, يَد, عَيْن, أُمّ.
- Some masculine words look feminine: خَلِيفَة, مَآء, مَوْلَىٰ.
- A sign is a good first guess. The words around the noun settle it: its adjective, its verb, its pronoun.
- Gender is fixed for each noun. You learn it with the word.
