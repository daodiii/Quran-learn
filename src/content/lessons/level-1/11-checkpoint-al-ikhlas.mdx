Read each verse from right to left. The English under each verse is our own plain meaning.

Count the written words: four, two, four and five. That makes **fifteen words**, as the Quranic Arabic Corpus counts them. The last word has a small ۢ above its tanween. It is a reading sign for how the verse joins what comes next. It does not change the case: the ending is still *-un*.

Two reading points to watch.

- In verse 1 the alif of ٱللَّهُ is silent after هُوَ. You say *huwa llāhu*, not *huwa allāhu*.
- In verse 4 the نْ of يَكُن has no sukūn written, and the lām of لَّهُۥ has a shadda. Together they tell you the nūn blends into the lām: *yakul-lahū*. This is a reading rule. It changes nothing in the grammar.

Arabic writes some little words onto the next word. The grammar books count each piece on its own.

- وَلَمْ is two pieces: وَ (*wa*, “and”) and لَمْ (*lam*, “not”). It comes twice, in verses 3 and 4.
- لَّهُۥ is two pieces: لِ (*li*, “to”) and هُ (*hu*, “him”). It comes once, in verse 4.

So the surah has 15 written words and **18 pieces**: 15 + 3. The next steps name each piece.

Verse 1 has two layers.

- **قُلْ** is a verb (L1.04): a command, “Say!”. It starts a verbal sentence (L1.09).
- **هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ** is what is said. It starts with a noun, so it is a nominal sentence (L1.09). Its mubtadaʾ is هُوَ. Its khabar is the whole sentence ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ.

Look inside the khabar. It is a nominal sentence too: ٱللَّهُ is its mubtadaʾ and أَحَدٌ is its khabar. So a nominal sentence can sit inside the khabar of another one. L2.01 and L2.02 teach this in full.

      items={[

هُوَ is a pronoun. L1.04 counted pronouns as nouns. It is masculine, singular and third person (L1.07, L1.08). A pronoun never changes its ending, so you cannot see its case. The grammar books still give it one, by its job: here it is the mubtadaʾ, so it is nominative. They say it is nominative “in the place of” the nominative.

<Note kind="know">**Two ways to read هُوَ.** Al-Jadwal reads هُوَ as the mubtadaʾ, with the sentence ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ as its khabar. Al-Durr al-Maṣūn explains that هُوَ itself can be understood in two ways. (1) It points back to the One the listeners asked about. The reports say people asked the Prophet to describe his Lord. (2) It is a **ḍamīr al-shaʾn**, a pronoun of the matter. It means “the matter is this”, and the sentence after it explains it. Al-Durr says this suits a place of great honour. With the first way, it also allows other readings of ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ: Allah as the first khabar and أَحَدٌ as the second, or Allah as a replacement word for هُوَ. Al-Jadwal records the first-khabar reading in a footnote. This lesson follows al-Jadwal's main reading. In every reading, ٱللَّهُ and أَحَدٌ are nominative. The ending you see does not change.</Note>

The ـٌ at the end of أَحَدٌ says two things at once.

1. The word is **indefinite**. It has no ٱل (L1.06).
2. The word is **nominative**, because tanween with a ḍamma is the nominative ending (L1.10).

The word is also masculine and singular (L1.07, L1.08). Compare ٱللَّهُ in the same verse. It is a name, so it is definite. It takes one ḍamma and no tanween (L1.06).

<Note kind="know">**The root and the meaning.** The Quranic Arabic Corpus lists the root of أَحَد as أ-ح-د. Al-Durr al-Maṣūn says its hamza is a replacement for a wāw, as the word comes from *waḥda* (oneness). That is the usual view, and it gives the root و-ح-د (L1.05). It also records one scholar who says the hamza is original. Al-Durr reports that Thaʿlab said *wāḥid* can be counted and *aḥad* cannot, and that another scholar answered with *aḥad wa-ʿishrūn* (“twenty-one”). Al-Jadwal says the name *aḥad* points to a being that no one shares, and *ṣamad* points to the One everyone turns to. Between them they cover every quality of perfection.</Note>

The Arabic word order is: *not, is, to Him, equal, anyone*. English says it as “no one is equal to Him”.

The verb is **يَكُن**, a form of كَانَ, “to be”. A sentence with كَانَ has two parts, like a nominal sentence, but it changes the second part's ending (see the next block).

- **أَحَدٌۢ** is the first part. It is the “someone” that the verse denies. With “not”, it means “no one”.
- **كُفُوًا** is the second part: “equal”.
- **لَّهُۥ** says to whom: “to Him”. It belongs to كُفُوًا.

The first word of the verse is وَ, “and”. It joins this verse to verse 3.

Al-Jadwal also explains the order. The verse is about denying that anything equals Him, so the phrase that holds “Him” comes first, as the most important part.

<Block id="v4-accusative" title="Why does كُفُوًا end in -an?">
  <Stage label="Two parts, two endings">
    <Tiles
      label="the first part stays -un, the second part becomes -an"
      items={[
        { ar: 'أَحَدٌۢ', en: 'first part: -un' },
        { ar: 'كُفُوًا', en: 'second part: -an' },

In verse 1, both parts of ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ ended in the nominative, *-u* and *-un*. Verse 4 is different.

- **أَحَدٌۢ** is nominative: *-un*. The tanween ـٌ shows it, as in verse 1 (L1.10).
- **كُفُوًا** is **accusative**: *-an*. The tanween ـً shows it.

The reason is the verb كَانَ. In a sentence with كَانَ, the first part stays nominative and the second part is accusative. L1.10 said this was coming. L2.11 teaches it in full.

Nothing is done to كُفُوًا here. It is not an object. The accusative has other jobs than the object (see Mistake 2).

<Block id="v4-lahu" title="لَّهُۥ: a little word and a pronoun">
  <Stage label="Two pieces, one written word">
    <Tiles
      label="li, a preposition, and hu, a pronoun after it"
      items={[
        { ar: 'لِ', en: 'to, for', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'هُ', en: 'him: genitive' },
      ]}
    />

لَّهُۥ is two pieces (see the block on pieces).

- **لِ** is a preposition, a little word (L1.04): “to” or “for”.
- **هُ** is a pronoun, a noun, masculine and singular: “him”. After a preposition a word is **genitive** (L1.10). A pronoun shows no ending, so it is genitive “by place”. The small wāw above the هُ is only a long *-ū* for reading (L1.02).

Al-Jadwal says the phrase لَّهُۥ is tied to the khabar, كُفُوًا. L2.07 teaches what “tied” means.

<Note kind="know">**Is the khabar of يَكُن لَّهُۥ or كُفُوًا?** Al-Jadwal and the first view in al-Durr al-Maṣūn say كُفُوًا is the khabar of يَكُن, أَحَدٌۢ is its first part, and لَّهُۥ is tied to كُفُوًا. The meaning: “no one was equal to Him”. Al-Durr also gives a second view, from Makkī and Abū al-Baqāʾ. Here لَّهُۥ is the khabar, and كُفُوًا is a **ḥāl**, a word that describes a state, taken from أَحَدٌۢ. A describing word that is moved in front of its noun can become a ḥāl. Abū Ḥayyān rejected this second view. He said لَّهُۥ cannot be a khabar here, as the sentence would not be complete. Al-Samīn al-Ḥalabī answers him in al-Durr. This lesson follows the first view. In both, كُفُوًا is accusative and أَحَدٌۢ is nominative. Only the reason for the -an differs: khabar of يَكُن in the first view, ḥāl in the second. L4.01 teaches the ḥāl.</Note>
</Block>

The ending tells you the case. It does not tell you the job. Ask what the word does in its sentence (L1.09, L1.10). L2.04 teaches the jobs of the nominative.

The simple picture of the accusative is “the object”: the word a verb is done to, like ٱلْكِتَابَ in a sentence about reading a book. That is one of its jobs.

In verse 4, كُفُوًا is accusative, and nothing is done to it. Nobody “equals” anything. It is the second part of a كَانَ sentence.

So do not decide a job from the case alone. Look at the sentence. L2.05 teaches the jobs of the accusative.
</Block>

<Block id="m4" title="Mistake 4: forgetting that a pronoun has a case">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="two pronouns with no ending you can see, and a case each"
      items={[
        { ar: 'هُوَ', en: 'nominative, by place' },
        { ar: 'هُ', en: 'genitive, by place' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

You can see no ending on هُوَ or on the هُ in لَّهُۥ. A pronoun never changes its ending. That does not mean it has no case.

- هُوَ is the mubtadaʾ in verse 1, so it is nominative.
- هُ comes after لِ in verse 4, so it is genitive.

The grammar books say “in the place of” the nominative or the genitive. A word that never changes, like a pronoun, still has a case. It just does not show it.

<Exercise n={2} q="Which words in the surah have tanween? List them and say what each tanween tells you.">

Three words have tanween. They are two different nouns.

1. **أَحَدٌ** (verse 1): ـٌ. Indefinite and nominative.
2. **كُفُوًا** (verse 4): ـً. Indefinite and accusative.
3. **أَحَدٌۢ** (verse 4): ـٌ with the small ۢ. Indefinite and nominative. The small sign is only for reading.

Tanween tells you two things at once: the word is indefinite, and the mark shows the case (ـٌ nominative, ـً accusative, ـٍ genitive). The definite words, ٱللَّهُ and ٱلصَّمَدُ, have one vowel and no tanween (L1.06).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Find ٱللَّهُ each time it comes in the surah. What ending does it have, and what does that tell you?">

**ٱللَّهُ** comes **twice**: in verse 1 (قُلْ هُوَ **ٱللَّهُ** أَحَدٌ) and in verse 2 (**ٱللَّهُ** ٱلصَّمَدُ).

Both times it ends in a single ḍamma, *-u*.

- The ḍamma is the **nominative** ending (L1.10).
- The ḍamma is single, not tanween, because ٱللَّهُ is a name and so **definite** (L1.06).

Its job is the same both times: it starts a nominal sentence, as the mubtadaʾ. In verse 1 that sentence is inside the khabar of هُوَ. In verse 2 it is the whole verse.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Give the root of each word: أَحَدٌ, يَلِدْ, يُولَدْ and ٱلصَّمَدُ. Which words share a root, and which Arabic word for “one” shares the root of أَحَدٌ?">

- **يَلِدْ** and **يُولَدْ** share the root **و-ل-د** (birth). One is active (“he fathers”) and one is passive (“he is born”) (L1.05).
- **ٱلصَّمَدُ** has the root **ص-م-د**.
- **أَحَدٌ**: the Corpus lists **أ-ح-د**. Al-Durr al-Maṣūn gives the usual view that the hamza replaces a wāw, so the root is **و-ح-د**, the root of **وَاحِد**, “one”. Another scholar says the hamza is original, so scholars differ on this root.

Both words mean “one”, but Thaʿlab's rule was that *wāḥid* can be counted and *aḥad* cannot. Another scholar answered with *aḥad wa-ʿishrūn* (“twenty-one”), so the difference is not fixed. Level 3 teaches roots in full (L3.03).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Read this verse aloud and write it in English letters: وَلَمْ يَكُن لَّهُۥ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌۢ (Al-Ikhlas 112:4).">

**wa-lam yakul-lahū kufuwan aḥadun**

- وَلَمْ: *wa-lam*. Two pieces, said together.
- يَكُن لَّهُۥ: *yakul-lahū*. The shadda on the lām shows that the nūn blends into it, so you say *yakul-lahū*, not *yakun lahū*.
- كُفُوًا: *kufuwan*. The tanween fatḥa is the *-an*.
- أَحَدٌۢ: *aḥadun*. The tanween ḍamma is the *-un*.

If you wrote *yakun lahū*, you read the letters but missed the shadda (L1.02).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Find any feminine or plural word in the surah. If there is none, say what the gender and number of the words are.">

There is no feminine word and no plural word in the surah (L1.07, L1.08).

- All 8 nouns are masculine and singular, including the two pronouns هُوَ and هُ.
- The verbs يَلِدْ, يُولَدْ and يَكُن are “he” forms: masculine and singular. The command قُلْ is addressed to one person.
- The grammar is the same throughout. That is why no word in the surah needs a different ending for gender or number.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Use your Level 1 tools on a new verse: وَإِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَٰحِدٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:163, start). Meaning: “Your God is one God.” For each of the three words, give its type, whether it is definite, its gender and number, and its case and mark. Which word is the mubtadaʾ?">

| Word | Type | Details | Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| وَإِلَٰهُكُمْ | Little word وَ + noun + pronoun -kum | Definite by its joined pronoun “your” (plural), masculine, singular | Nominative, -u |
| إِلَٰهٌ | Noun | Indefinite, masculine, singular | Nominative, -un |
| وَٰحِدٌ | Describing word | Indefinite, masculine, singular | Nominative, -un |

The word at the start, **إِلَٰهُكُمْ**, is the mubtadaʾ. The word **إِلَٰهٌ** is the khabar. **وَٰحِدٌ** describes إِلَٰهٌ and copies its case (L2.09). Al-Jadwal reads it this way. The little word وَ opens the sentence, and the pronoun -كُمْ (“your”) is joined to the noun (L3.02). All three words are nominative, so the ending is a ḍamma or tanween ḍamma.

</Exercise>

- Surah Al-Ikhlas has **four verses, fifteen written words and eighteen pieces**: eight nouns, four verbs and six little words.
- **Verses 1 and 2** are nominal sentences. In verse 1 a nominal sentence sits inside the khabar of هُوَ. **Verse 3** is made of verbs and little words only. **Verse 4** has a كَانَ sentence.
- All three cases appear. Nominative: ٱللَّهُ, أَحَدٌ, ٱلصَّمَدُ and هُوَ. Accusative: كُفُوًا. Genitive: the هُ after لِ. A pronoun has its case by place.
- Every noun is masculine and singular. Every verb is a “he” form, and the command is for one person.
- Two points have more than one reading. **هُوَ** in verse 1: al-Jadwal reads it as the mubtadaʾ, and al-Durr also gives the pronoun of the matter. **The khabar of يَكُن** in verse 4: كُفُوًا in al-Jadwal, لَّهُۥ in a second view. In both, the endings you see are the same.
