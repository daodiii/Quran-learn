This is the prayer of Ibrahim and Ismail while they raise the base of the House. It has three parts.

The first part is **رَبَّنَا**, “our Lord”. It is the call: the one who is spoken to. The second part is **تَقَبَّلْ مِنَّا**, “accept from us”. It is the request. The third part is إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلسَّمِيعُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ, “indeed You are the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing”. It gives a reason.

The grammar of every part is already in earlier lessons. This lesson looks at how the parts come together, how often, and where the shape breaks.

Al-Jadwal names each part in this verse.

| Part | In 2:127 | What al-Jadwal says |
|---|---|---|
| The call, مُنَادَى | رَبَّنَا | The one who is called. It is the first word of an idafah and it is accusative. The little word for “O” is left out. |
| The request | تَقَبَّلْ مِنَّآ | A command that is a prayer (أَمْر دُعَائِيّ). The sentence is the answer of the call, جَوَاب النِّدَاء, and it has no place of its own. |
| The reason, تَعْلِيلِيَّة | إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلسَّمِيعُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ | A sentence that explains. It has no place of its own. |

The labels above are al-Jadwal’s. The idea that a prayer has **these three parts** is a teaching summary made for this lesson. No grammar book in this project says it. The next blocks count how often the parts come and show verses where one is missing.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal gives two readings of أَنتَ in this verse. It can be a pronoun that stands between the two parts (ḍamīr faṣl) so that ٱلسَّمِيعُ is the khabar of inna, or it can be a mubtadaʾ of its own. This lesson follows the first.</Note>

Ibrahim speaks from 14:35 to 14:41. Read the verses in order and look at what comes right after each call.

| Verse | Call | Comes next | What the verse does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14:35 | رَبِّ | ٱجْعَلْ | A request: make this land safe, and keep me and my sons from idols. |
| 14:36 | رَبِّ | إِنَّهُنَّ | A reason, then two conditions with مَن. No command. |
| 14:37 | رَّبَّنَآ … رَبَّنَا | إِنِّىٓ … لِيُقِيمُوا۟ | A statement, a second call, then two requests: فَٱجْعَلْ and وَٱرْزُقْهُم. |
| 14:38 | رَبَّنَآ | إِنَّكَ | A statement of what Allah knows. No request. |
| 14:39 | none | ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ | Praise, spoken to Allah with no call. |
| 14:40 | رَبِّ … رَبَّنَا | ٱجْعَلْنِى … وَتَقَبَّلْ | Two requests, with a call before each. |
| 14:41 | رَبَّنَا | ٱغْفِرْ | A request for himself, his parents and the believers. |

There are **eight calls** in six verses: three رَبِّ and five رَبَّنَا. One speaker says both forms. Only 14:35, 14:40 and 14:41 start a command straight after the call.

Al-Jadwal calls the second call in 14:37 an interjected sentence that stresses the prayer, and the second call in 14:40 an interjected prayer. A second call is read as stressing the first, not as a new prayer.

In Arabic you call someone with the little word يَا, “O”. The one who is called is a **munādā** (مُنَادَى). Many prayers of the Quran open with one: رَبَّنَا or رَبِّ.

In the prayers of this lesson the word for “O” is not written. Al-Jadwal says in 2:127, 3:8 and 7:23 that it is left out before رَبَّنَا. In 14:35 he puts it in brackets in front of رَبِّ. The Quran writes it twice: يَٰرَبِّ in 25:30 and in 43:88.

Al-Jadwal explains the case of a called word in his note on 14:35. A single name, and an unnamed word that points at one person, are built on a ḍamma, as in يَٰمُوسَىٰ (L3.17) and يَٰجِبَالُ (L4.09). The other kinds are accusative. One of them is the **muḍāf**, the first word of an idafah. رَبَّنَا is a muḍāf: رَبَّ and نَا. So it is accusative.

<Check n={4} q="Why is رَبَّنَا accusative?" options={['It is the first word of an idafah and it is called', 'It is the object of a verb', 'It stands after a preposition']} answer={0} why="Al-Jadwal: a called word that is a muḍāf is accusative. نَا is the second word of the idafah." />
</Block>

There are two calls to Allah in these prayers.

- **رَبَّنَا** is رَبَّ and نَا, “our Lord”. The fatḥa on the ب shows the accusative. نَا is a joined pronoun (L3.02) and the second word of the idafah.
- **رَبِّ** is رَبِّ and the pronoun “my”. The pronoun is a yāʾ, and it is dropped. The full form would be رَبِّي. Al-Jadwal says the accusative fatḥa is on the letter before the dropped yāʾ, so you cannot see it. L3.13 shows the same in 23:29.

Quran Learn counted both calls in the Corpus file with a script. A word counts as a call when its lemma is رَبّ, it has the pronoun “my” and no written yāʾ (رَبِّ), or it has the pronoun “our” and a fatḥa on the ب (رَبَّنَا), and it is not the object or the subject of a sentence.

| Call | Words | Of which with a written يَا |
|---|---|---|
| رَبِّ | 67 | 2 (25:30 and 43:88) |
| رَبَّنَا | 70 | 0 |
| Both | 137, in 127 verses | 2 |

<Note kind="know">The Corpus tags 28 of the 67 calls رَبِّ as accusative and 39 as nominative. Al-Jadwal reads the call as accusative wherever his pages for the verses of this lesson analyse it (14:35, 14:37, 2:127, 3:8, 7:23, 20:25, and 2:200 for 2:201). The 39 nominative tags are errors. The Corpus tags all 70 calls رَبَّنَا as accusative. Two words of the form رَبَّنَا are not calls and were left out of the count: إِنَّ رَبَّنَا in 35:34 (the subject of inna) and نَرَىٰ رَبَّنَا in 25:21 (the object of “see”).</Note>

So 61 of 137 calls, a little under half, are followed at once by a command. This is a count of the first word. A command can come later in the verse, and the table does not say what is asked.

The request of a prayer is often a command, and it is said to Allah. It has the form you learned in L3.08. It is built on a sukūn or by dropping a weak letter, and its doer is a hidden “you”.

Quran Learn counted the commands that end in a joined نَا. There are **51** words in **46** verses of 25 surahs. **28** of the words stand in verses that also have a call رَبَّنَا or رَبِّ, and they are prayers. Earlier lessons met this too. L3.08 met this in 3:147 and 1:6, and L4.10 showed that a ban said to Allah is a request. What decides it is **who is addressed**.

        { role: 'khabarInna', label: 'khabar: a verb sentence', hint: 'a past verb: ت is the doer, “I”', words: [{ ar: 'أَسْكَنتُ', en: 'I have settled', say: 'askantu', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'tied', hint: 'min “of”: it describes a left-out “some”', words: [{ ar: 'مِن', en: 'some of', say: 'min', type: 'p' }, { ar: 'ذُرِّيَّتِى', en: 'my offspring', say: 'dhurriyyati' }] },
      ]}
      en={['Our Lord, I have settled some of my offspring…']}

A common idea is that “our Lord” belongs to a group and “my Lord” to one person, so that the choice of pronoun shows who is included.

Look at 14:37. One speaker, Ibrahim, says رَبَّنَا, and then says **إِنِّىٓ أَسْكَنتُ**, “I have settled”. The call is “our”, and the verb and the pronoun are “I”. In 14:41 he says رَبَّنَا ٱغْفِرْ لِى, “forgive me”. In 14:40 he says both calls in one verse.

The plural call is also said by two speakers: in 7:23 the verb قَالَا is dual. So رَبَّنَا is said by one speaker and by two.

Quran Learn found no approved source that says the two calls mark one speaker or many. Al-Jadwal does not mention it, so the lesson does not claim it.

<Block id="mis-every" title="Mistake 2: every prayer has a command and a call first">
  <Stage label="Prayers that break the shape">
    <Tiles
      label="Four verses"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ظَلَمْنَآ أَنفُسَنَا', en: 'a statement (7:23)', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'إِنَّكَ تَعْلَمُ', en: 'no command (14:38)', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'غُفْرَانَكَ رَبَّنَا', en: 'a noun, then the call (2:285)' },
        { ar: 'ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ', en: 'praise, no call (14:39)' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A common idea is that every prayer opens with رَبَّنَا or رَبِّ, and that its request always uses a command or a ban. The verses of this lesson show more.

- **7:23** asks by saying what would follow without forgiveness. Its main verb is a past verb, ظَلَمْنَآ.
- **14:38** has a call and a statement of what Allah knows. It has no request.
- **2:285** ends: غُفْرَانَكَ رَبَّنَا, “Your forgiveness, our Lord”. The request is a noun, and the call comes after it. L4.07 shows that al-Jadwal reads غُفْرَانَكَ as the absolute object of a left-out verb.
- **14:39** is praise: ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ. There is no call.

The count agrees. 61 of the 137 calls have a command straight after them, and 76 do not. Read the verse before you decide what kind of prayer it is.

<Block id="mis-time" title="Mistake 3: بَعْدَ إِذْ is a condition">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="Time and condition in two verses"
      items={[
        { ar: 'بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا', en: 'a time clause (3:8)', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'إِن لَّمْ تَغْفِرْ', en: 'a condition (7:23)', type: 'p' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A common idea is that 3:8 is a conditional prayer. The verse has a time clause. It has no condition.

A condition has a **condition word**: إِنْ, مَنْ and the others of L4.04. It has two parts, the condition and the answer. In 7:23 the word is إِنْ, and the answer is left out.

بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا has none of that. Al-Jadwal reads بَعْدَ as a time word and إِذْ as its second word, a noun for “time”. The clause هَدَيْتَنَا is the second word of an idafah, and it has a place in the genitive. It does not need an answer. The meaning is “after the time You guided us”.

Do not mix إِذْ with إِذَا. إِذَا is a condition word with no jazm (L4.04). إِذْ in 3:8 is a noun for a time that has passed.

<Check n={21} q="Is بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا a condition?" options={['No: it is a time clause', 'Yes: إِذْ is a condition word', 'Yes: the verb هَدَيْتَنَا is in jazm']} answer={0} why="There is no condition word and no answer. بَعْدَ is a time word and إِذْ is its second word." />
</Block>

<Block id="mis-tag" title="Mistake 4: trusting the nominative tag">
  <Stage label="Two readings of one word">
    <Tiles
      label="رَبِّ in 14:35"
      items={[
        { ar: 'رَبِّ', en: 'Corpus tag: nominative', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'رَبِّ', en: 'al-Jadwal: accusative', type: 'n' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The Corpus tags the call رَبِّ in 14:35 as nominative. Al-Jadwal reads it as accusative: a munādā that is a muḍāf.

Quran Learn counted the tags on the 67 calls رَبِّ: **28** are accusative and **39** are nominative. The two tags are mixed, and the 39 nominative tags are wrong. The case ending of رَبِّ cannot be seen, because the yāʾ was dropped. So read the case from the sentence, and do not take it from the tag. The 70 calls رَبَّنَا are all tagged accusative, and the fatḥa shows it.

Another tag error is in 7:23. The word وَتَرْحَمْنَا is tagged “she”, but it is “You”, joined to تَغْفِرْ.

<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: the parts, the form and the speaker">
  <Stage label="For each prayer, say">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>The call:</b> رَبِّ or رَبَّنَا? Is the “O” written?</li>
      <li><b>The first word after it:</b> a command, a ban, inna or something else?</li>
      <li><b>The reason:</b> is there an إِنَّ sentence?</li>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Read رَبَّنَآ ءَاتِنَا فِى ٱلدُّنْيَا حَسَنَةً وَفِى ٱلْءَاخِرَةِ حَسَنَةً وَقِنَا عَذَابَ ٱلنَّارِ (Al-Baqarah 2:201, part). Find (a) the call and its case, (b) the two commands and their roots, (c) the objects of ءَاتِنَا, (d) the objects of وَقِنَا.">

- (a) **رَبَّنَآ**: the call. It is accusative, because it is the first word of an idafah. The word for “O” is left out.
- (b) **ءَاتِنَا**, from أ ت ي: the last weak letter went when the command was built. **وَقِنَا**, from و ق ي: the first و and the last ي went.
- (c) Two objects: نَا, “us”, and حَسَنَةً, “good”. The second حَسَنَةً is joined to the first, so it is the object as well.
- (d) Two objects: نَا, “us”, and عَذَابَ ٱلنَّارِ, “the punishment of the Fire”. English needs “from” and Arabic does not.

Each verb has two objects, and the joined نَا counts as one of them.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Compare قَالَا رَبَّنَا ظَلَمْنَآ أَنفُسَنَا (Al-Aʿraf 7:23, start) and قَالَ رَبِّ ٱشْرَحْ لِى صَدْرِى (Ta-Ha 20:25). (a) How do the calls differ? (b) How many speak in each? (c) What is the first word after the call, and what kind is it? (d) Where is the condition in 7:23?">

- (a) **رَبَّنَا** is رَبَّ and نَا, with a fatḥa. **رَبِّ** is رَبِّ and a dropped yāʾ. Both are accusative, called words.
- (b) قَالَا has a dual ending: two speakers. قَالَ is “he said”: one speaker.
- (c) After رَبَّنَا comes ظَلَمْنَآ, a past verb: a statement. After رَبِّ comes ٱشْرَحْ, a command that is a prayer: a request.
- (d) It is at the end of the verse: وَإِن لَّمْ تَغْفِرْ لَنَا وَتَرْحَمْنَا. The word is **إِن**. Both verbs are in jazm. The answer is left out, and the oath sentence لَنَكُونَنَّ shows it.

Al-Jadwal says the condition word brings the jazm, and لَمْ only says “not”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Name the parts of رَبَّنَا لَا تُزِغْ قُلُوبَنَا بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا وَهَبْ لَنَا مِن لَّدُنكَ رَحْمَةً إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْوَهَّابُ (Al-Imran 3:8). Give the call, the negative request, the time clause, the second request and the reason.">

- **The call:** رَبَّنَا.
- **The negative request:** لَا تُزِغْ قُلُوبَنَا, “do not let our hearts deviate”. لَا is a ban that is a prayer. تُزِغْ is a present verb of Form IV in jazm: the long ī went and a sukūn is on the غ.
- **The time clause:** بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا, “after the time You guided us”. It is not a condition.
- **The second request:** وَهَبْ لَنَا مِن لَّدُنكَ رَحْمَةً, “and give us mercy from Yourself”. هَبْ is a command from وَهَبَ, and its first و went. رَحْمَةً is the object. لَنَا and مِن لَّدُنكَ are two phrases that go with هَبْ.
- **The reason:** إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْوَهَّابُ, “indeed You are the Giver”. ٱلْوَهَّابُ comes from the same root as هَبْ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Look at Ibrahim’s prayer, 14:35 to 14:41, and the table in block “A whole prayer”. (a) Which verse has no call? (b) Which verses have two calls? (c) Which verses have a call and no command? (d) In which verse does he say both رَبِّ and رَبَّنَا, and what does that show?">

- (a) **14:39.** It is praise: ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ.
- (b) **14:37** (رَّبَّنَآ … رَبَّنَا) and **14:40** (رَبِّ … رَبَّنَا).
- (c) **14:36** (a reason and two conditions) and **14:38** (a statement of what Allah knows).
- (d) **14:40.** One speaker says both. This shows that the two calls do not mark one speaker against many. Nothing in al-Jadwal says that they do.

The answers are read from the table in the block “A whole prayer”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Is each command said to Allah, or to a person? (a) رَبَّنَآ ءَاتِنَا مِن لَّدُنكَ رَحْمَةً (Al-Kahf 18:10, part) (b) ءَاتِنَا غَدَآءَنَا (Al-Kahf 18:62, part) (c) فَأْتِنَا بِمَا تَعِدُنَآ (Hud 11:32, part) (d) ٱهْدِنَا ٱلصِّرَٰطَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ (Al-Fatiha 1:6)">

- (a) **To Allah.** The call رَبَّنَآ comes first, and the young men say it in the cave.
- (b) **To a person.** Musa says it to his young man: قَالَ لِفَتَىٰهُ. It is an order, and al-Jadwal calls the verb a command.
- (c) **To a person.** Nuh’s people say it to him. Al-Jadwal reads the command as the answer of a left-out condition: “if you are truthful, bring us what you promise”.
- (d) **To Allah.** There is no call in this verse, but the verse before it speaks to Allah as “You”: إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ (1:5). L4.13 calls ٱهْدِنَا a command of supplication.

The form is the same in all four. The one who is addressed decides.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Two bans with the same verb: لَا تَذَرُنَّ ءَالِهَتَكُمْ (Nuh 71:23, part) and رَّبِّ لَا تَذَرْ عَلَى ٱلْأَرْضِ مِنَ ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ دَيَّارًا (Nuh 71:26). Who says each, to whom, and what does the verb look like after لَا?">

- **71:23:** Nuh’s people say it to each other: “do not leave your gods”. The verb is plural, so jazm shows as a lost nūn, and then a nūn of emphasis is added.
- **71:26:** Nuh says it to Allah, after the call رَّبِّ. The verb is “you” for one, so jazm shows as a sukūn on the ر.

The لَا is the same. The one who is addressed decides whether it is a ban between people or a request to Allah.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Find the root that comes back in the request and in the reason. (a) وَهَبْ لَنَا مِن لَّدُنكَ رَحْمَةً إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْوَهَّابُ (Al-Imran 3:8, end) (b) وَتُبْ عَلَيْنَآ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلتَّوَّابُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ (Al-Baqarah 2:128, end)">

- (a) **و ه ب.** هَبْ is the command, with its first و gone. ٱلْوَهَّابُ is the intense form of the same root, “the One who gives much”.
- (b) **ت و ب.** تُبْ is the command, with the long ū gone. ٱلتَّوَّابُ is the intense form, “the One who accepts repentance”.

Two verses fit. The lesson does not call it a rule, and no source we use says it.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Say what each call is and what is left out or written. (a) رَّبِّ ٱغْفِرْ لِى وَلِوَٰلِدَىَّ (Nuh 71:28, part) (b) رَبَّنَا ٱغْفِرْ لِى (Ibrahim 14:41, part) (c) وَقَالَ ٱلرَّسُولُ يَٰرَبِّ إِنَّ قَوْمِى (Al-Furqan 25:30, part)">

- (a) **رَّبِّ**: “my Lord”, a muḍāf, accusative. The yāʾ and the word for “O” are both left out.
- (b) **رَبَّنَا**: “our Lord”, a muḍāf, accusative, with a fatḥa you can see. The word for “O” is left out.
- (c) **يَٰرَبِّ**: here the word for “O” is written. The yāʾ of “my” is still dropped. Al-Jadwal reads the word as a muḍāf in the accusative with a fatḥa you cannot see.

Only 2 of the 137 calls in the Corpus have the written يَا. This is one of them.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Find the condition in each prayer. Name the condition word and the verb, and say how the answer is joined. (a) رَبَّنَآ أَخْرِجْنَا مِنْهَا فَإِنْ عُدْنَا فَإِنَّا ظَٰلِمُونَ (Al-Muminun 23:107) (b) رَبَّنَا لَا تُؤَاخِذْنَآ إِن نَّسِينَآ أَوْ أَخْطَأْنَا (Al-Baqarah 2:286, part)">

- (a) The word is **إِنْ**, and the verb is **عُدْنَا**, a past verb in the place of jazm. The answer is فَإِنَّا ظَٰلِمُونَ. It starts with إِنَّ, so the فَ ties it. Al-Jadwal says the second فَ is the one that ties the answer, and the sentence is in the place of jazm.
- (b) The word is **إِن**, and the verbs are **نَّسِينَآ** and **أَخْطَأْنَا**, both past. The answer is left out. The sentence before the condition shows it: “if we forget or make a mistake, then do not hold us to account”.

</Exercise>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="many prayers in the Quran have three parts"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'the call', small: 'رَبِّ, رَبَّنَا' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'the request', small: 'a command or لَا + jazm' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'the reason', small: 'إِنَّكَ …' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'a prayer', small: 'دُعَاء' },

- A prayer often has **three parts**: the call, the request and the reason. Al-Jadwal names the call (مُنَادَى), the answer of the call and the reason. The three-part shape is a teaching summary, and several verses have only some of the parts.
- The call is **رَبِّ** or **رَبَّنَا**. It is a muḍāf, accusative, and the word for “O” is left out. In the Corpus it is a call 67 times (رَبِّ) and 70 times (رَبَّنَا). The Corpus tags 39 of the 67 as nominative, and that is wrong.
- The request is a command that is a prayer, or **لَا and a verb in jazm**. The form is the same as a command to a person. The one who is addressed decides. 61 of the 137 calls have a command straight after them.
- A request can have an **answer**, a verb in jazm (20:28). The reason starts with **إِنَّ** and says what Allah is.
- A prayer can hold a **condition** (7:23, 14:36, 2:286). بَعْدَ إِذْ in 3:8 is a time clause and no condition.
- **One speaker** can say both رَبِّ and رَبَّنَا. The lesson found no source that says one marks one speaker and the other many.
