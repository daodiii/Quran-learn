Both verses are questions, and they work differently.

In 12:51 the king asks the women what happened. The next words of the verse are their answer: قُلْنَ حَٰشَ لِلَّهِ مَا عَلِمْنَا عَلَيْهِ مِن سُوٓءٍ, “they said, Allah forbid, we knew no evil of him”. Al-Jadwal names no figure for this question. It gives the places of the words and nothing more.

In 95:8 nobody has to give news. Al-Jadwal calls the hamza “a question of confirmation” (istifhām taqrīrī, اسْتِفْهَام تَقْرِيرِيّ). L4.18 met that name in 94:1 and 105:1.

So a question can ask, and a question can do something else. Some questions have a name in al-Jadwal, as 95:8 does. Others have none. Steps 1 to 3 take the words that make a question. Step 4 reads what al-Jadwal says some of them do.

Quran Learn counted with a script over the Corpus file. It looked at every segment tagged INTG. Those are **946 segments**, in **830 of the 6,236 verses** and in **94 of the 114 surahs**. Of the 946, the hamza prefix is 507 and هَلْ is 93. The other 346 are the question nouns and the words for place, time and manner, listed in the tiles above.

A tag is not the same as a question. The tag marks a question word. It does not say whether the speaker needs an answer. This lesson counts words. It does not count questions that ask and questions that do not.

The Corpus tags have errors, and four of them show above.

- **مَا in 12:50 and 12:51.** The Corpus calls both a joining noun (REL). Al-Jadwal calls both a question noun. L3.11 found the same kind of error in the مَن of 13:16. So 95 is the least the count of مَا can be.
- **أَمْ.** The Corpus tags 137 words as the joining أَمْ, “or”, in 123 verses, and does not count them as question words. Some of them start a question, as in 52:35 (block `am`).
- **لَا تَجِدُوا۟ in 17:68.** The Corpus tags it as a ban and as jazm. Al-Jadwal reads a negative لَا and a verb in naṣb, joined to يُرْسِلَ. You will see this in step 4.

A common summary says that the Quran asks hundreds of questions. The tags show 946 question words in 830 verses. That is what Quran Learn can show. It says nothing about how often an answer is expected.

L3.11 taught مَن for a person and مَا for a thing. Both are nouns, and each starts its sentence as the mubtadaʾ. In 2:245 al-Jadwal reads مَن as the mubtadaʾ and the pointing word ذَا as its khabar. The joining noun ٱلَّذِى comes next. Al-Jadwal calls it a badal of ذَا, or a word that explains it. The clause يُقْرِضُ ٱللَّهَ finishes it. So مَّن ذَا ٱلَّذِى reads “who is this one, the one who …”.

مَا works the same way. In 12:50 al-Jadwal reads مَا بَالُ ٱلنِّسْوَةِ with مَا as the mubtadaʾ and بَالُ as its khabar, and it reads 12:51 the same way. The pair in 101:2 is built alike: مَا ٱلْقَارِعَةُ, with مَا as the mubtadaʾ and ٱلْقَارِعَةُ as its khabar. L5.06 studies the longer question وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا, where the same مَا comes twice.

The word مَاذَا is مَا with ذَا. The Corpus tags 26 of them, as in مَاذَآ أَنزَلَ رَبُّكُمْ (16:30). Al-Jadwal’s page for 16:30 points back to an earlier verse for it, so the lesson gives no parse of this form.

The same مَن ذَا ٱلَّذِى comes in 2:255, before إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِۦ. L5.03 read it. Al-Jadwal names no figure there. Step 4 says what al-Durr says.

<Block id="kayfa" title="كَيْفَ: “how?”, and how it is read">

In all three phrases al-Jadwal reads كَيْفَ as a question noun in the place of a ḥāl (L4.01). A ḥāl says in what state someone is while he does the thing. Here it asks about that state: “how can you disbelieve?”, “how should I fear?”. In 6:81 and 88:17 al-Jadwal says the verb after كَيْفَ is the word that works on it.

The same word is read another way in 105:1, أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ. Al-Jadwal reads كَيْفَ there as a word that stands in for the absolute object ([L4.07](/learn/level-4/07-maf-ul-mutlaq/)), “what great deed He did”. So the same word can have two jobs. The verb next to it and the meaning decide.

Al-Durr says كَيْفَ is a question noun that asks about states, and it is built on a fatḥa because it holds the meaning of the hamza.

These words ask where, when and how long. Al-Jadwal calls a word like this a place word or a time word (ẓarf, ظَرْف, L4.04). Each one is tied to another word.

- **أَيْنَ** in 81:26 is a place word tied to the verb, with a left-out “to”: “to where are you going?”.
- **مَتَىٰ** in 67:25 is a time word tied to a left-out khabar that is put first. هَٰذَا is the mubtadaʾ.
- **أَيَّانَ** in 51:12 is read the same way. The left-out khabar is put first, and يَوْمُ is the mubtadaʾ.
- **كَمْ** in 2:259 is a time word tied to لَبِثْتَ.

أَنَّىٰ in 2:259 is “how”. Al-Jadwal says it means كَيْفَ and is a ḥāl. A footnote says al-ʿUkbarī allowed “when”, a time word. Al-Jadwal’s page for 67:25 does not parse ٱلْوَعْدُ. The lesson shows it as one idea with هَٰذَا.

أَيْنَ, مَتَىٰ and أَيَّانَ are rare. The Corpus tags them 12, 9 and 5 times.

In 6:81 al-Jadwal reads أَيّ as a question noun and the mubtadaʾ. The group ٱلْفَرِيقَيْنِ is its second part, in the genitive. أَحَقُّ is the khabar, and بِٱلْأَمْنِ is tied to it. So أَيّ starts an idafah, the pattern of L2.08.

Some summaries call أَيّ an “adjective that agrees with the noun”. Al-Jadwal does not read it that way here. It reads a noun with a second part. In 77:50, فَبِأَىِّ حَدِيثٍۭ بَعْدَهُۥ يُؤْمِنُونَ, the word comes after بِ, and al-Jadwal says only that the phrase is tied to the verb يُؤْمِنُونَ. The Corpus tags أَيّ 35 times.

The hamza is one letter, written at the start of the first word of the question. Joining words, like فَ and وَ, stand after it. So you read أَفَلَا as three parts: the hamza, the فَ and the لَا. L4.10 shows the same build in أَوَلَمْ (7:184).

Many of the questions in this lesson have no name in al-Jadwal: 12:51, 101:2, 2:245, 55:60 and 52:35 (apart from the footnote of 52:30). For these al-Jadwal gives the places of the words. This lesson keeps to that and does not add a name.

The fourth question is about what the verse itself shows. In 12:51 the women answer at once. In 2:28 no answer follows. The verse goes on with what Allah did: gave life, takes it, gives it again. In 17:68 another question follows in the next verse, 17:69, which begins with أَمْ. What follows a question is a fact about the text, and anyone can check it.

The order of the four questions is Quran Learn’s teaching summary. It is not a method from a classical book. L5.01 teaches the method for one verse. This lesson asks what a question word does inside it.

<Block id="m2" title="Mistake 2: claims about a word that the verses do not show">
  <Stage label="Three claims">
    <Tiles
      label="What the verses show"
      items={[
        { ar: 'هَلْ', en: 'before a verb in 59 of 93, not always', type: 'p' },

Three claims in some summaries do not hold up.

- **“هَلْ comes before a verb.”** In 59 of 93 tags it does. In 34 it does not.
- **“أَلَمْ is a double negation.”** The hamza is a question, and لَمْ is the only negative.
- **“أَيّ is an adjective that agrees with a noun.”** Al-Jadwal reads it as a question noun and the first part of an idafah.

A fourth claim, that the hamza has more force than هَلْ, could not be confirmed in an approved source, so the lesson leaves it out.

مَا asks, or negates, or joins a clause. L4.10 and L3.11 teach the three. In 12:51 the Corpus tag says joining, and the sentence says it asks. Put the question in English: “what is your business?” makes sense, so it is a question word. This is the same test L4.10 gave for the negative مَا.
