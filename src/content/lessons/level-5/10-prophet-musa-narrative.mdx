Read each verse from right to left. Both start with the same word, **قَالَ**, “he said”. In 20:49 Pharaoh speaks. In 20:50 Musa answers him. The verb does not tell you. It only says “one man spoke”.

The verse gives other clues. In 20:49 the speaker calls the other person: **يَٰمُوسَىٰ**, “O Musa”. Nobody calls himself, so Musa is not the one speaking. In 20:50 the words are an answer to the question before them. This lesson collects these clues and uses them across the story.

The Corpus counts the name Musa 136 times, in 34 surahs. That is more than the name of Pharaoh (74) or of Ibrahim (69). Quran Learn made these counts with a script over the Corpus file. This lesson reads one stretch of his story: Ta-Ha 20:9 to 20:71 and Al-Qasas 28:7 to 28:12.

In 20:9 to 20:50 the Corpus has nine قَالَ for one man, and one قَالَا. Four of the nine are Allah, four are Musa and one is Pharaoh. In 28:7 to 28:12 it has three قَالَتْ or قَالَتِ. These are counts from the Corpus tags, plus our reading of who speaks.

Some speeches have no قَالَ at all. In 20:11 to 20:17 the voice goes on from one verse to the next with no new “he said”. The first new قَالَ is in 20:18, and it is Musa’s answer.

The story opens with a question. **هَلْ** asks “has it?”. Al-Jadwal says this question is for *taqrīr*, “making the news firm”. L4.18 met the same word, taqrīr, in the question أَلَمْ. This lesson says no more about what the question is for.

The verb **أَتَىٰكَ** is a past verb with the object -ka, “you”. The doer is **حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰٓ**, “the story of Musa”, an idafah (L2.08). The verb comes first and the doer after it (L2.03). Al-Jadwal reads the sentence as a new start.

Al-Jadwal’s style note on this verse gives the question a name: *tashwīq*, التَّشْوِيق, “raising eagerness”, and it adds “urging the listener to listen”. That is al-Jadwal’s description. The lesson only reports it.

The speech has three layers. First, the story says Musa saw a fire. Then **فَقَالَ لِأَهْلِهِ**: “and he said to his family”. After that comes what he said. Al-Jadwal gives the whole speech a place of its own, the place of the accusative, because it is *what is said* (L1.09).

Inside the speech there is a command, **ٱمْكُثُوٓا۟**, “stay”, and a reason, **إِنِّىٓ ءَانَسْتُ نَارًا**, “I have perceived a fire”. The second sentence gives the reason for the first. Al-Jadwal calls it a reason sentence. The next steps show that this order comes back: a command first and its reason after it.

The verb **أَجِدُ** is a present verb from the root و ج د. In 93:6 to 93:8 ([L4.19](/learn/level-4/19-checkpoint-ad-duha/)) the same verb takes two objects: “He found you an orphan”. Here it takes **one**: هُدًى, “guidance”.

Al-Jadwal’s footnote gives the reason. In this verse the verb means “I meet”, and it takes a single object. So you cannot count objects from the letters of the verb. You have to read what the verb means in the verse.

Al-Jadwal also gives a note about the word before it. It reads **ءَاتِيكُم** as the khabar of لَعَلَّ, a word of hope (L2.10), with the كُم as the second word of an idafah. A footnote allows a second reading: a present verb, with كُم as its object and a hidden “I” as the doer.

The verb **نُودِىَ** is passive: “he was called”. L3.09 taught that a passive verb leaves the doer out. Here no word says who called. The next word, **يَٰمُوسَىٰٓ**, is the call itself. Al-Jadwal reads the call as an explaining start: it says what the calling was.

The first speech of the voice follows at once, in 20:12: “Indeed I am your Lord.” No قَالَ comes in between. The verse that follows, 20:13, has أَنَا ٱخْتَرْتُكَ, “I chose you”, and 20:14 has إِنَّنِىٓ أَنَا ٱللَّهُ, “Indeed I am Allah”. The speaker is named by what the voice says. L3.17 takes 20:11 word by word.

Verse 12 is the call itself, and it has three parts. The first says who speaks: **إِنِّىٓ أَنَا۠ رَبُّكَ**, “indeed I am your Lord”. The second is a command, **فَٱخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ**. The third is the reason for it, إِنَّكَ بِٱلْوَادِ ٱلْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى.

The words **إِنِّىٓ أَنَا** have “I” twice. Al-Jadwal reads the second one as a stress on the first, and gives the other reading in a footnote. We follow the main reading.

The **فَ** in فَٱخْلَعْ is a link, and al-Jadwal reads it as the link of an answer to a condition left out. Read it this way: “if you have taken this in, then take off your sandals”. This is the first *left-out word* of the lesson. The verse does not write it, and the grammar books put it in.

The answer has four parts. The first, **هِىَ عَصَاىَ**, “it is my staff”, answers the question. Then Musa goes on: “I lean on it, I beat down leaves with it onto my sheep, and I have other uses in it.”

Al-Jadwal gives this style a name: *iṭnāb*, إِطْنَاب, saying more than the plain answer needs. In plain English, it is using more words than the bare meaning needs. Al-Jadwal says “it is my staff” would have been enough, and that Musa widened his answer for the pleasure of the speech. It also says he answered a question nobody asked: “and what do you do with it?”. It calls this way of answering *talfīf*, تَلْفِيف.

Two words in the verse are left out. The object of **أَهُشُّ** is not written. Al-Jadwal gives it as “the leaves of the tree”. The khabar of the last part is also not written: **لِىَ فِيهَا**, “for me, in it”, stands in front of the mubtadaʾ **مَآرِبُ**. This is the fronted khabar of L2.01.

A woman speaks for the first time. The verb **وَقَالَتِ** ends in the tāʾ of “she”, and the doer follows it: **ٱمْرَأَتُ فِرْعَوْنَ**, “the wife of Pharaoh”. Here two clues agree: a written name and a feminine ending.

Her first words have no mubtadaʾ. **قُرَّتُ عَيْنٍ** is the news, and al-Jadwal puts in the word that is left out: “he is”. In English the sentence needs it: “He is a joy of the eye for me and for you.”

The ban **لَا تَقْتُلُوهُ** ends in -ū, so she speaks to a group. The verse does not say who they are. Al-Jadwal does not say either. The ending gives number and gender, and the verse does not give the people.

It is easy to think that a قَالَ in one verse is the same man as the قَالَ before it. In 20:9 to 20:50 the same word is used for three different speakers. The verb says “one man spoke” and gives no name.

An ending tells you how many people are spoken to and whether they are men or women. It does not give their names. In 28:9 the ban لَا تَقْتُلُوهُ ends in -ū, so she speaks to a group. A reader might add “Pharaoh and his court”. The verse does not say it, and al-Jadwal does not either.

(a) ٱذْهَبْ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ (Ta-Ha 20:24) (b) قَالَ رَبِّ ٱشْرَحْ لِى صَدْرِى (Ta-Ha 20:25) (c) قَالَ قَدْ أُوتِيتَ سُؤْلَكَ يَٰمُوسَىٰ (Ta-Ha 20:36) (d) قَالَا رَبَّنَآ إِنَّنَا نَخَافُ أَن يَفْرُطَ عَلَيْنَآ أَوْ أَن يَطْغَىٰ (Ta-Ha 20:45)">

**(a)** The voice speaks. There is no قَالَ: the voice goes on from 20:12. The verse is a command and its reason.

**(b)** Musa. The word رَبِّ, “my Lord”, shows he speaks to his Lord. ٱشْرَحْ is a command in form (L3.08), and in this verse it is a request, because of who speaks to whom. L5.07 teaches the patterns of requests.

**(c)** The one Musa asked. The called name يَٰمُوسَىٰ says it is not Musa, and the passive أُوتِيتَ, “you have been given”, answers his request.

**(d)** Two people: Musa and Harun. The verb قَالَا ends in the dual -ā. They say رَبَّنَآ, “our Lord”, with -nā, where Musa alone said رَبِّ with -ī. The pronoun shows how many speak.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Compare how two stories open. (a) وَٱتْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ نَبَأَ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ (Ash-Shuʿara 26:69) (b) وَهَلْ أَتَىٰكَ حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰٓ (Ta-Ha 20:9) For each, say what kind of sentence it is and which word names the story.">

**(a)** A command. **ٱتْلُ** is a command, “recite”, with a hidden “you” as the doer. Al-Jadwal reads it as a new start. The word that names the story is **نَبَأَ**, “news”, and its second word is the name Ibrahim, in an idafah. L5.09 takes this verse apart.

**(b)** A question. **هَلْ** asks “has it?”, and the verb is a past verb, **أَتَىٰكَ**. The word that names the story is **حَدِيثُ**, “story”, the doer, with the name Musa after it in an idafah.

The two openings have the same shape in one way: a noun for the story, and then the name of the prophet in an idafah. They differ in the sentence: a command in (a) and a question in (b). Do not say that a choice of sentence “reflects character” or “shows” a purpose. Al-Jadwal says nothing like that on these verses (L4.18, “Mistake 4”).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Find the command or the ban and the reason that follows. Say what al-Jadwal calls the second sentence, and who the command is for. (a) ٱذْهَبْ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ (Ta-Ha 20:24) (b) ٱذْهَبَآ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ (Ta-Ha 20:43) (c) وَلَا تَخَافِى وَلَا تَحْزَنِىٓ إِنَّا رَآدُّوهُ إِلَيْكِ (Al-Qasas 28:7, part) Then say how (a) and (b) differ.">

In all three, the second sentence is a reason sentence (*taʿlīliyya*) with no place.

- **(a)** The command is ٱذْهَبْ, for one man. The reason is إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ, “indeed he has transgressed”. The verb طَغَىٰ is a defective verb, root ط غ ي (L4.13).
- **(b)** The command is ٱذْهَبَآ, for two people. The reason is the same sentence.
- **(c)** The commands are bans, لَا تَخَافِى and لَا تَحْزَنِىٓ, for one woman. The reason is إِنَّا رَآدُّوهُ إِلَيْكِ, “indeed we will return him to you”.

(a) and (b) differ in the command only. In (a) it ends with a sukūn, for one man. In (b) it ends in -ā, for two people. The reason does not change.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="The sister’s offer is told twice. Compare the two. (a) إِذْ تَمْشِىٓ أُخْتُكَ فَتَقُولُ هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ مَن يَكْفُلُهُۥ (Ta-Ha 20:40, part) (b) فَقَالَتْ هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰٓ أَهْلِ بَيْتٍ يَكْفُلُونَهُۥ لَكُمْ (Al-Qasas 28:12, part)">

The offer has the same shape in both: هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ …. The differences are these.

1. **The verb of speech.** In 20:40 it is present, **فَتَقُولُ**, “and she says”. In 28:12 it is past, **فَقَالَتْ**, “so she said”.
2. **The doer.** In 20:40 the doer is written, **أُخْتُكَ**, “your sister”. In 28:12 it is a hidden “she”, and al-Jadwal says it points to the sister of Musa.
3. **Who is spoken to.** In 20:40 the pronoun -ka speaks to Musa, “your sister”. In 28:12 the story speaks about him: “him”, as in عَلَيْهِ earlier in the same verse.
4. **What she offers.** In 20:40 it is **مَن يَكْفُلُهُۥ**, “whoever will care for him”, with a singular verb. In 28:12 it is أَهْلِ بَيْتٍ يَكْفُلُونَهُۥ, “a household that will care for him”, with a plural verb.

Al-Jadwal reads the clause فَتَقُولُ in 20:40 as joined to the clause تَمْشِى, both in the place of the genitive after إِذْ.

</Exercise>

- **Many speakers.** In 20:9 to 20:50 the Corpus has nine قَالَ for one man: four are Allah, four are Musa and one is Pharaoh, by our reading of the story. The dual قَالَا (20:45), the plural قَالُوٓا۟ (20:70) and the feminine قَالَتْ (28:9 to 28:12) tell you how many speak and whether they are men or women.
- **Six clues.** A written name, a called name, the ending of قَالَ, a question and its answer, the pronoun in the words said, and what the words say. This list is our teaching summary.
- **Report and reason.** After قَالَ the words are what is said (L1.09). A command or a ban comes first and a reason sentence after it, in the six verses we read.
- **Question and answer.** مَا in 20:17 and مَن in 20:49 are question nouns and the mubtadaʾ. Musa’s answer in 20:18 says more than the question asked, which al-Jadwal names iṭnāb.
- **Objects.** أَجِدُ has one object, أَعْطَىٰ and نَتَّخِذَهُۥ have two, and the passive أُوتِيتَ keeps the second one.
- **Left out.** A condition (20:12, 20:49), a preposition (20:21), objects (20:46, 20:18), a mubtadaʾ (28:9) and a khabar (28:10). We say “al-Jadwal puts in …” and keep the verse as it is.
