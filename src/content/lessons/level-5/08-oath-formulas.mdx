Read each verse from right to left. The English under each verse is our own plain meaning, not a translation.

Verse 1 starts with **وَ**, the oath letter. The noun after it, ٱلَّيْلِ, is genitive. Al-Jadwal ties the phrase to a verb that is not written: أُقْسِمُ, “I swear”. Verses 2 and 3 name two more things to swear by. Verse 4 is a different kind of sentence: it starts with إِنَّ and has a لَ on its khabar. Al-Jadwal calls it the answer of the oath.

So three verses swear and one verse says what is sworn. The rest of the lesson takes these parts one at a time and meets them in other surahs.

The Corpus has no tag named “oath”. It does tag a wāw as a **preposition**, and not as “and”, in 28 words. We read all 28 verses: each is an oath wāw. It also tags the tāʾ of oath in 9 words. A bāʾ of oath looks like any other bāʾ, so it has no tag of its own.

| Letter | Words tagged | Where |
|---|---|---|
| وَ | 28 | 19 at the start of a surah (verse 1 or 2). 9 inside a surah: 4:65, 6:23, 6:30, 15:92, 19:68, 51:23, 64:7, 74:32, 86:11 |
| تَ | 9 | 12:73, 12:85, 12:91, 12:95, 16:56, 16:63, 21:57, 26:97, 37:56 |
| بِ | none | We read it in 26:44 and 38:82 |

A tag is a guide, and here it is not complete. The Corpus tags the wāw of 10:53, 34:3, 51:1, 51:7 and 52:1 as “and”, and al-Jadwal reads each one as an oath wāw. So there are at least 33 oath wāws (28 and 5). At least 21 of the 114 surahs open with an oath wāw: 19 tagged, and 51 and 52. That is about one surah in five. Oaths also come inside surahs, as the table shows.

**وَٱلْفَجْرِ** is one written word with two pieces. **وَ** is the oath letter and means “by”. **ٱلْفَجْرِ** is the noun after it, in the genitive, with a kasra that shows (-i). Al-Jadwal says the phrase belongs to a verb that is not written, أُقْسِمُ, “I swear”, and calls “I swear by the dawn” an opening sentence with no place.

The Corpus tags this wāw as a preposition. The wāw is the common oath letter: 28 tagged words against 9 for the tāʾ.

L2.07 gave a two-part check for an oath wāw: the next noun is genitive, and something is sworn by. The next block adds a third part, because a wāw that means “and” can also be followed by a genitive noun.

In 95:1 both wāws are followed by a genitive noun, so the case does not tell them apart. ٱلزَّيْتُونِ is genitive because it is joined to ٱلتِّينِ, and a joined word copies the case of the word before it (L2.06). In 2:158 the same thing happens in the accusative: al-Jadwal says ٱلْمَرْوَةَ is joined to ٱلصَّفَا and follows it in the accusative. The case follows the word it joins. The wāw does not set it.

So ask what stands before the wāw. In 95:1 **nothing** stands before the first wāw, so it has nothing to join and a noun is sworn by: it is the oath wāw. The second wāw joins one noun to another. The Corpus tags them the same way: the first wāw is a preposition, the second is “and”. Al-Jadwal ties the first phrase to “I swear”. On its page for 95:1 it says one later word outright is joined: هَٰذَا in 95:3 (the next block). For the other words it gives no line, and the Corpus tags their wāws “and”.

A third wāw, the wāw of ḥāl, means “while” and starts a sentence that gives a state (L4.01). It is not followed by a sworn-by noun.

<Note kind="know">The three questions above (is something sworn by, does anything stand before it to join, is the noun genitive) are a teaching summary. Quran Learn found no approved source that lists them as a test. They come from the verses read in this lesson.</Note>

After the first noun, a chain of oaths goes on with “and”. Al-Jadwal reads 95:3 like this: هَٰذَا is joined to ٱلتِّينِ, and takes its place in the genitive. A word like هَٰذَا is built, so its case is “by place” (L3.10). ٱلْبَلَدِ is a badal of هَٰذَا, or a clarifier (ʿaṭf bayān), and it is genitive. ٱلْأَمِينِ describes ٱلْبَلَدِ. The Corpus tags the four wāws of 95:1 to 95:3 in one pattern: the first is a preposition, the other three are “and”.

In 95:2, طُورِ is the first word of an idafah and سِينِينَ is the second (L2.08). Al-Jadwal calls سِينِينَ the name of a mountain in Egypt. On ٱلتِّينِ and ٱلزَّيْتُونِ it gives two readings: the names of mountains, or the known fruits as class nouns. This lesson does not choose between them.

Other chains look the same. On 52:1 al-Jadwal says the first wāw is the oath wāw and the wāws after it are joining wāws (it counts five places on its page, four of them in the oath). On 51:1 and 100:1 the words after the first noun begin with فَ, and al-Jadwal calls that فَ a joining letter too.

Two more forms of the oath show that the letters are not the whole story.

**لَعَمْرُكَ** has no oath letter. Al-Jadwal reads the لَ as the beginning lām, عَمْرُكَ as a mubtadaʾ in the nominative, and says its khabar is always left out: “my oath”. Nothing here makes the noun genitive. So “the sworn-by noun is genitive” holds after the three letters, and it does not hold for every oath formula. The answer is the إِنَّ sentence after it.

**وَرَبِّى** in 64:7 is an oath wāw in the middle of a verse. The Corpus tags it as a preposition. Al-Jadwal reads بَلَىٰ as a word that answers a denial, the wāw as the oath wāw, and the phrase as tied to the left-out “I swear”. The answer is لَتُبْعَثُنَّ: a لَ, a present verb and the nūn of emphasis (L4.06). Oaths are not only at the start of surahs: nine of the 28 tagged wāws are inside a surah.

Al-Jadwal reads it the same way on its pages for 89:1, 91:1, 92:1 and 93:1: a time word with the sense of a condition taken away, tied to the left-out “I swear”, and in the place of the accusative. Some oaths swear by doer words (ism fāʿil, L3.19) in the feminine plural. Each ends in -āt, with a kasra for the genitive. The first has the oath wāw. The next words begin with فَ, and al-Jadwal calls that فَ a joining letter in all three places of 51:2 to 51:4 and all four of 100:2 to 100:5.

ضَبْحًا and قَدْحًا are, for al-Jadwal, absolute objects of left-out verbs, with a footnote that allows a noun standing in for a ḥāl (L4.07, L4.01). In 100:3, صُبْحًا is a time word tied to ٱلْمُغِيرَٰتِ.

Verses 100:4 and 100:5, فَأَثَرْنَ بِهِۦ نَقْعًا and فَوَسَطْنَ بِهِۦ جَمْعًا, have verbs. Al-Jadwal joins the first to the doer words before it, and the second to the first, because a doer word with ٱلْ works like a clause: “those that charged, then raised dust, then went into the middle”. So the five verses hold **three** sworn-by doer words and two joined verb clauses, and not five sworn-by nouns.

<Note kind="know">The Corpus tags the wāw of 51:1 as “and”. Al-Jadwal reads it as the oath wāw: it ties وَٱلذَّٰرِيَٰتِ to the left-out “I swear”. Where the two differ, this lesson follows al-Jadwal.</Note>

An oath does not always have a spoken answer. Al-Jadwal says the answer is left out in three of the ten sections:

| Oath | What al-Jadwal says | A second view in its notes |
|---|---|---|
| 91:1 to 91:8 | The answer is left out. It suggests “you will surely be raised” | Another suggestion, from al-Kashshāf: “Allah will surely destroy the unbelievers of Makkah”. Or 91:9 is the answer, with the لَ dropped because the speech is long |
| 85:1 to 85:3 | The answer is left out. It suggests “the recompense is surely true” | 85:4, قُتِلَ أَصْحَٰبُ ٱلْأُخْدُودِ, can be the answer, with لَ and قَدْ understood |

In the note on 85:1, al-Jadwal records a rule: when the answer is a past verb, it should come with لَ and قَدْ, as in 12:91, تَٱللَّهِ لَقَدْ ءَاثَرَكَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا. So a past verb with neither would be an answer with those two left out. That is why 85:4 and 91:9 are given as possible answers.

So an oath does not always have an answer you can point to in the text. In these three, the answer is understood and not written.

Al-Jadwal names three figures on its page for 100:1. L4.17 and L4.18 teach figures of this kind, and this block uses their names where they have one.

- **100:2.** فَٱلْمُورِيَٰتِ قَدْحًا is a metaphor of the open kind (istiʿāra taṣrīḥiyya, L4.18). Al-Jadwal says the horses are said to strike out the fire of war and light it, so war is compared to a burning fire.
- **100:4.** Al-Jadwal names a difference between the joined word and the word it is joined to. The verb فَأَثَرْنَ is joined to nouns, to the doer words. In the sense, it is joined to the verb that the doer word stands for.
- **100:7 and 100:8.** The words شَهِيدٌ and شَدِيدٌ differ in one letter in the middle, ه and د. Al-Jadwal calls this jinās lāḥiq, a kind of jinās (L4.18 teaches two other kinds).

Two more notes are about sound and harmony. On 89:4, al-Jadwal says the yāʾ of يَسْرِ was dropped to match the endings of the verses. On 92:1, a note on harmony (tanāsuq wa-insijām) says that the night and the day at the start of the surah fit the two kinds of people it speaks of later: those who give and those who hold back. That is al-Jadwal’s reading, and this lesson does not weigh it.

The pages we read do not say why the Quran swears, and this lesson does not say either. It reads how an oath is built.

<Block id="m1" title="Mistake 1: every wāw at the start of a verse is an oath wāw">
  <Stage label="One oath wāw, then joining wāws">
    <Tiles
      label="the first two verses of Ash-Shams"
      items={[
        { ar: 'وَٱلشَّمْسِ', en: '91:1: the oath wāw', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'وَٱلْقَمَرِ', en: '91:2: a joining wāw', type: 'n' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

In a chain of oaths, **only the first** wāw is the oath letter. In Ash-Shams the wāw of 91:1 is tagged as a preposition in the Corpus, and the wāws of 91:2, 91:3, 91:4, 91:5, 91:6 and 91:7 are tagged “and”. The nouns after them are genitive because they are joined (L2.06). The same is true in 89:1 to 89:4 and 95:1 to 95:3.

The case of the noun cannot tell you. A joined noun after a genitive noun is genitive too. Ask what stands before the wāw.

The verse after an oath is not always its answer. In 89:5 a question follows the oath. Al-Jadwal calls هَلْ a question word for confirming and for making the listener agree, and calls the sentence a new start with no place. The answer of 89:1 to 89:4 is left out, as the block “When no answer is spoken” showed.

Look for the answer by its shape: إِنَّ with a لَ, لَقَدْ, مَا, a verb with لَ and the nūn, or إِن with لَمَّا. If none is there, the answer may be left out.

<Block id="m3" title="Mistake 3: every لَ is the same لَ">
  <Stage label="Two lāms in one verse">

100:6 has two lāms. The first, in لِرَبِّهِۦ, is the preposition **li-**: it has a kasra, and a noun follows it (L2.07). The Corpus tags it as a preposition. The second, in لَكَنُودٌ, is the lām that strengthens the khabar: it has a fatḥa and a noun follows it. The Corpus tags it as emphasis. Al-Jadwal calls it the slid lām or the lām of the oath.

The lām of لَقَدْ is a third place for the same lām: it starts an answer and changes no ending (L4.06).

The English “He who made” is one reading. “The making of” is the other. Say which one you are giving.

<Exercise n={2} q="Which wāw is the oath wāw? وَٱلشَّمْسِ وَضُحَىٰهَا (Ash-Shams 91:1) وَٱلْقَمَرِ إِذَا تَلَىٰهَا (Ash-Shams 91:2) وَٱلنَّهَارِ إِذَا جَلَّىٰهَا (Ash-Shams 91:3) وَٱلَّيْلِ إِذَا يَغْشَىٰهَا (Ash-Shams 91:4). Count the oath wāws and the joining wāws.">

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Why is the wāw of وَضُحَىٰهَا (Ash-Shams 91:1, part) not an oath wāw?">

It joins ضُحَىٰهَا to ٱلشَّمْسِ, which is already sworn by. The Corpus tags it “and”, and the oath wāw is the first wāw of the verse. ضُحَىٰهَا is genitive because it copies the case of ٱلشَّمْسِ (L2.06). The kasra does not show, because the word ends in an alif maqṣūra. The ـهَا is the second word of an idafah: “its brightness”.

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="an oath letter, a noun in the genitive and an answer make an oath"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'an oath letter', small: 'وَ, بِ or تَ' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'a noun in the genitive', small: 'or a clause with مَا' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'the answer', small: 'spoken or left out' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- An oath has **three parts**: an oath letter, a sworn-by noun and an answer. The sworn-by noun is genitive after the letter. The phrase is tied to a left-out “I swear” (al-Jadwal).
- The Corpus tags **28** oath wāws and **9** tāʾ words, all with ٱللَّهِ, and no bāʾ. It misses at least five oath wāws, so the real counts are higher. At least 21 of the 114 surahs open with an oath wāw.
- In a chain, only the **first** wāw is the oath letter. The later ones join, and their nouns are genitive because they are joined. فَ joins in the same way.
- A sworn-by item can be a noun, a noun with a describer, an idafah, a doer word or a clause with مَا. مَا has two readings in al-Jadwal.
- The answer in this lesson starts with **إِنَّ and a لَ** (4 of 10 sections), **لَقَدْ** (1), **مَا** (1), **إِن with لَمَّا** (1), or it is **left out** (3). The answer can wait several verses, and sentences can be joined to it.
- A surah can hold more than one oath (51:1 to 51:5 and 51:7 to 51:8).
- The pages we read do not say why the Quran swears. This lesson reads how an oath is built.
