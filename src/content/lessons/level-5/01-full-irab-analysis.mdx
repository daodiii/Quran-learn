This verse has four words. You can already say a lot about it.

The first word is a command, and the one told is a hidden “you” (L3.08). The second word is what the command is done to: the object (L2.03). The third word completes an idafah, “name of your Lord” (L2.08). The fourth word describes the Lord (L2.09).

That is a full reading, and every part came from Levels 1 to 4. **Iʿrāb** is the name for saying all of it, word by word, in a fixed order. Level 5 does this for whole surahs. This lesson gives the order.

After the five moves, two extras are open to you: the root and shape of a word (ṣarf), and style (balāgha). Step 4 shows when to use them.

<Note kind="know">The five moves are a teaching summary. They are Quran Learn’s order, built from Levels 1 to 4. No source in this project gives a numbered method, and this lesson does not say that a classical book does. Al-Jadwal’s pages for longer verses do have a shape: the iʿrāb (the jobs of words and the places of sentences) comes first, then ṣarf, balāgha and extra notes. The five moves fill the first part. The two extras are the next two.</Note>

Move 1 cuts the verse in two ways.

1. **Into words.** The Corpus writes this verse as five words.
2. **Into pieces.** A written word can hold more than one piece. Cut out a piece when it has a job of its own.

أَنزَلْنَٰهُ has three pieces. The verb is أَنزَلْ, “sent down”. The ending -nā is the doer, “we” (L3.05). The ending -hu is the object, “it” (L3.02). إِنَّآ has two: the little word inna (L2.10) and a joined -nā, “we”.

Little words that stick to the front of a word are pieces too: وَ, فَ, لَ, بِ, لِ and the article ٱلْ (L1.04, L1.06).

<Note kind="tip">Check your cuts in the Quranic Arabic Corpus. It splits each word into pieces and tags each piece. It is a help and not the last word: it gives no sentence roles, and some of its tags are wrong.</Note>

Look at the first word. Skip any little word in front of it (L1.09). Then ask: verb or noun?

- **A verb** makes a **verbal** sentence (L2.03). It has a verb, a doer and often an object. 87:1 is one.
- **A noun** makes a **nominal** sentence (L2.01). It has a mubtadaʾ, the starting point, and a khabar, the news.

11:4 and 97:1 are nominal, and each has a twist. The next block shows both.

**11:4: a phrase first.** إِلَى ٱللَّهِ is a preposition and its noun (L2.07). It is not a verb, and this sentence has no verb. So it is nominal. The phrase is the khabar, put first, and the mubtadaʾ is مَرْجِعُكُمْ (L2.01).

**97:1: inna first.** Skip it, as move 2 says. What comes next is the joined -nā, a pronoun. The sentence is nominal: the ism of inna is the -nā, and the khabar of inna is a whole verbal sentence, أَنزَلْنَٰهُ and what belongs to it (L2.10, and the kinds of khabar in L2.01). The verb sits inside the khabar. It does not make the whole verse verbal.

**Two sentences.** One verse can hold more than one. 11:4 has two, and the second starts after وَ. Run move 2 on each.

Move 3 is about links. A word has a job because of another word, so for each word ask what it belongs to. The six questions above cover every job in the four verses of this step.

The answer is not always a single word. A verb can carry its doer and its object inside it, as أَنزَلْنَٰهُ does. A phrase can be tied to a word that is left out, as the first phrase of 11:4 is. A joining word can join whole sentences.

Moves 3 and 4 go together on the cards of the next four blocks. Each card also has a line in the words of the Arabic grammar books, under the English. The last card of each verse is move 5.

Three things help you read the cards.

- **A joined pronoun** shows no ending, so its case is “by place”. It counts as nominative, accusative or genitive, and nothing is written.
- **A verb has no case.** A past verb and a command are **built** (مَبْنِي): their last sound is fixed (L3.05, L3.08).
- **A hidden mark** is still there. The word ends in an alif maqṣūra, so the vowel is understood and cannot show (L2.07).

**Move 3.** سَبِّحِ is a command. The doer is a hidden “you”. ٱسْمَ is the object: it says what is glorified. رَبِّكَ and ٱلْأَعْلَى belong to it.

**Move 4.** ٱسْمَ is accusative because it is the object (-a). رَبِّكَ is genitive because it completes the idafah (-i). ٱلْأَعْلَى copies the genitive, but the alif maqṣūra hides the kasra.

**The kasra on سَبِّحِ.** A command is built on a sukūn. Before ٱسْمَ, which starts with a silent letter, the sukūn is read with a kasra.

**Move 5.** Nothing needs this sentence to fill a spot, so it has no place. Al-Jadwal calls it an opening sentence.

L1.10 said that Level 5 would return to the word بِسْمِ. Here is the question, and it is move 3: what is the phrase بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ **tied to**?

A preposition phrase must be tied to something, a verb or a word that works like one. *Al-Durr al-Maṣūn* states this on this very verse, with a short list of exceptions. The verse writes no verb and no news. So the word it is tied to is **left out**, and the books give two kinds of answer.

| Point | The noun view | The verb view |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Al-Jadwal. In *al-Durr al-Maṣūn*, the people of Basra | In *al-Durr al-Maṣūn*, the people of Kufa |

*Al-Durr al-Maṣūn* adds more detail than the table. Some who take a verb put it before the phrase, and others put it after. Some say it is a command and others say it is a statement. This lesson keeps to the two views above.

Whichever view you take, the endings you see are the same: بِسْمِ is genitive after بِ, and ٱللَّهِ is genitive as the second word of the idafah (L1.10). What changes is the left-out word, and with it the kind of sentence. Take **one** view completely.

<Step id="practice" label="Mistakes and practice" title="Eight mistakes, practice and summary">
<Block id="mis-1" title="Mistake 1: all five moves on every word at once">
  <Stage label="Four passes over a verse">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Pass 1:</b> cut the verse, and find the kind.</li>
      <li><b>Pass 2:</b> name the main parts and the ties.</li>
      <li><b>Pass 3:</b> give the endings and the reasons.</li>
      <li><b>Pass 4:</b> place the sentences. Add shape and style if a question needs them.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

A verse can have many words, and each word has a job, a case, a mark and a reason. If you do all of it for word one before you look at word two, you work slowly and you slip. Do one move for the whole verse, then the next. The cards in this lesson went in that order.

You do not need every detail on the first pass. Get the kind, the main parts and the places first. Add the rest when you are sure of those.

</Block>

<Block id="mis-2" title="Mistake 2: skipping move 2">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="Al-Qadr 97:1: a verb inside, a nominal sentence outside"
      items={[
        { ar: 'إِنَّآ', en: 'inna + its ism: the sentence starts here', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'أَنزَلْنَٰهُ', en: 'the verb: inside the khabar', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The verse has a verb, so some learners call it verbal. Move 2 says to look at the **first** word after skipping little words. That word is the joined -nā, a pronoun, so the sentence is nominal. The verb belongs to the khabar of inna.

The same care applies to a sentence that starts with a phrase, as in 11:4. A phrase is not a verb.

Skipping move 2 causes another slip. A noun at the start of a nominal sentence is a mubtadaʾ. The same noun at the start of a verbal sentence may be an object put first (L2.03). Only the kind of sentence tells you which.

<Check n={18} q="97:1 has a verb. Why is it not a verbal sentence?" options={['It starts with inna and its ism, and the verb is inside the khabar', 'Verbs never start a sentence', 'أَنزَلْنَٰهُ is a noun']} answer={0} why="Move 2 looks at the start of the sentence. Inna and its ism come first, so the sentence is nominal." />
</Block>

<Block id="mis-3" title="Mistake 3: not asking what is left out">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="two phrases with nothing written to tie them to"
      items={[
        { ar: 'إِلَى ٱللَّهِ', en: '11:4: tied to a left-out khabar, a word like “is”' },
        { ar: 'بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ', en: '1:1: tied to a left-out noun or verb' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Some phrases have no word written to be tied to. When the parse will not work, ask: is a word left out? The books name the word. Al-Jadwal does this for 11:4, and both al-Jadwal and *al-Durr al-Maṣūn* do it for the Bismillah.

The mistake has two sides. One is to miss a left-out word and to force the phrase onto a word that does not fit. The other is to add left-out words for yourself. When a sentence is complete, nothing is left out (L4.18). Take the word from the book, not from a hunch.

<Check n={19} q="A phrase has no written word to be tied to. What do you ask?" options={['Is a word left out, and which one do the books name?', 'Can I delete the phrase?', 'Can I make it the doer?']} answer={0} why="Many phrases are tied to a left-out word, such as a khabar like “is”. The books say which word." />
</Block>

<Block id="mis-4" title="Mistake 4: following the English">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="Hud 11:4 and Al-Qadr 97:1: words the English needs"
      items={[
        { ar: 'إِلَى ٱللَّهِ مَرْجِعُكُمْ', en: 'English: “To Allah is your return”. The Arabic has no word for “is”' },
        { ar: 'أَنزَلْنَٰهُ', en: 'English: “we sent it down”. The Arabic has one word', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

English needs “is”, “we” and “it” as separate words. Arabic writes them inside the verb or leaves them out. If you cut the English, you look for a verb in 11:4 and find none, and you count three words in أَنزَلْنَٰهُ and find one.

Cut the Arabic. Use the English only for the meaning. The meaning lines in this course mark a missing word with a gap, as in “To Allah ⟨is⟩ your return”, so you can see what Arabic leaves out.

<Check n={20} q="The English of 11:4 says “is”. Does the Arabic have a verb for it?" options={['No: a nominal sentence needs no verb', 'Yes, إِلَى', 'Yes, مَرْجِعُكُمْ']} answer={0} why="Arabic does not write “is” in a nominal sentence (L2.01). The English word is a gap that English needs." />
</Block>

<Block id="mis-5" title="Mistake 5: stopping at the first sentence">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="Hud 11:4: two sentences in one verse"
      items={[
        { ar: 'إِلَى ٱللَّهِ مَرْجِعُكُمْ', en: 'sentence 1: a new start' },
        { ar: 'وَهُوَ', en: 'sentence 2 begins here: joined to sentence 1' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A verse is not always one sentence. 11:4 has two, and each has its own mubtadaʾ, khabar and place. If you stop after the first, you miss the pronoun هُوَ, the second khabar and the join.

Mark where a sentence ends before you start move 3. A new subject, a new verb or a وَ at the start are signs of a new sentence. They are not proof, so check the books.

<Check n={21} q="How many sentences does Hud 11:4 hold?" options={['Two', 'One', 'Three']} answer={0} why="The first ends at مَرْجِعُكُمْ. The second starts at وَهُوَ and has its own mubtadaʾ, هُوَ." />
</Block>

<Block id="mis-6" title="Mistake 6: a reason that is not a rule">
  <Stage label="Test the reason, then use it">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>A tempting reason:</b> “It cannot be an adjective, because an adjective cannot be the first word of an idafah.”</li>
      <li><b>The test:</b> find a verse where it happens.</li>
      <li><b>The result:</b> al-Jadwal reads رَبِّ in 1:2 as an adjective of ٱللَّهِ, and ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ as its second word.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

The reason sounds fair, but it is wrong. The word رَبِّ in Al-Fatiha 1:2 is the first word of an idafah, and al-Jadwal still reads it as an adjective of ٱللَّهِ. L5.02 goes through the verse.

So do not turn a hunch into a rule. If a reason rules out a reading, find the source for the reason first. If you cannot, give the reading and name the other.

<Check n={22} q="A reason says: “an adjective cannot be the first word of an idafah.” What do you do first?" options={['Test it on a verse and find its source', 'Accept it, because it sounds right', 'Drop al-Jadwal’s reading of 1:2']} answer={0} why="Al-Jadwal reads رَبِّ in 1:2 as an adjective that is also the first word of an idafah. A reason needs a source before it rules anything out." />
</Block>

<Block id="mis-7" title="Mistake 7: reading the shape and not the word">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="the same letters, two kinds of word"
      items={[
        { ar: 'أَعْلَى', en: 'without ٱلْ it could be a Form IV past verb', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'ٱلْأَعْلَى', en: 'with ٱلْ it is a noun or adjective' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A shape can look like a verb or like a doer word, and be neither. ٱلْأَعْلَى has the article ٱلْ, and a verb never takes it, so it is an adjective. The Corpus tags it that way. L3.13 shows the same trap with أَحْسَنَ and أَحْسَنُ.

Another case: ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ in 1:2. Its shape resembles a doer word, and some learners call it an active participle. The Corpus tags it as a plain noun, and al-Jadwal says ʿālam is a collective noun with no singular from its own letters.

Check the word in the Corpus and al-Jadwal before you name its type.

<Check n={23} q="Why is ٱلْأَعْلَى not a verb, although أَعْلَى can be one?" options={['It has the article ٱلْ, and a verb never takes it', 'It ends in ىٰ', 'It has a kasra']} answer={0} why="The article ٱلْ marks a noun or an adjective. The Corpus tags ٱلْأَعْلَى as an adjective." />
</Block>

<Block id="mis-8" title="Mistake 8: mixing two views">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="the Bismillah: do not take half of each view"
      items={[
        { ar: 'بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ', en: 'noun view: nominal, “my beginning” left out' },
        { ar: 'بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ', en: 'verb view: verbal, “I begin” left out', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A common slip is to call the Bismillah “a nominal sentence with a left-out verb”. That mixes the two views. With a left-out verb the sentence is verbal. With a left-out noun it is nominal.

Pick one view for the whole parse, say which one it is, and name the other.

<Check n={24} q="Someone calls the Bismillah “a nominal sentence with a left-out verb”. What is wrong?" options={['The two views are mixed: a left-out verb makes it verbal', 'Nothing is wrong', 'A verb can never be left out']} answer={0} why="With a left-out verb the sentence is verbal. With a left-out noun, “my beginning”, it is nominal." />
</Block>

<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: the first three moves">
  <Stage label="For each verse, say">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Cut:</b> the pieces, and the words.</li>
      <li><b>Kind:</b> verbal or nominal?</li>
      <li><b>Jobs:</b> the main parts, and what each phrase is tied to.</li>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Cut each verse into its pieces and say whether it is verbal or nominal. (a) فَصَلِّ لِرَبِّكَ وَٱنْحَرْ (Al-Kawthar 108:2) (b) إِنَّآ أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ ٱلْكَوْثَرَ (Al-Kawthar 108:1) (c) وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:218, end)">

| Verse | Pieces | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | فَ (joins), صَلِّ (a command, hidden doer “you”), لِ + رَبِّ + كَ, وَ (joins), ٱنْحَرْ (a command) | Verbal: after the joining فَ the first word is a verb |
| (b) | إِنَّ + نَا, then the verb أَعْطَيْ + نَا (doer) + كَ (object), then ٱلْ + كَوْثَر | Nominal: it starts with inna. The khabar of inna is the sentence أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ ٱلْكَوْثَرَ |
| (c) | وَ, then ٱللَّهُ, غَفُورٌ and رَحِيمٌ | Nominal: ٱللَّهُ is the mubtadaʾ, غَفُورٌ the khabar and رَحِيمٌ a second khabar |

In (a) there are two sentences, one for each command. In (b) the verb sits inside the khabar, as in 97:1.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="What is each phrase tied to? (a) لِرَبِّكَ (Al-Kawthar 108:2) (b) فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ (Al-Baqarah 2:218, part) (c) بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ (An-Nasr 110:3, part)">

- **(a)** The command صَلِّ. The phrase says for whom the prayer is. Al-Jadwal ties it to صَلِّ.
- **(b)** The verb جَٰهَدُوا۟, which comes earlier in the verse. Al-Jadwal ties it there.
- **(c)** You might say the command سَبِّحْ. Al-Jadwal ties the phrase to a left-out state of the doer of سَبِّحْ: “while joined with praise”. That is a ḥāl (L4.01). The meaning stays close, but the tie is different.

Ask “what question does the phrase answer?”, and then check the books. Do not stop at the nearest verb.

</Exercise>

|---|---|---|

| ٱلْقَدْرِ | Genitive | -i: the second word of an idafah |

<Exercise n={4} q="Take apart فَصَلِّ لِرَبِّكَ وَٱنْحَرْ (Al-Kawthar 108:2) with all five moves.">

|---|---|

<Exercise n={5} q="Look at these two verses: إِذَا زُلْزِلَتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ زِلْزَالَهَا (Az-Zalzalah 99:1) and وَأَخْرَجَتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ أَثْقَالَهَا (Az-Zalzalah 99:2). Which clause is added to إِذَا, what place does it have, and what place does the sentence of 99:2 have?">

The clause زُلْزِلَتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ زِلْزَالَهَا is added to إِذَا, as the first word of an idafah is added to the second. Al-Jadwal puts it in the place of the genitive.

The sentence of 99:2 is joined to that clause, so it takes the same place: the place of the genitive.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="The Bismillah بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ (Al-Fatiha 1:1) writes no verb. Give the two views on what بِسْمِ is tied to, and the kind of sentence each makes. Then say what is wrong with: “It is a nominal sentence with a left-out verb.”">

- **Noun view:** the phrase is tied to a left-out khabar, and the left-out topic is ٱبْتِدَائِي, “my beginning”. The sentence is nominal. This is al-Jadwal’s reading.
- **Verb view:** the phrase is tied to a left-out verb, such as “I begin”. The sentence is verbal.

The claim mixes the two views. A sentence with a left-out verb is verbal, so it cannot also be nominal. Choose one view and name the other.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Sort each statement: grammar, a reading with a named source, or a claim with no named source. (a) In 11:4, إِلَى ٱللَّهِ is tied to a left-out khabar and put first. (b) In 99:2, al-Jadwal says the act of bringing out is given to the earth as a figure. (c) In 87:1, the command comes first so that it feels urgent. (d) In 87:1, ٱلْأَعْلَى describes رَبِّكَ.">

- **(a) Grammar.** It is al-Jadwal’s tie, and the only way to complete the sentence (L2.07).
- **(b) A named reading.** Al-Jadwal names the figure, *al-isnād al-majāzī*, on 99:1 and 99:2.
- **(c) No named source.** Al-Jadwal prints no style note on 87:1. The lesson says nothing about urgency (L4.17).
- **(d) Grammar.** Al-Jadwal says رَبِّكَ. *Al-Durr al-Maṣūn* allows ٱسْمَ too.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="The word ٱلْأَعْلَى (Al-Aʿla 87:1) ends in an alif maqṣūra. (a) Why can its ending not say which noun it describes? (b) Give both readings and a plain meaning for each. (c) Which one does al-Jadwal give?">

**(a)** The alif maqṣūra hides the vowel. A genitive and an accusative look the same on it, so the ending cannot choose between رَبِّكَ (genitive) and ٱسْمَ (accusative).

**(b)** Genitive, describing رَبِّكَ: “Glorify the name of your Lord, the Most High.” Accusative, describing ٱسْمَ: “Glorify the most high name of your Lord.” Both meanings are ours.

**(c)** Al-Jadwal gives the first: the adjective of رَبِّكَ, genitive, with a hidden kasra.

</Exercise>

  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="a verse plus five moves equals its iʿrāb"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'a verse', small: 'any verse' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'five moves', small: 'in one order' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'iʿrāb', small: 'إِعْرَاب' },
      ]}

- The five moves are **cut**, **kind**, **jobs**, **endings** and **place**. They are a teaching order built from Levels 1 to 4, not a method from a classical book.
- 87:1 showed a verb with an object. 97:1 showed a verb with a phrase tied to it. 11:4 showed two nominal sentences. 99:2 showed a written doer and a sentence with a place.
- Where books differ, give the main reading, name the other and keep each whole. The Bismillah has a noun view and a verb view. ٱلْأَعْلَى in 87:1 has two readings, and a hidden ending cannot choose.
- Add shape and style only when a question needs them. Style comes only from a named book.
