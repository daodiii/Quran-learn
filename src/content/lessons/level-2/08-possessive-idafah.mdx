This is the start of a verse about the Prophet and his companions. Read it from right to left.

Look at the second and third words. رَسُولُ ٱللَّهِ means “the messenger **of** Allah”. Arabic has no word for “of” here. The two nouns just stand side by side, and the second one ends in **-i**.

That is an **idafah**. The first noun is the thing, and the second noun says whose it is or what it belongs to.

</Block>

The Arabic name is إِضَافَة (*iḍāfa*), “adding”. The second noun is added to the first, and together they make one idea.

- The first word is the **muḍāf**, مُضَاف: “the thing that is added”. Here it is رَسُولُ.
- The second word is the **muḍāf ilayh**, مُضَاف إِلَيْه: “the one it is added to”. Here it is ٱللَّهِ.

English has two ways to say this: “the messenger **of** Allah” and “Allah**’s** messenger”. Arabic has one way: put the two nouns together.

You met the idafah in L2.06, as the second way a noun becomes genitive. L2.07 showed it inside prepositional phrases, such as بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ, “with the name of Allah”. This lesson teaches it in full.

<Note kind="remember">The idafah is two nouns in a row. English adds “of”. Arabic adds nothing.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="rules" title="The first word loses two things">
  <Stage label="Alone, and in an idafah">
    <Tiles

        { ar: 'ٱلْكِتَابُ', en: 'the book' },
        { ar: 'كِتَابُ ٱلطَّالِبِ', en: 'the student’s book' },
      ]}
    />
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Made-up example.</p>

Look at the word “book” on its own, and then in an idafah.

1. **No tanwīn.** An indefinite noun ends in -un: كِتَابٌ, “a book”. As the first word of an idafah, it drops the “n”: كِتَابُ.
2. **No *al-*.** A definite noun starts with *al-*: ٱلْكِتَابُ, “the book”. As the first word of an idafah, it does not take *al-*: كِتَابُ.

The second word is different. It keeps its *al-* or its tanwīn, and it is **always genitive**: ٱلطَّالِبِ ends in -i.

So the first word is bare, and the second word carries the idafah's ending. The example words here are made up, to show the shape.

<Note kind="warn">These two rules are for ordinary nouns. A few verses break the *al-* rule, with words that come from verbs. You will see one in Step 4. This course does not teach that special case.</Note>

The first word has no *al-*, so it cannot be definite on its own. It takes its definiteness from the second word.

- ٱللَّهِ is a name, so it is definite. So the whole idafah is definite: “**the** messenger of Allah”.
- يَوْمٍ ends in -in, so it is indefinite. So the whole idafah is indefinite: “**a** punishment of a day”.

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* lists five kinds of definite noun: a pronoun, a name, a pointing word like هَٰذَا, a noun with *al-*, and the last one in the line above: “what is added to one of these four”. That is the idafah.

The word عَظِيمٍ, “terrible”, is an adjective. It comes **after** both nouns of the idafah and describes the day. L2.09 teaches adjectives.

</Block>

All three phrases come from one verse. Each is an idafah. Look at the **first** word of each.

- رَبُّ ends in **-u**. It is the doer of the verb, so it is nominative (L2.03).
- عَمَلَ ends in **-a**. It is the object of the verb “I do not waste”, so it is accusative (L2.05).
- سَبِيلِ ends in **-i**. It comes after the preposition فِى, so it is genitive (L2.07).

So the first word of an idafah is **not** always genitive. It has the case its own job in the sentence needs. In L2.06 you saw the same thing with made-up words.

In the second phrase, عَٰمِلٍ is the second word and ends in -in. In the third, the ى at the end of سَبِيلِى is the pronoun “my”. It is the second word. Both are genitive.

The second word is always genitive, but the **sign** of the genitive changes with the kind of word (L2.06):

| Second word | Its genitive sign | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Singular, with *al-* | -i | ٱللَّهِ |
| Singular, without *al-* | -in | يَوْمٍ، عَٰمِلٍ |
| A plural that is not “sound” | -in or -i | أَشْهُرٍ |
| Sound masculine plural | -īna | ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ |
| Dual | -ayni | ٱلْوَالِدَيْنِ |
| Sound feminine plural | -āti (a kasra) | ٱلطَّالِبَاتِ |
| A joined pronoun | no sign: it counts as genitive | هُمْ، هُ، ى |

The dual and the feminine plural in the table are made-up forms, to complete the list. The other examples are from verses in this lesson.

<Note kind="remember">The second word of an idafah is genitive. The sign depends on the word. It is not always -i.</Note>

</Block>

A joined pronoun can be the second word. Then the two parts are **one written word**: رَبُّهُمْ, “their Lord”. The first part is the muḍāf. The pronoun is the muḍāf ilayh.

The pronoun cannot show a case, but it counts as genitive: it is “in the place of” the genitive (L2.06). This is how the verses say “my Lord”, “their Lord”, “your Lord”.

Two things to watch:

- **The first word stays bare.** رَبُّ has no *al-* and no tanwīn. The pronoun makes it definite, because a pronoun is definite.
- **“My” hides the first word's ending.** The pronoun ـِي needs a kasra on the letter before it. So in رَبِّي you cannot see whether the first word is nominative, accusative or genitive. In 6:15 it is the object, so it is accusative. Al-Jadwal says the fatḥa is **understood** on the letter before the ي.

An idafah can be the second part of another idafah. That makes a **chain**. Here there are three nouns and two links:

- رَبُّ كُلِّ: “the Lord of every …”
- كُلِّ شَىْءٍ: “every thing”

The middle word, كُلِّ, does two jobs at once. It is the second word of the first link, so it is **genitive**. It is the first word of the second link, so it is **bare**: no *al-*, no tanwīn.

Only the **last** word can have *al-* or tanwīn. Here شَىْءٍ has the tanwīn, so the whole chain is indefinite: “the Lord of everything”.

The middle word has only **one** reason for its case: it is genitive because it is a second word. Being a first word gives it no extra case.

A dual noun ends in -āni (nominative) or -ayni (accusative and genitive). L2.04 and L2.05 taught these. The ending has a nūn at the end.

When a dual noun is the **first word of an idafah**, the nūn **drops**. يَدَانِ, “two hands”, becomes يَدَا, and the pronoun follows: يَدَاهُ, “his two hands”.

Look at the verse. يَدَاهُ is the mubtadaʾ, and it is nominative. Its sign is the alif of the dual. Al-Jadwal adds that “the nūn is dropped for the idafah”. The pronoun هُ is the muḍāf ilayh.

The khabar, مَبْسُوطَتَانِ, is also a dual. It is **not** the first word of an idafah, so it **keeps** its nūn.

</Block>

The same happens with the sound masculine plural. Its full form ends in **-ūna** (nominative) or **-īna** (accusative and genitive). As the first word of an idafah, it loses the nūn.

In this verse, مُعْجِزِينَ becomes مُعْجِزِى. The word is the second word of the idafah غَيْرُ مُعْجِزِى, so it is genitive. The sign is the **yāʾ**. Al-Jadwal says: “genitive, with the yāʾ as its sign, and the nūn is dropped for the idafah”. And مُعْجِزِى is itself the first word of the idafah مُعْجِزِى ٱللَّهِ, which is why the nūn drops.

In L2.04 you saw the nominative case of this: مُّلَٰقُوا۟ رَبِّهِمْ in Al-Baqarah 2:46, with a wāw and no nūn.

The last word of the whole chain keeps its ending in full. ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ, in the earlier block, keeps its nūn, because nothing is added to it.

<Block id="kinds" title="Two kinds of idafah, in the first grammar book">
  <Stage label="From al-Ajurrumiyyah">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote" lang="ar" dir="rtl">
      <p>وَأَمَّا مَا يُخْفَضُ بِالْإِضَافَةِ فَنَحْوُ قَوْلِكَ: غُلَامُ زَيْدٍ</p>
      <p>وَهُوَ عَلَى قِسْمَيْنِ: مَا يُقَدَّرُ بِاللَّامِ وَمَا يُقَدَّرُ بِمِنْ</p>
      <p>فَالَّذِي يُقَدَّرُ بِاللَّامِ نَحْوُ غُلَامُ زَيْدٍ</p>
      <p>وَالَّذِي يُقَدَّرُ بِمِنْ نَحْوُ ثَوْبُ خَزٍّ وَبَابُ سَاجٍ وَخَاتَمُ حَدِيدٍ</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>
    <Sentence
      reference="Al-Kahf 18:96 (start)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'ءَاتُونِى', en: 'bring me', say: 'atuni', type: 'v' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'زُبَرَ', en: 'pieces of', say: 'zubara' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْحَدِيدِ', en: 'the iron', say: 'al-hadidi' }] },
      ]}
      en={['Bring me pieces of iron.']}
    />
  </Stage>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* says that the idafah is of **two kinds**. The book says the idafah is “understood with” one of two little words. Try each between the two nouns, and see which one the meaning wants.

1. **With lām (*li-*, “for, belonging to”).** غُلَامُ زَيْدٍ, “Zayd's boy”, means “a boy **belonging to** Zayd”. The second word owns, or has, the first. In this lesson, يَدَاهُ, “his two hands”, reads well this way: two hands belonging to him.
2. **With *min* (“of, made from”).** ثَوْبُ خَزٍّ, “a garment of silk”, means a garment **made from** silk. The second word is the material. In the verse, زُبَرَ ٱلْحَدِيدِ, “pieces of iron”, is this kind.

The book's other examples are بَابُ سَاجٍ, “a door of teak”, and خَاتَمُ حَدِيدٍ, “a ring of iron”.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal gives the role of زُبَرَ in this verse (the second object of “bring me”), but it does not name ٱلْحَدِيدِ's role. The reading above is the plain one: a genitive noun after a noun, an idafah.</Note>

</Block>

<Block id="recognise" title="How to spot an idafah">
  <Stage label="Four quick questions">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Two nouns in a row?</b> One thing, then another.</li>
      <li><b>Is the first word bare?</b> No <i>al-</i>, no tanwīn.</li>
      <li><b>Is the second word genitive?</b> -i, -in, -īna, a yāʾ, or a pronoun.</li>
      <li><b>Does the meaning fit “the X of Y”?</b> Try “belonging to” or “made of”.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

If all four answers are yes, it is an idafah. Then do the next thing: find the **case of the first word** from its job in the sentence. The second word needs no work. It is genitive.

When a chain has three or four nouns, work from the right. The last word is the second word of the last link. The word before it is its first word, and it may also be the second word of the link before that.
</Block>
</Step>

<Block id="mistake-al" title="Mistake 1: putting al- or tanwīn on the first word">

    <Tiles

    <Sentence

This is the most common mistake. The first word of an idafah does not take *al-* and does not take tanwīn. Its definiteness comes from the second word.

If you see *al-* on the first of two nouns, the usual meaning is a noun with an adjective: ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلْجَدِيدُ, “the new book”. That is Mistake 4.

<Note kind="know">The verse above looks like an exception. ٱلْمُقِيمِى has *al-*, and ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ is its muḍāf ilayh (al-Jadwal). The nūn has dropped too: the full word is ٱلْمُقِيمِينَ. This happens with a word that comes from a verb, like “one who keeps up”. It is a special case that this course does not teach. For ordinary nouns, the rule holds.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-between" title="Mistake 2: putting a word between the two nouns">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="wrong and right"
      items={[
        { ar: 'كِتَابُ ٱلْجَدِيدُ ٱلطَّالِبِ', en: 'wrong: a word in the middle' },
        { ar: 'كِتَابُ ٱلطَّالِبِ ٱلْجَدِيدُ', en: 'right: the student’s new book (-u matches the book)' },
      ]}
    />
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Made-up examples.</p>
  </Stage>

The two nouns of an idafah stay together. An adjective goes **after** the whole idafah, never between the two nouns.

In the verse you saw, عَذَابَ يَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ, the adjective عَظِيمٍ comes after يَوْمٍ and copies its -in ending. Al-Jadwal says it is the adjective of يَوْمٍ: “a punishment of a terrible day”. Which noun an adjective belongs to is decided by its ending and its meaning. L2.09 teaches this.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-nun" title="Mistake 3: keeping the nūn">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="wrong and right"
      items={[
        { ar: 'يَدَانِ ٱللَّهِ', en: 'wrong: nūn kept' },

        { ar: 'مُعْجِزِى ٱللَّهِ', en: 'right: nūn dropped' },
      ]}
    />
    <p class="lx-quote-note">The first and third are made-up wrong forms. The second is the made-up pair from before; the fourth is in 9:2.</p>

A dual or a sound masculine plural that is the first word of an idafah loses its nūn: يَدَانِ becomes يَدَا, and مُعْجِزِينَ becomes مُعْجِزِى.

The same word loses nothing when it is the last word: مَبْسُوطَتَانِ in 5:64 and ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ in 9:2 keep their nūn.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-adj" title="Mistake 4: an idafah, or a noun and its adjective?">
  <Stage>

    <Sentence

Two nouns side by side may be an idafah, or a noun with its adjective. Two tests tell them apart:

| Test | Idafah | Noun and adjective |
|---|---|---|
| First word has *al-*? | No | Yes, if the phrase is definite |
| Second word's ending | Always genitive | The same as the first word |
| Meaning | “the X of Y” | “the Y X” (a quality) |

So كِتَابُ ٱلطَّالِبِ has endings -u and -i: not the same, so an idafah. ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلْجَدِيدُ has -u and -u, and both have *al-*: a noun and its adjective.

</Block>

<Block id="mistake-pron" title="Mistake 5: forgetting the pronoun is the second word">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="wrong and right"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلرَّبُّهُمْ', en: 'wrong: al- on the first word' },

      ]}
    />
    <p class="lx-quote-note">The first is a made-up wrong form.</p>

رَبُّهُمْ looks like one word. It is an idafah with two parts, and the pronoun is the second part. So the first part follows the same rules: no *al-*, no tanwīn.

Learners also try to give the pronoun a case ending. It has none. It counts as genitive in its place, but it never shows an ending. Do not look for -i on هُمْ.

The same goes for the first word with “my”: رَبِّي, “my Lord”. The ـِي hides the ending of the first word. You use the sentence to find its case, as in 6:15.
</Block>

<Step id="practice" label="Practice" title="Practice">
<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: find and explain">
  <Stage label="For each idafah, say">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>First word:</b> which word? Is it bare?</li>
      <li><b>Second word:</b> which word? Which genitive sign?</li>
      <li><b>First word's case:</b> from its job in the sentence.</li>
      <li><b>Definite or indefinite?</b> Look at the second word.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Find the idafah, and name the first and the second word: وَٱللَّهُ عِندَهُۥ حُسْنُ ٱلثَّوَابِ (Al-Imran 3:195, end). Meaning: “And with Allah is the best reward.”">

The idafah is **حُسْنُ ٱلثَّوَابِ**, “the best of the reward”.

- **حُسْنُ** is the first word. It is bare, and it ends in -u because it is the mubtadaʾ muʾakhkhar, the delayed starting point (L2.01).
- **ٱلثَّوَابِ** is the second word. It is genitive, -i. It is definite, so the whole idafah is definite.

The pronoun in عِندَهُۥ is another second word: عِندَ is the first word of a small idafah, “with him”, and the pronoun is its second word.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Say the case of each first word: (a) رَبُّهُمْ (b) عَمَلَ (c) سَبِيلِى. They come from Al-Imran 3:195. Give the reason for each.">

- (a) **رَبُّهُمْ** is **nominative** (-u). It is the doer of فَٱسْتَجَابَ.
- (b) **عَمَلَ** is **accusative** (-a). It is the object of أُضِيعُ, “I waste”. The sentence says: “I do not waste the work of a worker.”
- (c) **سَبِيلِى** is **genitive** (-i). It comes after the preposition فِى.

The first words have three different cases. Their second words (هُمْ, عَٰمِلٍ, ى) are all genitive.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="What happens to the nūn, and why? (a) يَدَاهُ (Al-Maidah 5:64) (b) غَيْرُ مُعْجِزِى ٱللَّهِ (At-Tawbah 9:2, part) (c) إِنَّا مُرْسِلُوا۟ ٱلنَّاقَةِ (Al-Qamar 54:27, start). Meaning of (c): “We are sending the she-camel.”">

- (a) يَدَانِ lost its nūn and became **يَدَا**. It is the first word of an idafah with the pronoun هُ.
- (b) مُعْجِزِينَ lost its nūn and became **مُعْجِزِى**. It is the first word of an idafah with ٱللَّهِ. It is also the second word of غَيْرُ مُعْجِزِى, so it is genitive: the yāʾ shows it.
- (c) مُرْسِلُونَ lost its nūn and became **مُرْسِلُوا**. It is the first word of an idafah with ٱلنَّاقَةِ. It is nominative, so the sign is the wāw (and an alif written after it).

In all three, the rule is the same: a dual or sound plural loses its nūn as the first word of an idafah.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Is the first word nominative, accusative or genitive? Explain: ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُم مَّرْجِعُكُمْ (Al-Anʿam 6:164, part). Meaning: “Then to your Lord is your return.”">

This sentence has two idafahs, and both have a pronoun as the second word.

- **رَبِّكُمْ**: the first word رَبِّ is **genitive**, because it comes after إِلَىٰ. Its second word, كُمْ, counts as genitive too.
- **مَرْجِعُكُمْ**: the first word مَرْجِعُ is **nominative**, -u. It is the mubtadaʾ muʾakhkhar (L2.01), and the phrase إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ is the khabar, put first. Its second word, كُمْ, counts as genitive.

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Exercise n={5} q="Which phrase is an idafah, and which is not? (a) وَٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ (Al-Aʿraf 7:128, end) (b) عَاقِبَةُ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ (made-up) (c) ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلْجَدِيدُ (made-up)">

- (a) **Not an idafah.** There is a preposition, لِ, between the two nouns. It is a nominal sentence: ٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ is the mubtadaʾ and لِلْمُتَّقِينَ is the khabar (L2.01). Also ٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ has *al-*.
- (b) **An idafah.** عَاقِبَةُ is bare, ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ is genitive (-īna). “The end of the God-fearing.”
- (c) **Not an idafah.** The first word has *al-*, and both endings are -u. It is a noun and its adjective.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Is it definite or indefinite? (a) رَّسُولُ ٱللَّهِ (Al-Fath 48:29, part) (b) عَذَابَ يَوْمٍ (Al-Anʿam 6:15, part) (c) رَبُّ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ (Al-Anʿam 6:164, part) (d) رَبُّهُمْ (Al-Imran 3:195, start)">

Look only at the **last** word.

- (a) **Definite.** ٱللَّهِ is a name.
- (b) **Indefinite.** يَوْمٍ has tanwīn.
- (c) **Indefinite.** شَىْءٍ has tanwīn. The whole chain takes its definiteness from the last word.
- (d) **Definite.** The pronoun is definite.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Lām or min? (a) رَبُّ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ (Al-Anʿam 6:164, part) (b) زُبَرَ ٱلْحَدِيدِ (Al-Kahf 18:96, start) (c) خَاتَمُ حَدِيدٍ (d) بَابُ ٱلْبَيْتِ (e) ثَوْبُ قُطْنٍ. Item (c) is from the book. Items (d) and (e) are made-up.">

Put *li-* (“belonging to”) or *min* (“made from”) between the two nouns.

- (a) **Lām.** A Lord for every thing: رَبٌّ لِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ.
- (b) **Min.** Pieces made from iron (Al-Kahf 18:96).
- (c) **Min.** A ring made from iron. It is the book's own example.
- (d) **Lām.** The door belongs to the house.
- (e) **Min.** A garment made from cotton.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Each of these has one mistake. Fix it, and say which rule it breaks. (a) ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلطَّالِبِ (b) كِتَابٌ ٱلطَّالِبِ (c) كِتَابُ ٱلطَّالِبُ (d) يَدَانِ زَيْدٍ. All four are made-up.">

- (a) Right form: **كِتَابُ ٱلطَّالِبِ**. The first word cannot have *al-*.
- (b) Right form: **كِتَابُ ٱلطَّالِبِ**. The first word cannot have tanwīn.
- (c) Right form: **كِتَابُ ٱلطَّالِبِ**. The second word must be genitive, not -u.
- (d) Right form: **يَدَا زَيْدٍ**. A dual that is the first word of an idafah loses its nūn.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Say the role and the case of every word: وَأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ مُخْزِى ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ (At-Tawbah 9:2, end). Meaning: “…and that Allah will shame the unbelievers.”">

- أَنَّ: a little word that goes with ٱللَّهَ.
- **ٱللَّهَ**: the ism of anna (L2.10), **accusative**, -a.
- **مُخْزِى**: the khabar of anna, **nominative**, with a hidden -u. It is the first word of an idafah.
- **ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ**: the second word, **genitive**. The sign is the yāʾ (-īna). It keeps its nūn because nothing is added to it.

Al-Jadwal reads this like the sentence before it in the verse (غَيْرُ مُعْجِزِى ٱللَّهِ). The hidden -u on مُخْزِى is the same hidden mark you met on مَوْلَىٰ in L2.01.

</Exercise>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="first word plus second word equals an idafah"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'first word', small: 'muḍāf: bare, any case' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'second word', small: 'muḍāf ilayh: genitive' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'idafah', small: 'إِضَافَة' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- An idafah is two nouns in a row that mean “the X of Y”. Arabic adds no word for “of”.
- The **first word** (muḍāf) has no *al-* and no tanwīn. It takes the case its own job needs: nominative, accusative or genitive.
- The **second word** (muḍāf ilayh) is always genitive. The sign depends on the word: -i, -in, -īna, a yāʾ, or nothing on a pronoun.
- The whole idafah is as definite as its second word.
- A pronoun can be the second word: رَبُّهُمْ, رَبِّي.
- In a chain, the middle words are second words and first words at once. Only the last can have *al-* or tanwīn.
- A dual or sound plural that is a first word loses its nūn: يَدَا, مُعْجِزِى.
- *Al-Ajurrumiyyah* names two kinds: with lām (belonging) and with *min* (made from).
