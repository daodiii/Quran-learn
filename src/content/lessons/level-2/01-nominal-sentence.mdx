<Step id="no-is" label="No “is”" title="Arabic leaves out “is”">
  <Stage label="The missing word">
    <Tiles
      label="هُوَ, then nothing, then ٱلْأَوَّلُ: He is the First"
      items={[
        { ar: 'هُوَ', en: 'he' },
        { ghost: 'is', note: 'English only' },
        { ar: 'ٱلْأَوَّلُ', en: 'the First' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

English needs a small word like *is*, *am* or *are*: “The sky <u>is</u> blue.” Arabic does not. It puts the two words side by side, and the reader understands “is”.

So do not look for a hidden verb. Nothing is missing in the Arabic. You add “is” only when you say the sentence in English.

<Note kind="remember">A nominal sentence has no word for “is”. You add it in English.</Note>
</Step>

<Step id="name" label="The name" title="Why it is called a “noun sentence”">
  <Stage label="Said in every prayer">
    <Sentence
      reference="Said in the prayer (not a verse)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'ٱللَّهُ', en: 'Allah', say: 'allahu' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'أَكْبَرُ', en: 'greater', say: 'akbaru' }] },
      ]}
      en={['Allah ', { gap: 'is' }, ' greater than all.']}
    />
  </Stage>

This kind of sentence is called a **nominal sentence**, جُمْلَة اِسْمِيَّة (*jumla ismiyya*). *Ism* means noun. It is a sentence that starts with a noun, and it needs no verb.

You already say one many times a day. ٱللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ is a nominal sentence: two nouns side by side, and English adds “is”.

<Note kind="know">The other kind is the **verbal sentence**, جُمْلَة فِعْلِيَّة, which starts with a verb. You met both in L1.09. L2.03 teaches the verbal sentence.</Note>
</Step>

<Step part="The two parts" id="parts" label="Two parts" title="Every nominal sentence has two parts">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Imran 3:68 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'وَٱللَّهُ', en: 'and Allah', say: 'wa-llahu' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'وَلِىُّ', en: 'protector of', say: 'waliyyu' }, { ar: 'ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ', en: 'the believers', say: 'al-muminina' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And Allah ', { gap: 'is' }, ' the protector of the believers.']}
    />
  </Stage>

- **Mubtadaʾ**, مُبْتَدَأ: “the thing you start with”. It is what the sentence is about. Here it is ٱللَّهُ.
- **Khabar**, خَبَر: “news”. It is what the sentence says about the mubtadaʾ. Here it is وَلِىُّ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ, “the protector of the believers”.

The little word وَ (and) at the front joins this sentence to the one before it in the verse. It is not part of the mubtadaʾ.

<Note kind="know">Many English grammar books say **subject** and **predicate**. They mean roughly the same as mubtadaʾ and khabar. This course uses the Arabic names, because the Arabic grammar books do.</Note>
</Step>

<Step id="rule" label="Both -u" title="The rule: both end in -u">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Imran 3:68 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'وَٱللَّهُ', en: 'and Allah', say: 'wa-llahu' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'وَلِىُّ', en: 'protector of', say: 'waliyyu' }, { ar: 'ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ', en: 'the believers', say: 'al-muminina' }] },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The mubtadaʾ and the khabar are both **nominative**, رَفْع (*rafʿ*). In L1.10 you saw what that looks like: **-u** (a ḍamma, ـُ) or **-un** (ـٌ) at the end.

- ٱللَّهُ ends in -u.
- وَلِىُّ ends in -u. It is the first word of the khabar, so the -u goes on it.

Why nominative? Because of their place in the sentence: one is the starting point, the other is the news about it. The khabar does not copy the mubtadaʾ. Later you will meet words like *inna* and *kāna*. When they come in front of a nominal sentence, the endings change (L2.10, L2.11).

<Note kind="warn">ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ ends in -īna, not -u. It is not the khabar on its own. It means “of the believers” and hangs on وَلِىُّ (an idafah, L2.08).</Note>

<Check n={2} q="Which ending do the mubtadaʾ and the khabar both take?" options={['-u or -un (ḍamma)', '-a or -an (fatḥa)', '-i or -in (kasra)']} answer={0} why="Both are nominative (rafʿ), the -u family." />
</Step>

<Step id="hidden" label="Hidden -u" title="Sometimes you cannot see the -u">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Imran 3:150 (start)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'بَلِ', en: 'no, rather', say: 'bal', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'ٱللَّهُ', en: 'Allah', say: 'allahu' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'مَوْلَىٰكُمْ', en: 'your protector', say: 'mawlakum' }] },
      ]}
      en={['No, Allah ', { gap: 'is' }, ' your Protector.']}
    />
  </Stage>

مَوْلَىٰ (*mawlā*, protector) ends in a long **ā**, written ى. A long ā cannot carry a vowel mark, so the -u cannot be written on it.

The word is still nominative. The grammar books say its ḍamma is **hidden**: *muqaddara*, “understood but not seen”. Al-Jadwal says exactly this about this verse.

<Note kind="warn">A word that ends in ى or ا keeps its case. You just cannot see it. Use the word’s place in the sentence to know its case.</Note>

<Check n={3} q="In ٱللَّهُ مَوْلَىٰكُمْ, which case is مَوْلَىٰ in?" options={['Nominative, with a hidden -u', 'Accusative, because it ends in -ā', 'Genitive']} answer={0} why="It is the khabar, so it is nominative. The -u is hidden because a long ā cannot carry it." />
</Step>

<Step id="pronoun" label="A pronoun" title="A pronoun can be the mubtadaʾ">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Imran 3:150 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'وَهُوَ', en: 'and he', say: 'wa-huwa' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'خَيْرُ', en: 'best of', say: 'khayru' }, { ar: 'ٱلنَّٰصِرِينَ', en: 'the helpers', say: 'an-nasirina' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And He ', { gap: 'is' }, ' the best of helpers.']}
    />
  </Stage>

The same verse goes on with a second nominal sentence. Its mubtadaʾ is هُوَ, “he”.

Pronouns like هُوَ never change their ending. It is still the mubtadaʾ, and it still counts as nominative. The grammar books say it is “in the place of” the nominative (*fī maḥall rafʿ*).

The khabar خَيْرُ ends in -u, as you expect.

<Note kind="know">The first grammar book many students learn, *al-Ajurrumiyyah*, lists twelve pronouns that can be a mubtadaʾ: أَنَا، نَحْنُ، أَنْتَ، أَنْتِ، أَنْتُمَا، أَنْتُمْ، أَنْتُنَّ، هُوَ، هِيَ، هُمَا، هُمْ، هُنَّ.</Note>
</Step>

<Step id="two-khabars" label="Two khabars" title="One mubtadaʾ, two khabars">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="An-Nisa 4:26 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'وَٱللَّهُ', en: 'and Allah', say: 'wa-llahu' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'عَلِيمٌ', en: 'all-knowing', say: 'alimun' }] },
        { role: 'khabar2', words: [{ ar: 'حَكِيمٌ', en: 'all-wise', say: 'hakimun' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And Allah ', { gap: 'is' }, ' All-Knowing, All-Wise.']}
    />
  </Stage>

One mubtadaʾ can have more than one khabar. Here ٱللَّهُ has two: عَلِيمٌ and حَكِيمٌ. Al-Jadwal calls حَكِيمٌ the **second khabar**. Both end in -un.

Look at the two khabars closely. They are built the same way:

- عَلِيم comes from the root ع ل م (knowing).
- حَكِيم comes from the root ح ك م (wisdom, judging).
- Both follow the pattern فَعِيل (*faʿīl*), a common pattern for a word that describes a quality.

<Note kind="know">You will meet roots and patterns properly in Level 3. For now: many names of Allah in the Quran follow فَعِيل, such as رَحِيم and سَمِيع.</Note>
</Step>

  <Stage label="From al-Ajurrumiyyah">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

      <p>وَالْخَبَرُ قِسْمَانِ: مُفْرَدٌ وَغَيْرُ مُفْرَدٍ</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* is a short grammar book by Ibn Ājurrūm (died 723 AH). For centuries it has been the first grammar book many students learn. Its chapter on the mubtadaʾ and the khabar says:

- “The mubtadaʾ is of two kinds: a plain noun and a pronoun.” You have seen both: ٱللَّهُ and هُوَ.
- “The khabar is of two kinds: single and not single.”

The rest of this lesson is about that second line: what a khabar can be.
</Step>

  <Stage>

        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'وَلِىُّ', en: 'protector of' }, { ar: 'ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ', en: 'the believers' }] },
      ]}

Every khabar you have seen so far is **single**, مُفْرَد (*mufrad*).

“Single” does not mean “one word”. وَلِىُّ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ is two words, and it is still a single khabar. Single means: **not a sentence, and not a phrase that starts with a little word** like *li-* or *fī*.

<Note kind="remember">Single (mufrad) means “not a sentence”. It does not mean “one word”.</Note>
</Step>

<Step id="phrase" label="A phrase" title="The khabar can be a phrase">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Aʿraf 7:128 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'وَٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ', en: 'and the good end', say: 'wal-aqibatu' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'لِلْمُتَّقِينَ', en: 'for the God-fearing', say: 'lil-muttaqina', type: 'p' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And the good end ', { gap: 'is' }, ' for those who fear Allah.']}
    />
  </Stage>

Here the khabar is a short phrase: the little word *li-* (for) and a noun, لِلْمُتَّقِينَ. The noun after *li-* is genitive, as you learned in L1.10.

The mubtadaʾ ٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ ends in -u as usual. The khabar does not end in -u, because a phrase like this has no ending of its own.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal words it more exactly: the phrase is “tied to a khabar that is left out”, a word like “is found”. So “the good end is (found) for the God-fearing”. In this course we simply call the phrase the khabar, as *al-Ajurrumiyyah* does.</Note>

<Check n={4} q="In وَٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ, which part is the khabar?" options={['وَٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ', 'لِلْمُتَّقِينَ']} answer={1} why="لِلْمُتَّقِينَ is the news about the good end: it is for the God-fearing." />
</Step>

<Step id="verbal" label="A verb sentence" title="The khabar can be a verbal sentence">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:15 (start)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'ٱللَّهُ', en: 'Allah', say: 'allahu' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', hint: 'a verb with its doer', words: [{ ar: 'يَسْتَهْزِئُ', en: 'mocks', say: 'yastahziu', type: 'v' }, { ar: 'بِهِمْ', en: 'at them', say: 'bihim', type: 'p' }] },
      ]}
      en={['Allah mocks them in return.']}
    />
  </Stage>

The khabar can be a whole **verbal sentence**: a verb with its doer. Here the khabar is يَسْتَهْزِئُ بِهِمْ.

Where is the doer? It is hidden inside the verb: يَسْتَهْزِئُ means “**he** mocks”. So the sentence is: “Allah: He mocks them.”

Notice that the English needs no extra “is” this time. The khabar has its own verb.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal explains in its notes that this is Allah paying them back for their mocking. The payback is given the name of what they did.</Note>
</Step>

<Step id="nominal" label="A noun sentence" title="The khabar can be a nominal sentence">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Imran 3:14 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'وَٱللَّهُ', en: 'and Allah', say: 'wa-llahu' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', hint: 'a whole nominal sentence', words: [{ ar: 'عِندَهُۥ', en: 'with him', say: 'indahu' }, { ar: 'حُسْنُ', en: 'the best of', say: 'husnu' }, { ar: 'ٱلْمَـَٔابِ', en: 'the return', say: 'al-maabi' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And Allah: with Him ', { gap: 'is' }, ' the best place to return to.']}
    />

The khabar can be a whole **nominal sentence**: a sentence inside a sentence.

Here the mubtadaʾ is ٱللَّهُ. The news about Allah is a complete sentence of its own: عِندَهُۥ حُسْنُ ٱلْمَـَٔابِ, “with Him is the best return”.

That inner sentence has its own two parts. You will see how they work in the next part of the lesson.

</Step>

<Step id="place" label="A place word" title="The khabar can be a place or time word">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Anʿam 6:59 (start)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'khabarFirst', words: [{ ar: 'وَعِندَهُۥ', en: 'and with him', say: 'wa-indahu' }] },
        { role: 'mubtadaLater', words: [{ ar: 'مَفَاتِحُ', en: 'keys of', say: 'mafatihu' }, { ar: 'ٱلْغَيْبِ', en: 'the unseen', say: 'al-ghaybi' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And with Him ', { gap: 'are' }, ' the keys of the unseen.']}
    />

The last kind of khabar is a word for a place or a time, a ظَرْف (*ẓarf*). Words like عِنْدَ (with, at), فَوْقَ (above) and يَوْمَ (on the day) are place and time words.

Here the khabar is عِندَهُۥ, “with Him”. A place word like this ends in -a, not -u.

In this verse the khabar comes **first**, and the mubtadaʾ مَفَاتِحُ comes after it. The next part of the lesson explains why.
</Step>

<Step id="kinds" label="The five kinds" title="The five kinds of khabar">
  <Stage label="The book’s own examples">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote lx-quote-list" lang="ar" dir="rtl">
      <p>زَيْدٌ قَائِمٌ</p>
      <p>زَيْدٌ فِي الدَّارِ</p>
      <p>زَيْدٌ عِنْدَكَ</p>
      <p>زَيْدٌ قَامَ أَبُوهُ</p>
      <p>زَيْدٌ جَارِيَتُهُ ذَاهِبَةٌ</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note"><i>Al-Ajurrumiyyah</i>, in order: single; phrase; place word; verbal sentence; nominal sentence. Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* says the khabar that is not single is “four things”. With the single khabar, that makes five kinds:

| Kind | In this lesson |
|---|---|
| Single, مُفْرَد | وَٱللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ |
| A phrase, جَارّ وَمَجْرُور | وَٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ |
| A place or time word, ظَرْف | وَعِندَهُۥ مَفَاتِحُ ٱلْغَيْبِ |
| A verbal sentence, جُمْلَة فِعْلِيَّة | ٱللَّهُ يَسْتَهْزِئُ بِهِمْ |
| A nominal sentence, جُمْلَة اِسْمِيَّة | وَٱللَّهُ عِندَهُۥ حُسْنُ ٱلْمَـَٔابِ |

The book’s own examples, on the right, all use the name Zayd: “Zayd is standing”, “Zayd is in the house”, “Zayd is with you”, “Zayd, his father stood up”, “Zayd, his servant is going”.

</Step>

<Step part="Which comes first" id="order" label="Usual order" title="The usual order">
  <Stage label="The usual order">
    <Formula
      label="mubtadaʾ first, then khabar"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'mubtadaʾ', small: 'first' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'khabar', small: 'second' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'nominal sentence', small: 'the usual order' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Usually the mubtadaʾ comes first and the khabar second. That is the order in almost every verse so far: ٱللَّهُ first, then the news about Allah.

But the khabar can come first. When it does, the two parts get new names:

- **khabar muqaddam**, خَبَر مُقَدَّم: the khabar, put first.
- **mubtadaʾ muʾakhkhar**, مُبْتَدَأ مُؤَخَّر: the mubtadaʾ, put later.

The roles stay the same; only their order changes.
</Step>

<Step id="by-choice" label="First by choice" title="The khabar first, by choice">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Kafirun 109:6"

        { role: 'mubtadaLater', words: [{ ar: 'دِينُكُمْ', en: 'your religion', say: 'dinukum' }] },
        { role: 'khabarFirst', words: [{ ar: 'وَلِىَ', en: 'and for me', say: 'wa-liya', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'mubtadaLater', words: [{ ar: 'دِينِ', en: 'my religion', say: 'dini' }] },

      en={['For you ', { gap: 'is' }, ' your religion, and for me ', { gap: 'is' }, ' my religion.']}
    />

This verse has two nominal sentences, and both put the khabar first. The khabar is the phrase (“for you”, “for me”). The mubtadaʾ is the religion.

The mubtadaʾ is still the word that ends in -u: دِينُكُمْ. Moving words to the front usually gives them weight: *for you* your religion, *for me* mine. L5.14 looks at word order and weight.

<Note kind="know">دِينِ is دِينِي, “my religion”, with the long ī of “my” left out. Its -u is hidden: al-Jadwal says the ḍamma is understood on the letter before the dropped ī.</Note>

</Step>

<Step id="must" label="First by rule" title="When the khabar must come first">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:10 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'khabarFirst', words: [{ ar: 'وَلَهُمْ', en: 'and for them', say: 'wa-lahum', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'mubtadaLater', words: [{ ar: 'عَذَابٌ', en: 'a punishment', say: 'adhabun' }, { ar: 'أَلِيمٌۢ', en: 'painful', say: 'alimun' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And for them ', { gap: 'is' }, ' a painful punishment.']}
    />
  </Stage>

Look at the mubtadaʾ: عَذَابٌ, “**a** punishment”. It ends in -un, so it is **indefinite**.

Arabic does not usually start a sentence with an indefinite noun like this. So when the khabar is a phrase or a place word, it **must** come first. *Al-Durr al-Maṣūn*, a classical book on the language of the Quran, calls this “a khabar put first that must come first”.

<Check n={8} q="Why does لَهُمْ come first here?" options={['Because the mubtadaʾ عَذَابٌ is indefinite', 'Because لَهُمْ is a verb', 'There is no reason']} answer={0} why="An indefinite mubtadaʾ with a phrase as khabar: the khabar must come first." />
</Step>

<Step id="place-first" label="Place word first" title="Finding the mubtadaʾ when the khabar is first">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Anʿam 6:59 (start)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'khabarFirst', words: [{ ar: 'وَعِندَهُۥ', en: 'and with him' }] },
        { role: 'mubtadaLater', words: [{ ar: 'مَفَاتِحُ', en: 'keys of' }, { ar: 'ٱلْغَيْبِ', en: 'the unseen' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And with Him ', { gap: 'are' }, ' the keys of the unseen.']}
    />
  </Stage>

Back to the keys of the unseen. Here the mubtadaʾ is **definite** (*the* keys of the unseen), so the khabar did not have to come first. The verse chose this order. It goes on: “No one knows them except Him.”

When a sentence starts with a phrase or a place word, find the mubtadaʾ like this: look for the next noun that ends in -u and does not come after a little word. Here it is مَفَاتِحُ.

<Note kind="tip">The mubtadaʾ is never the noun right after *li-*, *bi-* or *fī*. Those nouns end in -i. Look further for the -u.</Note>
</Step>

<Step part="Sentence or phrase" id="sentence-phrase" label="Sentence or phrase" title="A sentence or just a phrase?">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Made-up example"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْكِتَابُ', en: 'the book', say: 'al-kitabu' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'جَدِيدٌ', en: 'new', say: 'jadidun' }] },
      ]}
      en={['The book ', { gap: 'is' }, ' new.']}
    />
    <Sentence
      reference="Made-up example"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْكِتَابُ', en: 'the book', say: 'al-kitabu' }, { ar: 'ٱلْجَدِيدُ', en: 'the new', say: 'al-jadidu' }] },
      ]}
      en={['the new book …']}
    />
  </Stage>

The two lines look almost the same. Only one is a sentence.

- ٱلْكِتَابُ جَدِيدٌ: **the** book, **a** new … The first word is definite (it has *al-*), the second is not. This is a sentence: “The book is new.”
- ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلْجَدِيدُ: both words have *al-*. This is a phrase, a noun with its adjective: “the new book”. It is not finished. It waits for news about the new book.

So the usual pattern of a nominal sentence is a definite mubtadaʾ and an indefinite khabar. L2.09 teaches nouns and adjectives.

<Note kind="warn">This is a first guide, not a law. A khabar can be definite too. You saw one at the very start: هُوَ ٱلْأَوَّلُ, “He is the First”.</Note>
</Step>

<Step id="phrase-mubtada" label="A phrase as mubtadaʾ" title="A noun and its adjective can be the mubtadaʾ">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:10 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'khabarFirst', words: [{ ar: 'وَلَهُمْ', en: 'and for them', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'mubtadaLater', hint: 'a noun and its adjective', words: [{ ar: 'عَذَابٌ', en: 'a punishment' }, { ar: 'أَلِيمٌۢ', en: 'painful' }] },

      en={['And for them ', { gap: 'is' }, ' a painful punishment.']}
    />
  </Stage>

In this verse, عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌۢ is a phrase: a noun, “a punishment”, and its adjective, “painful”. Both are indefinite, and the adjective copies the noun’s ending: -un and -un.

The whole phrase is the mubtadaʾ. The khabar is لَهُمْ, put first. Al-Jadwal: أَلِيمٌ is “an adjective of عَذَاب, nominative like it”.

<Step id="gender" label="Feminine" title="The khabar matches a feminine mubtadaʾ">

    <Sentence

In 2:259, هِىَ, “she, it”, is a town, which is feminine in Arabic. The khabar خَاوِيَةٌ (“fallen down, in ruins”) is feminine too: it ends in -atun (ـَةٌ). A khabar like this matches its mubtadaʾ.

In 27:88, هِىَ is the mountains. The khabar is a verb, and the verb shows the feminine too: تَمُرُّ starts with *ta-*.

Look at the English of 2:259: “was”, not “is”. A nominal sentence has no time of its own. The verse around it tells you the time.

<Note kind="know">Arabic often uses هِىَ, “she”, for a plural of things, like “the mountains”. You will meet this rule again.</Note>

</Step>

  <Stage label="Nothing is missing">

Learners who think in English often look for a word for “is”, or think a word has fallen out of the verse. Nothing has fallen out. Two nouns side by side, both ending in -u, already make a full sentence.

And when the khabar is a verbal sentence, like ٱللَّهُ يَسْتَهْزِئُ بِهِمْ, English needs no “is” at all.

  <Stage>

    />
    <Sentence

      groups={[

In both verses ٱللَّهُ ends in -u. But it has a different job in each.

- In 2:15, ٱللَّهُ comes **before** the verb. It starts a nominal sentence, so it is the **mubtadaʾ**.
- In 2:7, ٱللَّهُ comes **after** the verb خَتَمَ. It is the doer of that verb, the **fāʿil**, فَاعِل. That is a verbal sentence.

Both are nominative, so the ending does not tell them apart. The order does. L2.03 teaches the verbal sentence and the fāʿil.
</Step>

<Step id="mistake-order" label="Mistake 3" title="Mistake 3: thinking word order does not matter">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Kafirun 109:6 (start)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'khabarFirst', words: [{ ar: 'لَكُمْ', en: 'for you', type: 'p' }] },

Endings show the role of each word, so learners sometimes think the order is free. It is not.

- Sometimes the order is a rule: with an indefinite mubtadaʾ and a phrase as khabar, the khabar must come first (2:10).
- Sometimes it is a choice, and the choice changes the weight of the sentence (109:6).
</Step>

  <Stage>

        { at: [1, 2, 3], role: 'the khabar of ٱللَّهُ: a whole nominal sentence', case: 'it stands in the place of the nominative', why: 'The whole inner sentence is the news about Allah.', irab: 'وجملة «عنده حسن المآب» في محل رفع خبر' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

This is the hardest of the four. It has a sentence inside a sentence, and the inner sentence puts its khabar first.

Go slowly. The outer sentence is ٱللَّهُ + (the news). The news is the inner sentence: عِندَهُۥ (khabar, first) + حُسْنُ ٱلْمَـَٔابِ (mubtadaʾ, later).
</Step>

<Step part="Practice" id="practice-1" label="Practice 1" title="Practice: find and parse">
  <Stage label="For each word, say">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Role:</b> mubtadaʾ, khabar, or something else?</li>
      <li><b>Case:</b> nominative, accusative or genitive?</li>
      <li><b>Mark:</b> -u, -un, a hidden -u, a letter, or none?</li>
      <li><b>Reason:</b> why does it have this case?</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Find the mubtadaʾ and the khabar: وَٱللَّهُ بَصِيرٌۢ بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ (Al-Imran 3:163, end). Meaning: “And Allah sees what they do.”">

ٱللَّهُ is the **mubtadaʾ** (-u). بَصِيرٌ, “seeing”, is the **khabar** (-un).

بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ, “what they do”, is a phrase that belongs to بَصِيرٌ. It is not the khabar. (Al-Jadwal: “tied to بصير”.)

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="This verse has two nominal sentences. Find both parts of each: وَلِلَّهِ مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ وَٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ (Al-Imran 3:189). Meaning: “And to Allah belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, and Allah has power over everything.”">

**First sentence:** لِلَّهِ, “to Allah”, is the **khabar, put first**. مُلْكُ, “the kingdom”, is the **mubtadaʾ, put later** (-u). ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ, “of the heavens and the earth”, complete it.

**Second sentence:** ٱللَّهُ is the **mubtadaʾ**. قَدِيرٌ is the **khabar**. The phrase عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ belongs to قَدِيرٌ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Parse every word: هُمْ فِيهَا خَٰلِدُونَ (Al-Baqarah 2:39, end). Meaning: “They will stay in it forever.”">

- هُمْ, “they”: a pronoun, the **mubtadaʾ**. It never changes, but counts as nominative.
- فِيهَا, “in it”: a phrase that belongs to the khabar.
- خَٰلِدُونَ, “staying forever”: the **khabar**, nominative. Its mark is -ūna, with the letter و: a sound plural shows its case with a letter (L2.04).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="What case is مَوْلَىٰ in, and how can you tell? أَنتَ مَوْلَىٰنَا (Al-Baqarah 2:286, part). Meaning: “You are our Protector.”">

أَنتَ, “you”, is a pronoun and the **mubtadaʾ**. مَوْلَىٰ is the **khabar**, so it is **nominative**. You cannot see the -u, because the word ends in a long ā (ى). The ḍamma is hidden. نَا means “our”.

</Exercise>
</Step>

<Step id="practice-2" label="Practice 2" title="Practice: kinds, building and matching">
  <Stage label="The five kinds of khabar">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Single</b> (مُفْرَد): not a sentence</li>
      <li><b>A phrase</b> with a little word: لِ، بِ، فِي</li>
      <li><b>A place or time word</b>: عِنْدَ، فَوْقَ، يَوْمَ</li>
      <li><b>A verbal sentence</b>: a verb and its doer</li>
      <li><b>A nominal sentence</b>: a sentence inside a sentence</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

<Exercise n={5} q="What kind of khabar does each one have? (a) وَٱللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ (b) وَٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ (c) وَعِندَهُۥ مَفَاتِحُ ٱلْغَيْبِ (d) ٱللَّهُ يَسْتَهْزِئُ بِهِمْ (e) وَٱللَّهُ عِندَهُۥ حُسْنُ ٱلْمَـَٔابِ">

- (a) **Single**: عَلِيمٌ and حَكِيمٌ, two single khabars.
- (b) **A phrase**: لِلْمُتَّقِينَ.
- (c) **A place word**: عِندَهُۥ, put first.
- (d) **A verbal sentence**: يَسْتَهْزِئُ بِهِمْ.
- (e) **A nominal sentence**: عِندَهُۥ حُسْنُ ٱلْمَـَٔابِ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Build a nominal sentence from these two words, and say which is the mubtadaʾ and which the khabar: ٱلْعِلْمُ (knowledge) and نُورٌ (a light). This is a made-up example.">

ٱلْعِلْمُ نُورٌ, “Knowledge is a light.”

ٱلْعِلْمُ is the **mubtadaʾ**: definite (*al-*), ends in -u. نُورٌ is the **khabar**: indefinite, ends in -un. It is a single khabar.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Why does one khabar end in ـَةٌ and the other not? ٱلسَّمَاءُ عَالِيَةٌ (The sky is high) and ٱلْبَحْرُ عَمِيقٌ (The sea is deep). These are made-up examples.">

ٱلسَّمَاءُ, “the sky”, is **feminine** in Arabic, so its khabar is feminine too: عَالِيَةٌ, with ـَةٌ.

ٱلْبَحْرُ, “the sea”, is **masculine**, so its khabar عَمِيقٌ has no ـَة.

In both, the mubtadaʾ is definite and ends in -u, and the khabar is indefinite and ends in -un.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Sentence or phrase? (a) ٱلْكِتَابُ جَدِيدٌ (b) ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلْجَدِيدُ (c) عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ (from 2:10) (d) وَٱللَّهُ وَلِىُّ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ (from 3:68)">

- (a) **Sentence**: “The book is new.” Definite, then indefinite.
- (b) **Phrase**: “the new book”. Both definite: a noun and its adjective.
- (c) **Phrase**: “a painful punishment”. A noun and its adjective. In 2:10 the whole phrase is the mubtadaʾ.
- (d) **Sentence**: “Allah is the protector of the believers.”

</Exercise>
</Step>

<Step id="done" label="Done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="mubtadaʾ plus khabar equals a nominal sentence"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'mubtadaʾ', small: 'the starting point' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'khabar', small: 'the news' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'nominal sentence', small: 'جُمْلَة اِسْمِيَّة' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- A nominal sentence starts with a noun and needs no verb. English adds “is”.
- It has two parts: the **mubtadaʾ** (the starting point) and the **khabar** (the news). Both are nominative: -u, -un, a hidden -u, or a pronoun that never changes.
- The khabar can be single, a phrase, a place or time word, a verbal sentence or a nominal sentence.
- The khabar can come first. With an indefinite mubtadaʾ and a phrase as khabar, it must.
- Definite + indefinite usually makes a sentence. Two definite words usually make a phrase.
- *kāna* makes the khabar accusative (L2.11).
