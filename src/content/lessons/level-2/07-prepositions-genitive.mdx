It is the end of a verse about how people were made. Read it from right to left.

There are two little words here: مِن (*min*, “from”) and كَ (*ka*, “like”). Each one stands in front of a noun. Look at the end of those nouns: صَلْصَٰلٍ ends in **-in**, and ٱلْفَخَّارِ ends in **-i**. Both are the genitive ending you met in L1.10 and L2.06.

This lesson is about those little words, what they mean, and what they do to the noun after them.

In English a preposition is a small word like *in*, *from*, *to* or *on*. It stands **before** a noun and tells you where, when or how: “from clay”, “in the earth”, “to Allah”.

Arabic has the same kind of word. It is called a **ḥarf jarr**, حَرْف جَرّ. *Ḥarf* means “a little word”, and *jarr* is the genitive case. So the name says: “the little word that gives the genitive”.

One difference from English: in Arabic the preposition changes the **next word**. The noun after it must take the genitive ending.

<Note kind="know">*Al-Ajurrumiyyah*, the short grammar book you met in L2.01, uses another name for the same case: *khafḍ*, خَفْض, and calls such a noun *makhfūḍ*. Al-Jadwal and most modern books say *jarr* and *majrūr*. They mean the same. This course says *jarr*.</Note>
</Block>

The noun after the preposition is called the **ism majrūr**, اِسْم مَجْرُور: “the noun that has been given the genitive”. In this verse ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ and ٱلْأَرْضِ are both majrūr, and both end in **-i**.

The preposition itself has no case. A little word never does. It only gives the case to the next word.

You know the genitive marks from L1.10, and L2.06 teaches them in full. The short list:

| The noun is | Genitive mark | Example from this lesson |
|---|---|---|
| a single noun | -i or -in | ٱلْأَرْضِ, صَلْصَٰلٍ |
| a sound feminine plural | -i (the same mark) | ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ |
| a sound masculine plural | -īna | ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ |

<Note kind="remember">After a preposition the noun is always genitive. The preposition decides, not the meaning.</Note>

A preposition never stands alone. It always has its noun, and the two work together as one block: **فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ**, “in the land”. The grammar books call this block **jār wa-majrūr**, جَارّ وَمَجْرُور: “the one that gives the genitive, and the one that receives it”.

The block has no ending of its own. It is not a sentence either. It is a piece that adds something to another word: where, when, about what, for whom.

Which word does it add to? That is the question of step 4. Before that, step 2 meets the prepositions one by one.

In its chapter on the genitive (*makhfūḍāt*), *al-Ajurrumiyyah* says the noun that takes the genitive by a little word is the one after these: *min*, *ilā*, *ʿan*, *ʿalā*, *fī*, *rubba*, the letter *bāʾ*, the letter *kāf*, the letter *lām*, then the oath letters (*wāw*, *bāʾ*, *tāʾ*), the *wāw* of *rubba*, and *mudh* and *mundhu*.

This lesson teaches the main eight, and the oath letters.

| Little word | Main meanings | You meet it in |
|---|---|---|
| مِن *min* | from, among, made of | 55:14, 2:8 |
| إِلَى *ilā* | to, as far as | 3:109, 10:56 |
| عَن *ʿan* | about, away from | 17:85, 6:26 |
| عَلَى *ʿalā* | on, following, for or against | 2:5, 2:6 |
| فِي *fī* | in (a place, a time, a state) | 3:109, 12:73 |
| بِ *bi-* | in, with, by means of | 2:8 |
| كَ *ka-* | like | 55:14 |
| لِ *li-* | for, belonging to | 3:109, 2:156 |
| وَ تَ بِ *wa-, ta-, bi-* of an oath | by | 103:1, 12:73 |

<Note kind="know">*Rubba* (“many a”), *mudh* and *mundhu* (“since”) are in the book's list but this lesson does not teach them.</Note>
</Block>

Five of the prepositions are **separate words**: مِن، إِلَى، عَن، عَلَى، فِي.

Three are **one letter** and join the next word: بِ، لِ، كَ. So are the oath letters. You saw this in the first block: كَٱلْفَخَّارِ is *ka* + ٱلْفَخَّارِ.

When the next word starts with ٱل (“the”), the spelling differs a little:

- **لِ + ٱل** loses the alif of ٱل in writing: لِ + ٱللَّهِ becomes لِلَّهِ, and لِ + ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ becomes لِلْمُتَّقِينَ.
- **بِ and كَ** keep it. You still see the sign ٱ in بِٱللَّهِ and كَٱلْفَخَّارِ, but you do not say it.

A one-letter preposition is easy to miss, so look at the first letter of a word before you read on.

<Note kind="tip">If a word starts with بِ، لِ or كَ, ask: could this be a preposition joined to a noun? Then read the rest as the ism majrūr.</Note>

**مِن** (*min*) starts from a point or takes part of a whole:

- a starting point: مِن صَلْصَٰلٍ, “from clay” (55:14),
- a part of a group: وَمِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ, “and among the people” (2:8),
- the stuff something is made of, and, after a word like “better”, the thing it is compared with.

**فِي** (*fī*) puts something inside a place, a time or a state: فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ, “in the land” (12:73), “in that day”, “in loss”. It is the plainest of the prepositions.

<Note kind="tip">The mubtadaʾ is never the noun right after a preposition. In this verse the noun ٱلنَّاسِ is majrūr. The mubtadaʾ is مَن, which comes later (L2.01).</Note>
</Block>

**إِلَى** (*ilā*) points toward a goal or an end: وَإِلَى ٱللَّهِ, “and to Allah”. Think of an arrow that ends at the noun.

**عَلَى** (*ʿalā*) puts something on top of something, and from there it grows more meanings:

- on: “on a seat”,
- following a path: عَلَىٰ هُدًى, “on guidance”, that is, following it (2:5),
- a duty or a gift to someone: فَضْلُ ٱللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ, “the favour of Allah on you” (2:64),
- against someone.

Context decides. In step 5 you will see why one English word never fits every verse.

The word هُدًى after عَلَىٰ is genitive, but you cannot see the mark. Step 3 explains why.
</Block>

**عَن** (*ʿan*) has two main meanings:

- **about**: يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلرُّوحِ, “they ask you about the soul”,
- **away from**: يَنْهَوْنَ عَنْهُ, “they hold others back from it” (6:26).

Both come from one idea: movement off or away from a thing, or talk that turns toward it.

<Note kind="know">In English both *min* and *ʿan* can be “from”. *Min* says where something starts or what it is part of. *ʿAn* says that it moves away from something, or that the talk is about it.</Note>
</Block>

**بِ** (*bi-*) joins two things together. That gives it several meanings:

- **in** or **at**, after a verb of belief: ءَامَنَّا بِٱللَّهِ, “we believe in Allah”,
- **with** or **by means of**: بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ, “with the name of Allah”,
- **together with**, and **because of**.

The grey bars under the verse say that each phrase belongs to the verb. Step 4 explains how.
</Block>

**لِ** (*li-*) joins a thing to a person or a purpose:

- **belonging**: وَلِلَّهِ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ, “to Allah belongs what is in the heavens”,
- **for the sake of**, **for the use of**: هُدًى لِّلْمُتَّقِينَ, “guidance for the God-fearing” (2:2).

**كَ** (*ka-*) means **like**. You met it in the first block: كَٱلْفَخَّارِ, “like pottery”. It is always joined to the noun after it.

When someone swears, Arabic can use a single letter that means **by**. *Al-Ajurrumiyyah* lists three: **وَ** (*wa-*), **بِ** (*bi-*) and **تَ** (*ta-*). They also give the genitive. In 103:1, ٱلْعَصْرِ is majrūr after the oath *wāw*. In 12:73, ٱللَّهِ is majrūr after the oath *tāʾ*.

A *wāw* of oath looks like the *wāw* that means “and”. Check two things: is the noun after it genitive, and is something being sworn by? Then it is an oath.

Al-Jadwal says a phrase of oath is tied to a verb that is left out, “I swear” or “we swear”. Step 4 explains what that means.

<Note kind="know">A lām in front of a particle, like the لَ of لَقَدْ in 12:73, is a different lām. It is the lām of the oath. It does not make a noun genitive.</Note>

        { kind: 'm', text: 'عَلَىٰ', small: 'on' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'هِمْ', small: 'them' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'عَلَيْهِمْ', small: 'on them' },
      ]}
    />

The noun after a preposition can be a **pronoun**. A pronoun like *him*, *her* or *them* cannot stand alone in Arabic here. It joins the end of the preposition and makes one word: عَلَىٰ + هِمْ becomes عَلَيْهِمْ, “on them”.

The pronoun is the **ism majrūr**. It is genitive. You cannot see an ending on it, because pronouns never change. The books say it is genitive “in the place of” (*fī maḥall jarr*), as they said for هُوَ in L2.01, which is nominative in the same way.

The joined pronouns you will see most:

| Ending | Meaning | With *bi-* |
|---|---|---|
| ـهُ / ـهِ | him, it | بِهِ |
| ـهَا | her, it | بِهَا |
| ـهُمْ / ـهِمْ | them | بِهِمْ |
| ـكَ | you (one man) | بِكَ |
| ـكُمْ | you (all) | بِكُمْ |
| ـنَا | us | بِنَا |

<Block id="change" title="hum becomes him">
  <Stage label="Look at the vowel on the hā">
    <Tiles
      label="Four words with the pronoun them"
      items={[
        { ar: 'مِنْهُمْ', en: 'from them', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'لَهُمْ', en: 'for them', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'بِهِمْ', en: 'with them', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'فِيهِمْ', en: 'among them', type: 'p' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Look at the joined pronoun *them*. It is spelled **هُمْ** (*hum*) in some words and **هِمْ** (*him*) in others.

The pronouns **هُ, هُمْ, هُمَا, هُنَّ** change their vowel to a kasra, **هِ, هِمْ, هِمَا, هِنَّ**, in a simple case: the sound before them is a **kasra**, or a **ي with sukūn**.

- بِهِمْ: the letter before the hā is بِ, with a kasra. So *hum* becomes *him*.
- فِيهِمْ and عَلَيْهِمْ: the letter before is ي with sukūn. So *him*.
- رَبِّهِمْ in 2:5: the letter before is بِّ with a kasra. So *him*. It is the same rule for a noun.
- لَهُمْ and مِنْهُمْ: before the hā there is a fatḥa (لَ) or a letter with sukūn (the نْ of مِنْ). No kasra, no change. So *hum*.

The pronouns **هَا, كُمْ, نَا** never change, so you will always see بِهَا, بِكُمْ and بِنَا.

<Note kind="know">The Tanzil text has a few exceptions to this pattern. One is عَلَيْهُ ٱللَّهَ in 48:10, where the hā keeps its ḍamma. You may also see a tiny و or ي after the pronoun, as in لَهُۥ and بِهِۦ. It is a mark for reading the sound long. It does not change the grammar.</Note>

<Check n={5} q="Which spelling is written in the Quran for “with them”?" options={['بِهُمْ', 'بِهِمْ']} answer={1} why="The letter before the hā has a kasra, so the hā takes a kasra too: bihim." />
</Block>

Two prepositions end in a long ā that is written **ى**: إِلَى and عَلَى. When a pronoun joins them, the ى turns into **يْ** (a ya with sukūn): إِلَيْهِ, عَلَيْهِمْ, إِلَيْنَا, عَلَيْكُمْ.

That is why the hā takes a kasra here: the letter before it is now a ي with sukūn (see the last block).

Look at 2:156 for one verse with both kinds: قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ, “they said: we belong to Allah, and we are returning to Him”. The first phrase is لِلَّهِ, the second is إِلَيْهِ.

The preposition **لِ** takes a **fatḥa** in front of a joined pronoun: لِ + هُ becomes **لَ**هُ, لِ + كَ becomes **لَ**كَ, and so on.

There is one exception, the pronoun **ي** (“me”). It keeps the kasra: لِي. You met it in L2.01: وَلِىَ دِينِ, “and for me my religion” (109:6).

The other prepositions keep their own vowels before the pronoun. With “me”, *min* and *ʿan* double their nūn: مِنِّى in 2:38, “from me”.

<Block id="hidden" title="When you cannot see the genitive">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:5 (part)"
      groups={[

    />

The rule is always true: the noun after a preposition is genitive. But you cannot always see it.

- **A word that ends in a long ā** (ى) cannot carry a kasra. هُدًى is genitive after عَلَىٰ, and the kasra is **hidden** (*muqaddara*, “understood but not seen”). Al-Jadwal says exactly this about this verse. You met a hidden ending on مَوْلَىٰ in L2.01.
- **A joined pronoun** like هِمْ never changes, so it is genitive “in the place of” the genitive.
- **Words that never change**, like ذَٰلِكَ (“that”) or مَن (“who”), are genitive in the same way when a preposition comes before them.

In رَّبِّهِمْ the noun رَبِّ is genitive after مِّن, with a visible -i. The pronoun هِمْ after it is also genitive, for another reason: it is the second word of an idafah, “their Lord” (L2.08).

<Note kind="warn">A word that does not look genitive can still be the ism majrūr. Use its place right after the preposition.</Note>

<Step id="tied" label="What it is tied to" title="What the phrase is tied to">
<Block id="why" title="A phrase belongs to a word">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Isra 17:85 (start)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'fiil', words: [{ ar: 'وَيَسْـَٔلُونَكَ', en: 'and they ask you', say: 'wa-yasalunaka', type: 'v' }] },

    />
  </Stage>

A preposition phrase is not a full part of the sentence on its own. It adds to another word. Here it tells you **what they ask about**.

The grammar books say the phrase is **tied** to that word. The Arabic is **mutaʿalliq**, مُتَعَلِّق. Al-Jadwal writes it as *mutaʿalliq bi-…*, “tied to …”, after almost every preposition phrase.

To find the word, ask a question: they ask … about what? About the soul. So عَنِ ٱلرُّوحِ is tied to يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ.
</Block>

The most common case is a phrase tied to a **verb**. It says who, what, where, when or why. Both phrases here belong to the same verb, ءَامَنَّا. The second one is joined to the first by وَ, “and”.

The phrase فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ of 12:73 is tied to a verb too. So is وَإِلَى ٱللَّهِ in 3:109, where the verb تُرْجَعُ comes **after** the phrase: the phrase can stand before the word it is tied to.

A phrase can also be tied to a **noun or adjective that describes an action**, like “returning”, “equal” or “all-knowing”.

- رَٰجِعُونَ means “returning”. It works like a verb, so إِلَيْهِ is tied to it: “returning *to him*”. (*Inna* and its news are taught in L2.10.)
- سَوَآءٌ means “equal”, so عَلَيْهِمْ, “for them”, is tied to it: “the same *for them*”.

In both, al-Jadwal says the phrase is tied to this word.

Sometimes you look for a word to tie the phrase to, and the sentence has none. Arabic does not say “is” in a nominal sentence (L2.01), and often it does not say “there”, “found”, “that is” either. The grammar books then say the phrase is tied to a **word that is left out** (*maḥdhūf*), a word like “is found”.

- In **وَلِلَّهِ مَا …** the phrase is the news about مَا. It is tied to a left-out *khabar*, a word like “belongs” (and it comes first, as L2.01 taught).
- In **مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ** the phrase completes مَا, “whatever”, and says which things: “whatever *is* in the heavens”. It is tied to a left-out word that finishes the meaning of مَا. The grammar books call that part the *ṣila*, “the link”.
- In **عَلَىٰ هُدًى** (2:5) the phrase is the news about أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ: “those *are* on guidance”. The next phrase, مِّن رَّبِّهِمْ, is tied to a left-out adjective of هُدًى: “guidance *that is* from their Lord”.

Al-Jadwal reads all these verses in this way.

<Note kind="know">In L2.01, *al-Ajurrumiyyah* simply calls a phrase like لِلْمُتَّقِينَ the khabar. Al-Jadwal says exactly what you see here: the phrase is tied to a left-out khabar. The two agree on the meaning.</Note>

<Block id="find" title="How to find what a phrase is tied to">
  <Stage label="Three questions">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Find the pair:</b> a preposition and the noun after it, in the genitive.</li>
      <li><b>Look for a word it adds to:</b> a verb, or a word like “returning”, “equal”, “all-knowing”. It can come before or after the phrase.</li>
      <li><b>Nothing fits?</b> Then the phrase is the news itself, or finishes a word like “whatever”. It is tied to a left-out word like “is”.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Follow the three questions in order. Many mistakes come from skipping the second one.

A phrase is tied to **one** word. Two phrases can be tied to the same word, as in 2:8.

<Note kind="know">Scholars differ on one famous phrase. In بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ, al-Jadwal says بِسْمِ is tied to a left-out khabar, with a left-out mubtadaʾ “my beginning”. This is the view of the Basran grammarians: the phrase is tied to a noun (“my beginning is by the name of Allah”). Al-Durr al-Maṣūn also gives the Kufan view, which ties it to a verb (“I begin by the name of Allah”). You will see this in exercise 8.</Note>
</Block>

<Step id="word-by-word" label="Mistakes, word by word, practice" title="Four mistakes, four verses word by word, and practice">
<Block id="mistake-meaning" title="Mistake 1: one English word for each preposition">
  <Stage label="One preposition, two meanings">
    <Tiles
      label="ʿala in two verses"
      items={[
        { ar: 'عَلَىٰ هُدًى', en: 'following guidance', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'سَوَآءٌ عَلَيْهِمْ', en: 'the same for them', type: 'p' },
      ]}
    />

A learner who knows “ʿalā means *on*” will translate عَلَىٰ هُدًى as “on guidance”, and سَوَآءٌ عَلَيْهِمْ as “equal on them”. The second one is not English.

Each preposition has a main idea and then several meanings. The verse and the word before it decide which one fits.

- 2:5: ʿalā means “following” (a path of guidance).
- 2:6: ʿalā means “for” (it is the same *for* them).
- بِ is “in” after *believe* (2:8) and “with” in بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ.

<Note kind="tip">When a literal English word sounds odd, try another meaning of the preposition from step 2.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-lam" title="Mistake 2: every lām is a preposition">
  <Stage label="Two lāms">
    <Tiles
      label="a preposition lam and a lam of command"
      items={[
        { ar: 'لِلَّهِ', en: 'for Allah (preposition)', type: 'p' },
        { ar: 'فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ', en: 'let them rely (lām of command)', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The letter ل appears in many other jobs. Only one of them is the preposition **li-**, which stands before a **noun** and makes it genitive: لِلَّهِ.

- The lām in **فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ** (14:11, “then let them rely”) is the *lām of command*. It stands before a **verb** and tells it “let” (al-Jadwal names it so in 14:11). *Al-Ajurrumiyyah* lists it among the little words that change a verb (the chapter on the verb in the jussive).
- The lām in **لَقَدْ** (12:73) is the lām of the oath. It stands before a particle.

Ask: is the word after the lām a noun? If it is a verb, it is not the preposition.
</Block>

      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Learners sometimes say that the نْ of مِنْ melts into the next letter before a sun letter, as in مِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ. That is wrong. The sun-letter rule from L1 changes the **lām of ٱل**, not the preposition.

Written: مِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ. The word *min* keeps its ن and takes a fatḥa. You say *minan-nāsi*, because the lām of ٱل sounds like the ن of ٱلنَّاسِ.

The same is true of عَنِ ٱلنَّاسِ.
</Block>

<Exercise n={1} q="Find the preposition, the ism majrūr and what the phrase is tied to: وَهُمْ يَنْهَوْنَ عَنْهُ (Al-Anʿam 6:26, part). Meaning: “and they hold others back from it.”">

The preposition is **عَن**. The ism majrūr is the joined pronoun **هُ**, “it”, genitive in place. The word before the hā is نْ with a sukūn, so the hā keeps its ḍamma: *ʿanhu*.

The phrase عَنْهُ is tied to **يَنْهَوْنَ**, “they hold back”. Al-Jadwal says the same. The meaning of ʿan is “away from”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="What is the phrase tied to? لِنُفْسِدَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ (Yusuf 12:73, part). Meaning: “to spread trouble in the land.”">

The preposition is **فِى**, the ism majrūr is **ٱلْأَرْضِ** (-i), and the phrase is tied to the verb **نُفْسِدَ**, “we spread trouble”. Al-Jadwal says so. Ask “where?” and the verb gives the answer: spread trouble *in the land*.

The first letter, لِ, is another kind of lām that joins a purpose to a verb: “in order to”. Leave it for later.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Break the first word into its parts, and say what the phrase is tied to: ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ (Al-Baqarah 2:28, end). Meaning: “then you will be brought back to Him.”">

إِلَيْهِ is **إِلَى + هِ**. The ى turned into يْ before the pronoun, and the hā took a kasra after the ي with sukūn.

The preposition is **إِلَى**, “to”. The ism majrūr is **هِ**, “him”, genitive in place. The phrase is tied to **تُرْجَعُونَ**, “you are brought back”. Al-Jadwal reads it so.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="There are two preposition phrases here. Find each and say what it is tied to: لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ هُدًى لِّلْمُتَّقِينَ (Al-Baqarah 2:2, end). Meaning: “there is no doubt in it, a guidance for the God-fearing.”">

**فِيهِ** is **فِى + هِ**, “in it”. The pronoun is the ism majrūr. The phrase is tied to a **left-out khabar** of لَا, a word like “is”: “no doubt *is* in it”.

**لِّلْمُتَّقِينَ** is **لِ + ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ**, “for the God-fearing”. ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ ends in -īna, the genitive mark of a sound masculine plural. Al-Jadwal gives two ways: the phrase is tied to **هُدًى** (“guidance for the God-fearing”), or to a left-out adjective of هُدًى (“a guidance *that is* for the God-fearing”). The meaning is the same.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="What is the first letter here, and what is the word after it? وَٱلضُّحَىٰ (Ad-Duha 93:1)">

The wāw is the **wāw of oath**, “by”, not the wāw that means “and”. Nothing comes before it for it to join, and the noun after it is genitive.

The ism majrūr is **ٱلضُّحَىٰ**, “the morning light”. It is genitive with a **hidden kasra**, because it ends in a long ā (ىٰ). The phrase is tied to a left-out verb, “I swear”. Al-Jadwal agrees on the tying.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Both of these nouns are genitive after a preposition. What does each preposition add to the meaning? إِلَى ٱللَّهِ مَرْجِعُكُمْ (Hud 11:4, start). Meaning: “to Allah is your return.” And لَمَغْفِرَةٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ (Al-Imran 3:157, part). Meaning: “a forgiveness from Allah.”">

In both, ٱللَّهِ ends in **-i**, because it follows a preposition. The case is the same. The meaning is not.

- **إِلَى ٱللَّهِ**: *ilā* points to a goal. Your return ends **at** Allah. The phrase is tied to a left-out khabar, and it comes first. The mubtadaʾ is مَرْجِعُكُمْ, “your return”.
- **مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ**: *min* points to a source. The forgiveness **starts with** Allah. The phrase is tied to a left-out adjective of مَغْفِرَةٌ.

Case follows the grammar of the sentence. Meaning follows the preposition.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Give the role and case of every word: وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ (Hud 11:4, end). Meaning: “and He has power over everything.”">

- وَهُوَ: **هُوَ** is a pronoun and the **mubtadaʾ**. It never changes, but counts as nominative.
- **عَلَىٰ**: the preposition.
- **كُلِّ**: the ism majrūr, genitive (-i), because of عَلَىٰ. It is also the first word of an idafah.
- **شَىْءٍ**: the second word of the idafah, genitive (-in).
- **قَدِيرٌ**: the **khabar**, nominative (-un).

The phrase **عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ** is tied to **قَدِيرٌ**. Al-Jadwal says so.

Note that كُلِّ has one case, genitive, and one reason: the preposition عَلَىٰ. Being the first word of an idafah does not give it a second case. The word شَىْءٍ is genitive for a different reason, because it is the second word of the idafah (L2.08).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Find the preposition, the noun after it, and say what the whole phrase is tied to: بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ (Al-Fatiha 1:1).">

- **بِ**: the preposition, “with”.
- **سْمِ**: the ism majrūr, “name”, genitive (-i). In the Tanzil text the alif of ٱسْم is not written after بِ.
- **ٱللَّهِ**: the second word of an idafah (“name *of Allah*”), genitive (-i).
- **ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ، ٱلرَّحِيمِ**: two adjectives of ٱللَّهِ, genitive (-i), like it.

What is بِسْمِ tied to? Al-Jadwal says it is tied to a **left-out khabar**, with a left-out mubtadaʾ, “my beginning”. That is the Basran view: a left-out noun. Al-Durr al-Maṣūn also gives the Kufan view: a left-out verb, “I begin”. Either answer is right if you say which view you follow.

</Exercise>

        { kind: 's', text: 'jār wa-majrūr', small: 'جَارّ وَمَجْرُور' },

- A **preposition** (ḥarf jarr) stands in front of a noun and makes it **genitive**: -i, -in, -īna. The preposition has no case.
- *Al-Ajurrumiyyah* lists them: *min, ilā, ʿan, ʿalā, fī, rubba*, and the letters *bāʾ, kāf, lām*, with the oath letters *wāw, bāʾ, tāʾ* and *mudh, mundhu*.
- Five stand alone (مِن، إِلَى، عَن، عَلَى، فِي). Three join the next word (بِ، لِ، كَ), and so do the oath letters.
- Each has a main idea and several meanings. The verse decides which one fits.
- A joined pronoun is the ism majrūr: بِهِمْ, عَلَيْهِمْ, لَهُ. The *hum* becomes *him* after a kasra or a ي with sukūn. إِلَى and عَلَى turn into إِلَيْ and عَلَيْ. Before a pronoun, لِ becomes لَ, except for “me”.
- The genitive is sometimes hidden (هُدًى) or only “in the place” of the genitive (a pronoun).
- A preposition phrase is **tied** (mutaʿalliq) to a verb, to a word that acts like a verb, or to a left-out word like “is”. An extra bi- is tied to nothing.

**Next:** L2.08 teaches the idafah, the second way a noun becomes genitive. L2.09 teaches the adjective, which copies the case of its noun. Before you go on, review L2.06 if the genitive marks felt new.
