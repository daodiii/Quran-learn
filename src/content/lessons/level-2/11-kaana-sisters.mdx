This sentence has a verb, وَكَانَ (*wa-kāna*, “and was”), and then two parts that look like a nominal sentence: ٱللَّهُ and مُّقِيتًا.

Look at their endings. ٱللَّهُ ends in **-u**, like the mubtadaʾ of a nominal sentence. مُّقِيتًا ends in **-an**. In L2.01 the news part ended in -un. The verb كَانَ has changed it.

</Block>

The first line is a nominal sentence from L2.01: a mubtadaʾ and a khabar, both ending in -u or -un.

In the second line the verb كَانَ comes in front. Two things happen:

- The first part keeps its -u. It gets a new name: **ism kāna**, اِسْم كَانَ, “the subject of kāna”.
- The khabar changes from -un to **-an**. It is now the **khabar of kāna**, خَبَر كَانَ.

L2.01 showed this change for the first time, with two verses from An-Nisa side by side (4:26 and 4:17). This lesson explains it.

<Note kind="remember">After kāna: the first part stays nominative (-u). The news part becomes accusative (-a).</Note>

<Block id="rule" title="Why “incomplete”?">
  <Stage label="The rule">
    <Formula
      label="kāna, then ism kāna in the nominative, then khabar kāna in the accusative"
      items={[
        { kind: 'v', text: 'كَانَ', small: 'the verb' },
        { kind: 'm', text: 'ism kāna', small: 'nominative: -u' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'khabar kāna', small: 'accusative: -a' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* says it in one line: kāna and her sisters raise the noun (rafʿ, nominative) and put the khabar in naṣb (accusative). In plain words: the first part stays nominative and the khabar turns accusative.

Kāna is a verb, but it is a special one. Al-Jadwal calls it **nāqiṣa**, نَاقِصَة, “incomplete”. A verb like كَتَبَ (“he wrote”) is complete: it has a doer and the sentence is finished. Kāna is incomplete: it needs the khabar to finish the sentence. “Allah was” says nothing. “Allah was a Guardian” does.

The case of each part is not new. The ism kāna is nominative for the reasons in L2.04. The khabar kāna is accusative, and L2.05 listed it as one of the jobs of the accusative.

<Note kind="know">Some English books call the ism “the name of kāna”. We say *ism kāna*, as al-Jadwal does.</Note>
</Block>

| What happens | After inna (L2.10) | After kāna (this lesson) |
|---|---|---|
| The first part | Accusative (-a) | Nominative (-u) |

This verse has both. After إِنَّ, the first part ٱللَّهَ is accusative (-a). Then comes كَانَ. Its ism is hidden (you will see how in Step 2), so the one visible word after it, شَهِيدًا, is the khabar of kāna, and it is accusative too.

The whole sentence كَانَ ... شَهِيدًا is the khabar of إِنَّ. Al-Jadwal says so about this verse, pointing to the same words in 4:29. You will go through that kind of verse again in Step 3.

<Note kind="warn">Kāna is a verb, so it changes shape (kāna, kānat, kuntum, yakūnu, kun). Inna never changes. Step 2 shows the forms.</Note>

A nominal sentence has no time of its own (L2.01). Kāna gives it one. In the made-up sentence, كَانَ ٱلطَّالِبُ صَابِرًا, the student was patient, and that is over.

In the verse, كَانَ is about Allah. This course writes **“has always been”** here, not just “was”, because Allah’s attributes do not stop. This is a choice of English, not a different grammar. The grammar is the same in both sentences.

<Note kind="know">The same words, with the same change of endings, appear in An-Nisa 4:17 (“and Allah has always been All-Knowing, All-Wise”). L2.01 used that verse as a preview, next to 4:26. You do not need it again here.</Note>

The khabar of kāna is accusative, but the mark depends on the word, as L2.05 taught:

- A singular word takes **-a** or **-an**: مُّقِيتًا (4:85).
- A sound masculine plural takes **-īna**. In this verse, مُخْتَلِفِينَ is the khabar of يَزَالُونَ, and its ـِينَ is the accusative mark.
- A dual takes **-ayni**. The ism kāna of 18:80, أَبَوَاهُ, was in L2.04, and its khabar, مُؤْمِنَيْنِ, is dual accusative.

The ism of يَزَالُونَ is hidden inside the verb: the wāw of “they”. Step 2 explains hidden isms.

Kāna changes its shape to fit the doer and the time. These forms are the ones you will meet most:

- **Past:** كَانَ (he was), كَانَتْ (she was), كَانُوا (they were), كُنتَ (you were), كُنتُمْ (you were, plural), كُنتُ (I was).
- **Present:** يَكُونُ (he is, he will be), تَكُونُ (she is), يَكُونُونَ (they are).
- **Command:** كُنْ (be!), كُونُوا (be!, to a group).

The sisters follow the same rule in every form: the ism stays nominative, the khabar turns accusative.

In this verse the present tense appears twice. Both verbs follow the word لِ (“so that”), which changes the ending of a present-tense verb. Level 3 teaches those endings (L3.07). For now, look at the nouns: شُهَدَآءَ and شَهِيدًا are accusative, and ٱلرَّسُولُ is nominative. Al-Jadwal reads both verbs as kāna with a khabar. The ism of the first is the wāw of تَكُونُوا (“you”).

<Note kind="know">In the first half, the ism is hidden inside the verb (the wāw). In the second half, the ism is the noun ٱلرَّسُولُ, written after the verb.</Note>

The command also takes a khabar. **كُنْ** speaks to one person. Its ism is a hidden “you” (*anta*). Here the khabar is a phrase, مِّنَ ٱلشَّٰكِرِينَ, “among the thankful”.

**كُونُوا** speaks to a group. Its ism is the wāw of the group. In 17:50 the khabar is حِجَارَةً (accusative, -an). The word حَدِيدًا is joined to it with “or”, and it is accusative too. Al-Jadwal says both.

<Note kind="warn">A phrase such as مِّنَ ٱلشَّٰكِرِينَ cannot show -a. Al-Jadwal says it is tied to a left-out khabar, a word like “found”. It stands in the place of the accusative. We call it the khabar of كُنْ, as L2.01 did for a phrase khabar.</Note>

In 4:85 the ism, ٱللَّهُ, is a separate word. Often it is not.

In كُنتُمْ أَعْدَآءً, the ism is the ending **-tum** (“you”) that is attached to the verb. Al-Jadwal calls it “the ism of kāna”, a pronoun in the place of the nominative. There is no separate nominative noun to look for. The only noun left, أَعْدَآءً, is the khabar. It ends in -an (the hamza has its tanwīn written as ءً).

This is the most common shape in the Quran: look at the verb’s own ending first.

<Note kind="tip">If the verb ends in -tu, -ta, -tum, -nā or has a wāw, the ism is inside the verb. Then the next noun is the khabar, and it ends in -a or -an.</Note>

- **أَصْبَحَ** shows a change: “you were enemies; you became brothers”. Its ism is the **-tum** again, and إِخْوَٰنًا is the khabar.
- **ظَلَّ** shows that something keeps on. Its ism is وَجْهُهُۥ (nominative, -u) and its khabar مُسْوَدًّا (accusative, -an, with a shadda on the dal). Al-Jadwal says exactly this.

  <Stage>
    <Sentence

    <Sentence
      roles

        { role: 'ismKana', label: 'ism laysa', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلذَّكَرُ', en: 'the male', say: 'adh-dhakaru' }] },
        { role: 'khabarKana', label: 'khabar laysa', hint: 'a phrase', words: [{ ar: 'كَٱلْأُنثَىٰ', en: 'like the female', say: 'kal-unthā' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And the male is not like the female.']}
    />
  </Stage>

لَيْسَ means “is not”. It works like kāna: the ism is nominative, the khabar accusative.

- In لَسْتَ مُرْسَلًا the ism is the attached **-ta** (“you”), as in كُنتُمْ. The khabar is مُرْسَلًا, with -an.
- In وَلَيْسَ ٱلذَّكَرُ كَٱلْأُنثَىٰ the ism is ٱلذَّكَرُ (-u). The khabar is the phrase كَٱلْأُنثَىٰ, “like the female”. A phrase shows no -a. Al-Jadwal says it is tied to a left-out khabar.

Laysa is **jāmid**, a verb with only a few forms. It looks like a past verb (the endings are the past ones: لَيْسَ, لَسْتَ, لَسْنَ). It has no present form and no command. It is not a tense: it just means “is not”.

<Note kind="warn">The negation is in the meaning of laysa. The grammar is the same as kāna: ism nominative, khabar accusative. A khabar after laysa is not “negative” in some other way.</Note>

The negative word can change. In 11:118 it is لَا (`وَلَا يَزَالُونَ`), in 20:91 it is لَن (`لَن نَّبْرَحَ`). In 12:85 it has been left out: al-Jadwal says it is understood, “you do not stop”. In all three, the verb’s ism is hidden inside it and a khabar follows.

<Step id="shapes" label="Shapes and order" title="The khabar in different shapes">
<Block id="phrase" title="The khabar can be a phrase">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Hud 11:43 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'kāna', hint: 'the verb', words: [{ ar: 'فَكَانَ', en: 'so he was', say: 'fa-kana', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'khabarKana', hint: 'a phrase', words: [{ ar: 'مِنَ', en: 'of', type: 'p' }, { ar: 'ٱلْمُغْرَقِينَ', en: 'the drowned', say: 'al-mughraqina' }] },

      en={['So he was one of the drowned.']}
    />
  </Stage>

This is the quiet shape of the khabar: a phrase with a little word. Nothing in it shows -a, because a phrase has no ending of its own.

The ism is hidden: the pronoun “he”, which stands for Nuh’s son. So here is the whole sentence: kāna, a hidden “he”, and the phrase مِنَ ٱلْمُغْرَقِينَ as the khabar. Al-Jadwal says this about this verse.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal says: the phrase is tied to a khabar that is left out, as in L2.01. We call the phrase “the khabar”, to keep it simple.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="sentence" title="The khabar can be a sentence">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Yusuf 12:85 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'taftaʾu', hint: 'you do not stop', words: [{ ar: 'تَفْتَؤُا۟', en: 'you do not stop', type: 'v' }] },

    />
  </Stage>

In L2.01 you saw that a khabar can be a whole sentence. The same is true after kāna. Here the khabar is a verb with its object: تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ.

A sentence cannot take -a. Al-Jadwal says the sentence is “in the place of the accusative” (*fī maḥall naṣb*), as it said “in the place of the nominative” for a sentence khabar in L2.01.

<Note kind="remember">A khabar that is a phrase or a sentence shows no ending. You still call it the khabar of kāna.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="first" title="The khabar can come first">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Kahf 18:82 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'kāna', hint: 'the verb', words: [{ ar: 'وَكَانَ', en: 'and was', say: 'wa-kana', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'khabarKana', label: 'khabar kāna, put first', hint: 'a place word', words: [{ ar: 'تَحْتَهُۥ', en: 'under it', say: 'tahtahu' }] },
        { role: 'ismKana', label: 'ism kāna, put later', words: [{ ar: 'كَنزٌ', en: 'a treasure', say: 'kanzun' }] },
        { role: 'tied', hint: 'describes كَنزٌ', words: [{ ar: 'لَّهُمَا', en: 'for the two of them', type: 'p' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And under it ', { gap: 'was' }, ' a treasure that belonged to the two of them.']}
    />
  </Stage>

As in L2.01, the khabar can come before the ism. Here the khabar is a place word, تَحْتَهُۥ, “under it”. It ends in **-a**, as place words do. The ism, كَنزٌ, comes later and is nominative (-un).

Why first? Because the ism is **indefinite** (“a treasure”). This is the same reason you met in L2.01 for the khabar muqaddam. Here, the khabar is a place word, so it takes the front. Al-Jadwal says the place word is tied to the khabar of kāna, and the ism of kāna comes later.

<Note kind="tip">A word with -a in front of a word with -un, after kāna: do not take the first one as the ism. The ism is the nominative one.</Note>
</Block>

      ]}
      en={['Their guidance ', { gap: 'is' }, ' not your duty.']}
    />
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:177 (start)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'laysa', hint: 'is not', words: [{ ar: 'لَّيْسَ', en: 'is not', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'khabarKana', label: 'khabar laysa, put first', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْبِرَّ', en: 'righteousness', say: 'al-birra' }] },
        { role: 'ismKana', label: 'ism laysa, put later', hint: 'a whole clause', words: [{ ar: 'أَن', en: 'that', type: 'p' }, { ar: 'تُوَلُّوا۟', en: 'you turn', type: 'v' }, { ar: 'وُجُوهَكُمْ', en: 'your faces' }] },

In 2:272, the phrase عَلَيْكَ is the khabar, put first. The ism is هُدَىٰهُمْ, “their guidance”. It ends in a long ā, so its -u is hidden, like مَوْلَىٰ in L2.01.

In 2:177 the order is the other way round from what you might expect. ٱلْبِرَّ has -a, so it is the **khabar**. The ism comes after it, and it is a whole clause: أَن تُوَلُّوا۟ وُجُوهَكُمْ, “that you turn your faces”. Al-Jadwal says: the khabar of laysa, put first; the clause is the ism, put later, in the place of the nominative.

So the case tells you which is which. The noun with -a is the khabar, even when it comes first.

<Note kind="warn">If you take the first noun after laysa as the ism, you will get this verse upside down. Check the ending first.</Note>

<Block id="kana-inna" title="Kāna inside a sentence with inna">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="An-Nisa 4:33 (end)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'إِنَّ', en: 'indeed', type: 'p' }] },

This pattern is very common: إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ ... Here are the pieces:

- ٱللَّهَ is the **ism of inna**, accusative (-a).
- كَانَ is the verb. Its ism is a hidden “he”, which points back to Allah.
- شَهِيدًا is the **khabar of kāna**, accusative.
- The whole sentence كَانَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ شَهِيدًا is the **khabar of inna**. A sentence there is “in the place of the nominative”.

So there are two accusatives, and each has its own reason. The first comes from inna, the second from kāna. L2.10 teaches inna in full.

<Note kind="warn">The old version of this lesson wrote this verse without إِنَّ and with ٱللَّهُ in the nominative (كَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ شَهِيدًا). That is not the verse. The Quran says ... إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="tamma" title="Kāna with no khabar: tāmma">
  <Stage label="A complete kāna">
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:280 (part)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'وَإِن', en: 'and if', say: 'wa-in', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'kāna', hint: 'there is', words: [{ ar: 'كَانَ', en: 'there is', say: 'kana', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'fail', hint: 'the one who is', words: [{ ar: 'ذُو', en: 'one with', say: 'dhū' }, { ar: 'عُسْرَةٍ', en: 'hardship' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And if there is someone in hardship…']}
    />
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Maryam 19:31 (part)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'أَيْنَ', en: 'wherever', type: 'p' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'مَا', en: 'what', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'kuntu', hint: 'I am (somewhere)', words: [{ ar: 'كُنتُ', en: 'I am', say: 'kuntu', type: 'v' }] },
      ]}
      en={['…wherever I am…']}
    />
  </Stage>

Sometimes كَانَ does not need a khabar. It means “there is”, “to exist” or “to be somewhere”. Al-Jadwal calls this kāna **tāmma**, تَامَّة, “complete”. It is a normal verb, with a doer (fāʿil) in the nominative, and **no** khabar.

- In 2:280, كَانَ has a doer: ذُو عُسْرَةٍ, “someone in hardship”. Nothing is in the accusative. Al-Jadwal reads it as tāmma.
- In 19:31 the same verse has two kinds. أَيْنَ مَا كُنتُ is tāmma: “wherever I am”, so -tu is the doer. But مَا دُمْتُ حَيًّا (end of the verse) is nāqiṣa: the khabar is حَيًّا.

So the test is always the same: is there a noun in the accusative that says what the subject is or was? If yes, kāna is nāqiṣa. If not, it may be tāmma.

<Note kind="know">Al-Durr al-Maṣūn on 2:280 gives two views. The clearer one is the one above: kāna means “comes to be” and has a doer. The other says kāna is nāqiṣa and the khabar was left out, which some Kufan grammarians said. The same book reports readings of Ibn Masʿūd, Ubayy and ʿUthmān with ذَا عُسْرَةٍ in the accusative, which suits that second view.</Note>

<Note kind="know">The old lesson gave مَا دَامَتِ ٱلسَّمَوَاتُ (11:107) as an example of the sister مَا دَامَ. In that verse al-Jadwal reads دَامَتْ as tāmma, with ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتُ as its doer. It has no khabar. Another tāmma is كُن فَيَكُونُ (2:117), “be, and it is”.</Note>

</Block>

</Step>

<Step id="word-by-word" label="Mistakes, word by word" title="Five mistakes, and four verses word by word">
<Block id="mistake-inna" title="Mistake 1: mixing kāna with inna">
  <Stage label="Opposite changes">
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="An-Nisa 4:33 (end)"

        { role: 'tied', hint: 'belongs to شَهِيدًا', words: [{ ar: 'عَلَىٰ', en: 'over', type: 'p' }, { ar: 'كُلِّ', en: 'every' }, { ar: 'شَىْءٍ', en: 'thing' }] },

After إِنَّ the first part has -a. After كَانَ the first part has -u. Learners often say “kāna makes things accusative”. It makes the **khabar** accusative. The ism stays as it was.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-flat" title="Mistake 2: reading kāna as plain “was”">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Imran 3:103 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'kuntum', hint: 'you were', words: [{ ar: 'كُنتُمْ', en: 'you were', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'khabarKana', words: [{ ar: 'أَعْدَآءً', en: 'enemies' }] },
      ]}
      en={['You were enemies.']}
    />
  </Stage>

Kāna can mean “was”, but also “has always been” (with Allah’s names), “is” or “will be” (yakūnu), and “be!” (kun). The meaning comes from the form of the verb and from the verse.

And it is not only kāna. The family also says “became”, “stayed”, “kept on”, “is not”.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-khabar" title="Mistake 3: not looking for the hidden ism">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Hud 11:43 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'kāna', hint: 'the verb', words: [{ ar: 'فَكَانَ', en: 'so he was', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'khabarKana', hint: 'a phrase', words: [{ ar: 'مِنَ', en: 'of', type: 'p' }, { ar: 'ٱلْمُغْرَقِينَ', en: 'the drowned' }] },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

In فَكَانَ مِنَ ٱلْمُغْرَقِينَ there is no nominative noun after the verb. That is not a mistake in the verse. The ism is hidden inside كَانَ: “he”.

Do not turn ٱلْمُغْرَقِينَ into the ism. It ends in -īna, after مِنَ, so it is genitive. It belongs to the phrase that is the khabar.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-order" title="Mistake 4: trusting the order">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:177 (start)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'laysa', hint: 'is not', words: [{ ar: 'لَّيْسَ', en: 'is not', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'khabarKana', label: 'khabar laysa, put first', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْبِرَّ', en: 'righteousness' }] },
        { role: 'ismKana', label: 'ism laysa, put later', words: [{ ar: 'أَن', en: 'that', type: 'p' }, { ar: 'تُوَلُّوا۟', en: 'you turn', type: 'v' }, { ar: 'وُجُوهَكُمْ', en: 'your faces' }] },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The usual order is: verb, ism, khabar. But the khabar can come first (18:82, 2:272, 2:177). Do not decide by position. Decide by the ending: the ism is nominative, and the khabar is accusative.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-tamma" title="Mistake 5: calling every kāna “incomplete”">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:280 (part)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'وَإِن', en: 'and if', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'fiil', label: 'kāna', hint: 'there is', words: [{ ar: 'كَانَ', en: 'there is', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'fail', words: [{ ar: 'ذُو', en: 'one with' }, { ar: 'عُسْرَةٍ', en: 'hardship' }] },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

If you look for a khabar after كَانَ here, you will not find one. This kāna is tāmma. The noun after it is a **doer**, like the doer of any verb in L2.03.

Check for an accusative noun after the verb. It is the best test.
</Block>

<Step id="practice" label="Practice" title="Practice">
<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: find the ism and the khabar">
  <Stage label="For each sentence, say">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>The verb:</b> kāna or a sister?</li>
      <li><b>The ism:</b> a noun, an attached ending, or hidden?</li>
      <li><b>The khabar:</b> a word, a phrase or a sentence?</li>
      <li><b>The mark:</b> why does the khabar end in -a?</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Work out each answer first. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Find the sister of kāna, its ism and its khabar: وَلَا يَزَالُونَ مُخْتَلِفِينَ (Hud 11:118, end). Meaning: “And they do not stop differing.”">

The sister is **يَزَالُونَ** (from مَا زَالَ: “keep on”). Its **ism** is the wāw inside the verb, “they”. Its **khabar** is مُخْتَلِفِينَ, accusative. The mark is **-īna**, because a sound plural shows the accusative with ـِينَ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Which word is the ism of laysa, and what is the khabar? وَلَيْسَ ٱلذَّكَرُ كَٱلْأُنثَىٰ (Al-Imran 3:36, part). Meaning: “And the male is not like the female.”">

ٱلذَّكَرُ is the **ism**, nominative (-u). The khabar is the phrase **كَٱلْأُنثَىٰ**, “like the female”. A phrase shows no ending, so it stands in the place of the accusative.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Give the ism and the khabar of the verb: مَا دُمْتُ حَيًّا (Maryam 19:31, end). Meaning: “…as long as I am alive.”">

دُمْتُ is a sister of kāna (**مَا دَامَ**, “as long as”). The **ism** is the attached **-tu** (“I”). The **khabar** is **حَيًّا**, accusative with -an. Here مَا is not a negative. It makes the time: “as long as”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="The khabar comes first here. Find it and find the ism: وَكَانَ تَحْتَهُۥ كَنزٌ لَّهُمَا (Al-Kahf 18:82, part). Meaning: “And under it was a treasure for the two of them.”">

**تَحْتَهُۥ** (“under it”) is the **khabar**, put first. It is a place word, so it ends in -a. **كَنزٌ** is the **ism**, put later, nominative (-un). The phrase لَّهُمَا describes the treasure.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="This verse has two accusative words. Which comes from inna and which from kāna? إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ شَهِيدًا (An-Nisa 4:33, end). Meaning: “Indeed, Allah has always been a Witness over everything.”">

**ٱللَّهَ** is accusative because of **إِنَّ**: it is the ism of inna. **شَهِيدًا** is accusative because of **كَانَ**: it is the khabar of kāna. The ism of kāna is a hidden “he”. The whole clause from كَانَ to شَهِيدًا is the khabar of inna.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Which kāna has a khabar? (a) وَإِن كَانَ ذُو عُسْرَةٍ (Al-Baqarah 2:280, part) and (b) أَيْنَ مَا كُنتُ (Maryam 19:31, part)?">

Neither. Both are **tāmma**. In (a), ذُو عُسْرَةٍ is the doer, nominative. In (b), the doer is the -tu of كُنتُ. There is no accusative khabar. (Compare مَا دُمْتُ حَيًّا, which has one.)

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Block id="practice-2" title="Practice: more, and making sentences">
  <Stage label="The family">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>كَانَ</b> was, has always been</li>
      <li><b>أَصْبَحَ / ظَلَّ / بَاتَ</b> became, stayed, spent the night</li>
      <li><b>لَيْسَ</b> is not</li>
      <li><b>مَا زَالَ / مَا بَرِحَ / مَا فَتِئَ</b> keep on</li>
      <li><b>مَا دَامَ</b> as long as</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

<Exercise n={7} q="Laysa with the khabar first. Which is the khabar, and what is the ism? لَّيْسَ ٱلْبِرَّ أَن تُوَلُّوا۟ وُجُوهَكُمْ (Al-Baqarah 2:177, start). Meaning: “Righteousness is not that you turn your faces…”">

The khabar is **ٱلْبِرَّ**, put first, accusative (-a). The ism is the whole clause **أَن تُوَلُّوا۟ وُجُوهَكُمْ**, put later: “that you turn your faces”. It stands in the place of the nominative. The case of ٱلْبِرَّ tells you it is not the ism.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Why does the khabar end in -īna, not -ūna? فَظَلَّتْ أَعْنَٰقُهُمْ لَهَا خَٰضِعِينَ (Ash-Shuara 26:4, part). Meaning: “…then their necks would stay bowed to it.”">

ظَلَّتْ is a sister of kāna. Its **ism** is أَعْنَٰقُهُمْ, nominative. Its **khabar** is خَٰضِعِينَ, accusative: the mark of a sound masculine plural in the accusative is **-īna**. Al-Jadwal calls it the khabar, with لَهَا tied to it.

Why a masculine plural for “necks”? Al-Durr al-Maṣūn lists several answers. One is that “necks” means the leaders or the people. Another is that the word “their” brings in the people.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Put kāna in front of this nominal sentence and write the new sentence. ٱلرَّجُلُ صَابِرٌ (The man is patient). This is a made-up example.">

كَانَ ٱلرَّجُلُ صَابِرًا, “The man was patient.”

ٱلرَّجُلُ keeps its -u: it is now the **ism kāna**. صَابِرٌ changes from -un to **-an**: it is the **khabar kāna**.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={10} q="Make the same change with a feminine mubtadaʾ, using كَانَ: ٱلْمَرْأَةُ صَابِرَةٌ (The woman is patient). This is a made-up example.">

كَانَتِ ٱلْمَرْأَةُ صَابِرَةً, “The woman was patient.”

The verb takes the feminine ending: كَانَتْ. In the usual spelling its last letter gets a kasra, كَانَتِ, before the *al-* of the next word. ٱلْمَرْأَةُ stays nominative (-u) and صَابِرَةً turns accusative (-an).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={11} q="Make the nominal sentence negative with laysa, and change the endings: ٱلْوَلَدُ مَرِيضٌ (The boy is ill). This is a made-up example.">

لَيْسَ ٱلْوَلَدُ مَرِيضًا, “The boy is not ill.”

ٱلْوَلَدُ keeps its -u: it is the **ism laysa**. مَرِيضٌ changes to **مَرِيضًا** (-an): it is the **khabar laysa**.

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="kāna plus subject plus khabar equals a changed nominal sentence"
      items={[
        { kind: 'v', text: 'kāna', small: 'كَانَ' },
        { kind: 'm', text: 'ism kāna', small: 'stays -u' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'khabar kāna', small: 'turns -a' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- Kāna and her sisters (the thirteen in *al-Ajurrumiyyah*, and the forms made from them) go in front of a nominal sentence. The first part stays nominative: the **ism kāna**. The khabar turns accusative: the **khabar kāna**.
- They are verbs, so they have forms: كَانَ, كُنتُمْ, يَكُونُ, كُنْ, كُونُوا. Laysa has only a few.
- The ism can be a noun, an attached ending (كُنتُمْ, لَسْتَ, دُمْتُ) or hidden inside the verb (كُونُوا, فَكَانَ).
- The khabar can be a word, a phrase, a place word or a sentence. It can come first. The case, not the place, tells you which is which.
- With Allah’s names, this course writes “has always been”.
- Kāna can also be **tāmma**: “there is”, with a doer and no khabar.
- After inna, the first part is accusative. After kāna, the khabar is. A verse can have both (4:33).
