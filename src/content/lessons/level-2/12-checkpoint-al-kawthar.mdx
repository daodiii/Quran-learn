Read each verse from right to left. The English under each verse is our own plain meaning.

Count the written words: three, three and four. That makes **ten words**, as the Quranic Arabic Corpus counts them. A written word can hold more than one piece. إِنَّآ is two pieces (*inna* and *-nā*). أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ is three (the verb, *-nā* and *-ka*). The next steps take the pieces apart.

Verse 1 is an inna sentence (L2.10). Its pieces are:

- **inna** and its first part, **-nā** (“we”). Together they are written إِنَّآ.
- **The khabar of inna.** It is a whole verbal sentence: أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ ٱلْكَوْثَرَ, “we gave you Al-Kawthar”.

Inside that verbal sentence you find the three parts from L2.03: the verb, the doer (-nā) and the object (-ka, then ٱلْكَوْثَرَ). Al-Jadwal says it in one line: the sentence أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ ٱلْكَوْثَرَ is in the place of the nominative, as the khabar of inna.

إِنَّآ is written as one word, but the pronoun نَا (*nā*, “we”) is only written onto the end of إِنَّ.

- إِنَّ is a little word. It has no case.
- نَا is the **ism inna**, the first part. After inna the first part is accusative (L2.05, L2.10). A pronoun never changes its ending, so the grammar books say it is “in the place of” the accusative.
- The khabar of inna stays nominative (L2.04). Here the khabar is a whole sentence, so it shows no ending.

<Note kind="warn">The same letters نَا come again inside أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ. There they are the **doer**, not the ism inna. Same letters, different job. Look at the word each one is joined to.</Note>

أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ is a complete verbal sentence up to its second object (L2.03).

- **أَعْطَيْ** is the verb, “gave”. A verb has no case.
- **نَا** is the doer, “we”.
- **كَ** is the first object, “you”: the one who received.

The verb “give” takes two objects. L2.03 showed the same pattern in ءَاتَيْنَٰكَ سَبْعًا (15:87): *-nā* the doer, *-ka* the first object, and a second object after it. In our verse the second object is ٱلْكَوْثَرَ.

<Note kind="warn">The attached **-ka** after a verb is the object, “you”. After a noun it means “your” (رَبِّكَ, verse 2). Look at the word it is joined to: a verb or a noun (L2.03, L2.08).</Note>

<Note kind="know">The form of the verb comes from Level 3: the past tense (L3.03) and Form IV (L3.14). The attached pronouns come in L3.09. Al-Durr al-Masun records that some early readers read this word with a nūn, أَنْطَيْنَٰكَ, and calls it a real old Arab form. This lesson follows the usual reading.</Note>

ٱلْكَوْثَرَ is a definite noun (it has ٱل). It is the **second object**: the thing that was given. It ends in **-a** (a fatḥa), the mark of the accusative (L2.05).

What does it mean? Al-Jadwal says it is a name for a river in Paradise, or a describing word for a left-out noun, “the great good”. It records sixteen sayings about the meaning. It also gives the word's build: the pattern فَوْعَل from the root ك ث ر (“much”). Al-Durr al-Masun says this pattern gives the idea of very much. Level 3 teaches roots and patterns (L3.01).

Verse 1 has **three** words that are accusative by place. Each has its own reason.

| Word | Job | Why accusative | Taught in |
|---|---|---|---|
| نَا in إِنَّآ | ism inna | It is the first part after inna. | [L2.10](/learn/level-2/10-inna-sisters/), [L2.05](/learn/level-2/05-accusative-case/) |
| كَ in أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ | first object | It is what the verb is done to. | [L2.03](/learn/level-2/03-verbal-sentence/), [L2.05](/learn/level-2/05-accusative-case/) |
| ٱلْكَوْثَرَ | second object | It is what the verb is done to. | [L2.03](/learn/level-2/03-verbal-sentence/), [L2.05](/learn/level-2/05-accusative-case/) |

Verse 2 changes from a statement to commands. It has two verbal sentences (L2.03):

- فَصَلِّ لِرَبِّكَ, “so pray to your Lord”.
- وَٱنْحَرْ, “and sacrifice”.

Each one starts with a verb. There is no written doer and no object. The doer is there, but hidden.
</Block>

<Block id="v2-command" title="A command and its hidden doer">
  <Stage label="The doer you cannot see">
    <Tiles
      label="the command pray, and the hidden doer you"
      items={[
        { ar: 'صَلِّ', en: 'pray!', type: 'v' },
        { ghost: 'you', note: 'hidden doer' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A command is a verb. Its doer is “you”, the person who is told. You do not say “you” (أَنتَ), so the doer is **hidden** inside the verb. L2.03 showed this with تَقَبَّلْ (3:35). Al-Jadwal writes it the same way for a command: the doer is “you”.

A verb has no case. A command is built on a fixed ending that does not change with its job.

- **صَلِّ** lost its last letter, a weak letter ى (the root is ص ل و). Al-Jadwal calls this “built on dropping the weak letter”.
- **ٱنْحَرْ** ends in a sukūn, a letter with no vowel.

Level 3 teaches commands (L3.06).

<Note kind="warn">Do not look for a written doer before or after a command. Every verb has a doer. Here it is hidden (L2.03).</Note>

<Note kind="know">Al-Durr al-Masun says the thing ٱنْحَرْ is tied to is left out: “sacrifice to Him”. It also notes that نَحْر is the word for killing a camel, and that some say the verse means something else: placing the hands at the chest in prayer. The grammar is the same either way.</Note>

</Block>

Two little words join the sentences of this surah.

- **فَ** at the start of the verse joins it to verse 1. Al-Jadwal says the فَ joins and shows a cause: the gift is the reason for the command. Al-Durr al-Masun says the same: the gift is the cause, and thanks and worship follow it.
- **وَ** before ٱنْحَرْ joins the second command to the first.

Al-Jadwal adds that the sentence صَلِّ is joined to a sentence that is not written, something like “pay attention to this, so pray”. A sentence joined to that has no place of its own. The same goes for ٱنْحَرْ, joined to صَلِّ.

<Block id="v3-hater" title="شَانِئَكَ: ism inna and an idafah">
  <Stage label="Two pieces">
    <Tiles
      label="shāniʾa, ka"

شَانِئَكَ has two pieces.

- **شَانِئَ** is the ism inna. It ends in **-a**, the accusative. It is also the first word of an idafah.
- **كَ** is the second word of the idafah, in the place of the genitive (L2.08, L2.06). It is the same ending as in رَبِّكَ, and the pattern is the same: a noun and a pronoun. Here the meaning is “you” (the one who is hated). Al-Jadwal reads the same shape in 9:103, إِنَّ صَلَوٰتَكَ: the noun is the ism inna and the كَ is the muḍāf ilayh.

شَانِئ comes from a verb, “to hate” (root ش ن أ). Al-Jadwal calls it a doer-word, an active participle, on the pattern فَاعِل. Level 3 teaches these (L3.19).

<Note kind="know">Al-Durr al-Masun records another reading, from Ibn ʿAbbās: شَنِئَكَ, without the alif. It says this may be a pattern of strong meaning. The reading in our text is the one used here.</Note>

The pronoun هُوَ can be read in more than one way. Al-Jadwal gives two.

1. **A separating pronoun** (ضَمِير فَصْل, *ḍamīr al-faṣl*). It stands between the two parts of the sentence and is not one of them. It adds emphasis: “he, and no one else”. Then ٱلْأَبْتَرُ is the khabar of inna. This is Al-Jadwal's main reading, and it is the one in the panel at the end of this step.
2. **A mubtadaʾ.** Then هُوَ ٱلْأَبْتَرُ is a nominal sentence of its own (L2.01): هُوَ is the mubtadaʾ, a pronoun that counts as nominative, and ٱلْأَبْتَرُ is its khabar. The whole sentence is then the khabar of inna.

Both readings give ٱلْأَبْتَرُ the same ending, -u. So your answer for that word does not change.

<Note kind="know">Al-Durr al-Masun allows both readings. It names a third, from Abū al-Baqāʾ: that هُوَ is an emphasis word for شَانِئَكَ. Al-Durr calls that a mistake, because a full noun is not given emphasis by a pronoun. It adds that both of the first two readings give emphasis: the separating pronoun also says “this one only”, and a mubtadaʾ says the statement twice.</Note>

<Note kind="tip">Why put a pronoun here at all? Without it, شَانِئَكَ ٱلْأَبْتَرُ would be two definite words side by side. L2.01 taught that two definite words can read as one phrase: “your cut-off hater”. The pronoun in the middle shows that ٱلْأَبْتَرُ is the news.</Note>

  <Stage label="Root and pattern">

ٱلْأَبْتَرُ is the **khabar of inna**. It stays nominative, with **-u** (L2.04, L2.10).

- It is **definite** (it has ٱل). A definite khabar says who the person is: he is *the* cut-off one (L2.02). Al-Durr al-Masun says the ٱل shows that he is the one complete in this quality.
- It is masculine and singular, like هُوَ and like شَانِئ, so the parts match (L2.02, L2.09).
- Al-Jadwal says it is a describing word on the pattern أَفْعَل, from the root ب ت ر (“to cut”). It means cut off from offspring. Al-Durr al-Masun explains the root: the first idea is a thing that is cut off.

<Note kind="know">ٱلْأَبْتَرُ has the shape of a describing word, but here it is a **khabar**, not a naʿt (a describing word that follows its noun). A naʿt would sit right after the noun it describes and copy its case (L2.09). In this verse the pronoun هُوَ stands between them.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="m1" title="Mistake 1: giving the two -nā the same job">

The word “he” is easy to take for the subject. In the English, “he is cut off”, it is the subject. In the Arabic, the ism inna is **شَانِئَكَ**, the word right after inna, and it is accusative.

A separate pronoun like هُوَ is not the ism inna. When the ism of inna is a pronoun, it is joined to inna, as -nā is in إِنَّآ (verse 1). Here the ism is شَانِئَ, and its -a shows it.
</Block>

<Block id="m3" title="Mistake 3: thinking the first word of an idafah is always genitive">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence

        { role: 'mudaf', label: 'muḍāf: genitive', hint: 'genitive because of the preposition لِ', words: [{ ar: 'لِرَبِّ', en: 'to the Lord of' }] },

    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Kawthar 108:3 (start)"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'إِنَّ', en: 'indeed', type: 'p' }] },

    />

رَبِّ in verse 2 and شَانِئَ in verse 3 are both the first word of an idafah. They end differently.

- رَبِّ is **genitive**: the preposition لِ pulls it.
- شَانِئَ is **accusative**: it is the ism inna.

The second word of an idafah is always genitive. The first word takes the case its own place gives it (L2.06, L2.08).

</Block>

How many genitive words are in the surah? If you look only for a kasra, you find one: رَبِّ. There are **three**. The two pronouns -ka are genitive too, because each is the second word of an idafah. A pronoun never shows its case, but it has one (L2.06).

The same goes for the other cases. Of the words that have a case, only four show it with a visible ending: ٱلْكَوْثَرَ, شَانِئَ, رَبِّ and ٱلْأَبْتَرُ.
</Block>

<Exercise n={1} q="Find the ism inna and the khabar inna in each sentence, and give their cases: إِنَّآ أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ ٱلْكَوْثَرَ (Al-Kawthar 108:1) and إِنَّ شَانِئَكَ هُوَ ٱلْأَبْتَرُ (Al-Kawthar 108:3).">

**Verse 1.** The ism inna is **نَا**, “we”: accusative by place. The khabar is the whole sentence أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ ٱلْكَوْثَرَ. A sentence has no ending, but it stands in the place of the nominative.

**Verse 3.** The ism inna is **شَانِئَ**: accusative, **-a**. The khabar is **ٱلْأَبْتَرُ**: nominative, **-u**. (In the second reading, the khabar is the sentence هُوَ ٱلْأَبْتَرُ.)

Both sentences follow the same rule: the ism is accusative and the khabar is nominative. Only the kind of khabar differs: a verbal sentence in verse 1, one word in verse 3. The هُوَ is not the ism inna, and in the first reading it is not part of the khabar.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Find every genitive word in the surah and say why it is genitive.">

There are **three**.

1. **رَبِّ** (in لِرَبِّكَ): genitive, **-i**. It follows the preposition لِ (L2.07).
2. **كَ** (in رَبِّكَ): genitive by place. It is the second word of the idafah “your Lord” (L2.08).
3. **كَ** (in شَانِئَكَ): genitive by place. It is the second word of the idafah “your hater” (L2.08).

Every genitive here comes from one of two causes: a preposition, or the second word of an idafah. (Where the old version of this lesson counted two, the third is the -ka of شَانِئَكَ.)

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Find every attached pronoun in the surah. For each, say what it means, what it is joined to, and its job.">

There are **five**.

| Pronoun | In | Meaning | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| نَا | إِنَّآ | we | ism inna, accusative by place |
| نَا | أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ | we | doer, nominative by place |
| كَ | أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ | you | first object, accusative by place |
| كَ | لِرَبِّكَ | your | second word of an idafah, genitive by place |
| كَ | شَانِئَكَ | you (the one who hates you) | second word of an idafah, genitive by place |

Two (the -nā) refer to Allah. Three (the -ka) refer to the Prophet. The separate pronoun هُوَ is not attached. It is a word on its own.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Which words in the surah show their case with a visible ending? Give the ending and the case.">

Four words:

- **ٱلْكَوْثَرَ**: **-a**, accusative.
- **شَانِئَ**: **-a**, accusative.
- **رَبِّ**: **-i**, genitive.
- **ٱلْأَبْتَرُ**: **-u**, nominative.

The pronouns (نَا, كَ, هُوَ) never show a case. The verbs and the little words have none.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Both verses end in the word Allah, but the endings differ: فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ (Al-Imran 3:76, end) and وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا (An-Nisa 4:96, end). Name the parts and say why ٱللَّهَ and ٱللَّهُ differ.">

**3:76.** إِنَّ makes ٱللَّهَ the **ism inna**: accusative, **-a**. The khabar is the sentence يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ, in the place of the nominative. Inside it, ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ is the object: accusative, shown by **-īna** (L2.05).

**4:96.** كَانَ makes ٱللَّهُ the **ism kāna**: nominative, **-u**. The khabar kāna is غَفُورًا: accusative, **-an**. رَحِيمًا is a second khabar, also **-an**.

The two words work in opposite ways. After **inna**, the first part is accusative and the khabar stays nominative. After **kāna**, the first part stays nominative and the khabar is accusative (L2.10, L2.11). Al-Jadwal agrees with both parses.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Look at this command: ٱسْتَعِينُوا۟ بِٱلصَّبْرِ وَٱلصَّلَوٰةِ (Al-Baqarah 2:153, part). Meaning: “Ask for help with patience and prayer.” Find the doer, say what the phrase is tied to, and give the cases of ٱلصَّبْرِ and ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ.">

The verb is a command, like صَلِّ. Its **doer** is the ending **-ū**, “you all”: an attached doer (L2.03). In صَلِّ the doer was hidden.

بِٱلصَّبْرِ is a preposition phrase **tied to the verb** (L2.07). ٱلصَّبْرِ is genitive, **-i**, after بِ. ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ is joined to ٱلصَّبْرِ by وَ, and it is genitive too, **-i**, like the word before it (L2.06). Al-Jadwal says: “joined to al-ṣabr, genitive like it”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Start from ٱلْعِلْمُ نُورٌ (“Knowledge is a light”, a made-up example from L2.01). (a) Put إِنَّ in front. (b) Put كَانَ in front. Say what changes. Both new sentences are made-up examples.">

**(a)** إِنَّ ٱلْعِلْمَ نُورٌ. ٱلْعِلْمَ becomes the ism inna: **-a**, accusative. نُورٌ stays nominative: **-un**.

**(b)** كَانَ ٱلْعِلْمُ نُورًا. ٱلْعِلْمُ stays nominative, **-u**, as the ism kāna. نُورًا becomes the khabar kāna: **-an**, accusative.

Verse 3 shows (a) in the surah. (b) does not appear in Al-Kawthar.

</Exercise>

- Surah Al-Kawthar has **three verses and ten written words**. Its pieces number eighteen: ten nouns and pronouns, three verbs and five little words.
- **Verses 1 and 3** are inna sentences. The ism inna takes **-a**. The khabar stays **-u**: a verbal sentence in verse 1, one word in verse 3.
- **Verse 2** has two commands. Each is a verbal sentence with a hidden “you”. فَ shows cause, and لِرَبِّكَ is tied to صَلِّ.
- All three cases appear. Nominative: ٱلْأَبْتَرُ. Accusative: ٱلْكَوْثَرَ and شَانِئَ. Genitive: رَبِّ. The pronouns have their cases by place.
- **هُوَ** has two readings. Al-Jadwal prefers the separating pronoun and allows a mubtadaʾ. Both give ٱلْأَبْتَرُ the same -u.
