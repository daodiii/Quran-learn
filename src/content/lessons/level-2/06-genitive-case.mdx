This is part of a verse that tells the believers how to ask Allah for help. Read it from right to left.

Look at the last letter of the two nouns. ٱلصَّبْرِ ends in **-i**, a small slanted stroke under the letter. ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ ends in **-i** too.

You know two case endings from L1.10: **-u** for the nominative and **-a** for the accusative. This is the third: **-i**. It is called the **genitive**, جَرّ (*jarr*).

</Block>

Jarr means “dragging” or “pulling”. The idea is that something in front of the noun pulls it down into the -i ending. The noun that has been pulled is called **majrūr**, مَجْرُور.

The same word can wear all three endings. ٱلْكِتَابُ, ٱلْكِتَابَ and ٱلْكِتَابِ are all “the book”. Which ending it gets depends on what the word is doing in the sentence. These three words are a made-up example.

<Note kind="know">Some old grammar books, including *al-Ajurrumiyyah*, call this case **khafḍ**, خَفْض, “lowering”. It is the same case with another name. This course uses *jarr*, as al-Jadwal does.</Note>
</Block>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* is a short grammar book by Ibn Ājurrūm (died 723 AH). Its chapter on the genitive says that there are three kinds of genitive noun:

1. **After a little word** (a preposition): ٱلصَّبْرِ in “with patience”.
2. **As the second word of an idafah**: the “of …” part in “the share of the two females”.
3. **A word that follows a genitive word** and copies its case: ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ in “with patience and prayer”.

This is the plan of the next step. Level 2 teaches the first two in more depth: L2.07 (prepositions) and L2.08 (the idafah). Here you learn what each one looks like and why the noun is genitive.

<Block id="preposition" title="Cause 1: after a little word">
  <Stage>

      en={['…on guidance from their Lord.']}

The most common cause is a **preposition**, حَرْف جَرّ (*ḥarf jarr*, “a little word that pulls”). Here there are two: عَلَىٰ (on) and مِن (from).

- عَلَىٰ pulls هُدًى into the genitive.
- مِن pulls رَبِّ into the genitive. It ends in the **kasra** that goes with the shadda: رَبِّ.

The preposition itself has **no case**. It is a little word, and little words do not change. It only pulls the noun after it.

Some common prepositions: فِى (in), مِن (from), إِلَىٰ (to), عَلَىٰ (on), لِ (for, belonging to), بِ (with, by), عَن (about, from), كَ (like). L2.07 teaches them one by one.

<Note kind="remember">A preposition has no case. The noun after it is genitive.</Note>
</Block>

  <Stage>

      ]}
      en={['…from their Lord.']}

Look again at رَبِّهِمْ, “their Lord”. It has two parts: رَبِّ, “Lord of”, and هِمْ, “them”. This is a small idafah: “Lord of them”.

The word هِمْ is a pronoun joined to the end of the word. A pronoun does not change its ending. But it still counts as genitive, because it is the second word of the idafah. Al-Jadwal says it is “in the place of” the genitive (*fī maḥall jarr*). You met the same wording for a pronoun that counts as nominative in L2.01.

The same happens after a preposition: لَهُ (for him), فِيهِ (in it), مِنْهُمْ (from them). The pronoun is genitive, and you cannot see it.
</Block>

<Block id="idafah" title="Cause 2: the second word of an idafah">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence

        { role: 'mudafIlayh', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْأُنثَيَيْنِ', en: 'the two females', say: 'al-unthayayni' }] },
      ]}
      en={['For the male ', { gap: 'is' }, ' the like of the share of the two females.']}
    />

The second cause is the **idafah**, إِضَافَة: two nouns in a row, meaning “the X of Y”. The first word is the **muḍāf**. The second word is the **muḍāf ilayh**, مُضَاف إِلَيْه, “the one it is added to”.

This verse, about shares in an inheritance, has two idafahs joined in a chain:

- مِثْلُ حَظِّ: “the like of the share”
- حَظِّ ٱلْأُنثَيَيْنِ: “the share of the two females”

Each **second** word is genitive: حَظِّ and ٱلْأُنثَيَيْنِ both end in the genitive. The first word مِثْلُ is not genitive. It ends in -u, because it is the mubtadaʾ of the sentence. The phrase لِلذَّكَرِ before it is the khabar, put first (L2.01).

<Note kind="remember">The second word of an idafah is always genitive. The first word takes the case its own place in the sentence needs. L2.08 teaches the idafah in full.</Note>

</Block>

  <Stage>

      ]}
      en={['…in the creation of the heavens and the earth.']}

Two causes can meet in one phrase. Take them one word at a time.

- خَلْقِ is genitive because of **فِى**. This is its only reason.
- ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ is genitive because it is the **second word** of the idafah خَلْقِ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ.
- ٱلْأَرْضِ is genitive because it **follows** ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ (see the next block).

خَلْقِ is also the first word of an idafah. That does not add a second reason. Being the first word of an idafah never makes a word genitive. So each genitive word has exactly one cause.

<Note kind="tip">To find the cause, ask: is there a preposition right before this word? If not, is it the second word of an idafah? If not, does it copy the word before it?</Note>
</Block>

  <Stage>
    <Sentence

      ]}
      en={['…on a straight path.']}
    />

The third cause is a **following word**, تَابِع (*tābiʿ*). It is a word that copies the case of the word it follows. Two kinds appear in this lesson:

- **An adjective**, نَعْت (*naʿt*). Here مُسْتَقِيمٍ, “straight”, describes صِرَٰطٍ, “a path”. Because صِرَٰطٍ is genitive, so is مُسْتَقِيمٍ. Al-Jadwal calls it “a naʿt of صِرَاط, genitive”.
- **A word joined with “and”**, called *maʿṭūf*. You saw ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ after “and” in 2:153. Al-Jadwal calls it “joined to الصبر, genitive like it”. In 3:190, ٱلْأَرْضِ is joined to ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ in the same way.

The copying word is not pulled by anything in front of it. It only copies. That is why *al-Ajurrumiyyah* lists it apart, as its own kind. The book names two more following words, a word of emphasis and a replacement word. This lesson does not need them.

L2.09 teaches adjectives in full.

<Block id="which-cause" title="Which cause is it? A second look">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:153 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mudaf', hint: 'a place word, “with”, ends in -a', words: [{ ar: 'مَعَ', en: 'with', say: 'maʿa' }] },
        { role: 'mudafIlayh', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ', en: 'the patient', say: 'as-sabirina' }] },
      ]}
      en={['…with those who are patient.']}
    />
  </Stage>

Here ٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ comes after مَعَ, “with”. Is مَعَ a preposition? It is not one of the little words, but it is a **place word** (*ẓarf*, L2.01). It sits in front of the noun like a preposition, but the noun is the second word of an idafah: the “with” of the patient ones.

Al-Jadwal reads it this way: مَعَ is a place word in the accusative, and ٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ is its muḍāf ilayh, genitive. So the cause is the idafah, not a preposition.

<Note kind="know">English “with” is a preposition. The Arabic word مَعَ is a noun, so it can be the first word of an idafah. Do not sort Arabic words by what their English meaning is.</Note>

<Step id="marks" label="The marks" title="How to see that a word is genitive">
<Block id="marks-table" title="The marks of the genitive">
  <Stage label="From al-Ajurrumiyyah">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote lx-quote-list" lang="ar" dir="rtl">
      <p>وَلِلْخَفْضِ ثَلَاثُ عَلَامَاتٍ:</p>
      <p>الْكَسْرَةُ وَالْيَاءُ وَالْفَتْحَةُ</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>
  </Stage>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* says the genitive has three marks: the kasra, the letter yāʾ, and the fatḥa. Here is where each one appears:

| Word | Mark | Example in this lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Singular, one word | **-i** or **-in** (kasra) | ٱلصَّبْرِ, صِرَٰطٍ |
| Sound feminine plural (ends in ـَات) | **-i** (kasra) | ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ |
| Broken plural | **-i** or **-in** (kasra) | رِجَالٍ (made-up example) |
| Dual | **ـَيْنِ** (yāʾ) | ٱلْأُنثَيَيْنِ |
| Sound masculine plural | **ـِينَ** (yāʾ) | ٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ |
| A word that cannot show its mark | the mark is **hidden** | هُدًى |
| A diptote (a word that gets no -in) | **-a** (fatḥa) | فِرْعَوْنَ |

The next blocks take them one by one. A broken plural can also be a diptote. Then its genitive ends in -a, like فِرْعَوْنَ.

<Note kind="know">The “diptote” is *mamnūʿ min al-ṣarf*, مَمْنُوع مِنَ ٱلصَّرْف, “a word kept from the full ending”. The name is long. What matters here is that the genitive of such a word ends in -a.</Note>
</Block>

Most genitive words you meet are singular, and they show the genitive with a kasra.

- A word with *al-* ends in **-i**: ٱلصَّبْرِ.
- A word without *al-* ends in **-in**: صِرَٰطٍ and مُّسْتَقِيمٍ. These are two small kasras under the last letter (كَسْرَتَانِ), as you learned in L1.10.

The sound feminine plural, with ـَات, takes a plain kasra as well. ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ in 3:190 is one. Its genitive looks the same as its accusative.

</Block>

<Block id="yaa" title="A letter instead of a vowel: the dual and the plural">
  <Stage>

    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:153 (end)"

A dual word (two of something) and a sound masculine plural show their case with a **letter**, not a vowel.

- The dual ends in **ـَيْنِ**: ٱلْأُنثَيَيْنِ, “the two females”.
- The sound masculine plural ends in **ـِينَ**: ٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ, “the patient ones”.

In both, the letter that shows the genitive is **ي** (yāʾ). Al-Jadwal says the same for both verses: the mark of the genitive is the yāʾ. In the nominative these words end differently: ـَانِ for the dual and ـُونَ for the plural (L2.04).

<Note kind="warn">The dual and the sound masculine plural look the **same** in the genitive and in the accusative (L2.05). Only the place of the word in the sentence tells you which one it is.</Note>

</Block>

<Block id="hidden" title="A mark you cannot see">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Baqarah 2:5 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'jar', words: [{ ar: 'عَلَىٰ', en: 'on', say: 'ala', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'majrur', hint: 'a hidden kasra', words: [{ ar: 'هُدًى', en: 'guidance', say: 'hudan' }] },
      ]}
      en={['…on guidance.']}
    />
  </Stage>

هُدًى, “guidance”, ends in a long **ā**, written ى. A long ā cannot carry a kasra. So the kasra is **hidden**.

The word is still genitive. Al-Jadwal says the mark is a kasra that is understood on the alif, because it is too hard to say. The grammar books say *muqaddara*, “understood but not seen”.

You met the same idea in L2.01, where a hidden -u sat on مَوْلَىٰ. A word that ends in a long ā still has a case, whatever its job. You just cannot see it.

<Note kind="tip">When a word ends in ى or ا, look at what is in front of it. A preposition in front means the word is genitive, even if no kasra is written.</Note>
</Block>

<Block id="diptote" title="A genitive that ends in -a">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Taha 20:24 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'fiil', words: [{ ar: 'ٱذْهَبْ', en: 'go', say: 'idhhab', type: 'v' }] },
        { role: 'jar', words: [{ ar: 'إِلَىٰ', en: 'to', say: 'ila', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'majrur', hint: 'genitive, but ends in -a', words: [{ ar: 'فِرْعَوْنَ', en: 'Pharaoh', say: 'firawna' }] },
      ]}
      en={['Go to Pharaoh.']}
    />
  </Stage>

Here the preposition إِلَىٰ pulls فِرْعَوْنَ into the genitive. But فِرْعَوْنَ ends in **-a**, which looks like an accusative.

It is a **diptote**. Some words, many of them names like Pharaoh, are kept from the full ending. They never take -in, and their genitive is a fatḥa. *Al-Ajurrumiyyah* says: “the fatḥa is the mark of the genitive in the noun that is not fully inflected.” Al-Jadwal says the same of this word: “the mark of the genitive is the fatḥa, because it is kept from the full ending”.

You need not learn which words are diptotes yet. Learn this one rule: **a preposition in front means genitive, even when the word ends in -a**.

<Note kind="warn">Do not decide the case from the ending alone. Look at what is in front of the word first.</Note>

<Block id="three-cases" title="The three cases side by side">
  <Stage label="The same noun, three cases">
    <Tiles
      label="the book in three sentences"
      items={[
        { ar: 'ٱلْكِتَابُ جَدِيدٌ', en: 'nominative: -u' },
        { ar: 'قَرَأْتُ ٱلْكِتَابَ', en: 'accusative: -a' },
        { ar: 'فِى ٱلْكِتَابِ', en: 'genitive: -i' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

You now know all three cases. This table repeats them for each kind of word. These words are made-up examples.

| Kind of word | Nominative | Accusative | Genitive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Singular with *al-* | ٱلْكِتَابُ | ٱلْكِتَابَ | ٱلْكِتَابِ |
| Singular without *al-* | كِتَابٌ | كِتَابًا | كِتَابٍ |
| Dual | كِتَابَانِ | كِتَابَيْنِ | كِتَابَيْنِ |
| Sound masculine plural | مُسْلِمُونَ | مُسْلِمِينَ | مُسْلِمِينَ |
| Sound feminine plural | مُسْلِمَاتٌ | مُسْلِمَاتٍ | مُسْلِمَاتٍ |

Three things to see:

- A singular word has three different endings.
- The dual and the sound masculine plural have two: one for the nominative, and one shared by the accusative and genitive.
- The sound feminine plural has two as well. The nominative is -un, and the accusative and genitive are both **-in** here (without *al-*). With *al-*, the accusative and genitive are both -i.
</Block>
</Step>

<Block id="mistake-first" title="Mistake 1: making the first word of an idafah genitive">

      ]}
      en={['In the student’s book.']}
    />

The old rule “the second word of an idafah is genitive” is easy to turn around. Only the **second** word is always genitive. The first word takes the case its own job needs.

- In the first sentence كِتَابُ is the mubtadaʾ, so it is nominative.
- In the second it is the object of “read”, so it is accusative: كِتَابَ.
- In the third it comes after فِى, so it is genitive: كِتَابِ.

In all three, ٱلطَّالِبِ stays genitive. You can build a longer chain the same way: كِتَابُ صَدِيقِ ٱلطَّالِبِ, “the book of the friend of the student”. Both صَدِيقِ and ٱلطَّالِبِ are genitive.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-two" title="Mistake 2: giving a word two reasons">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Imran 3:190 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'jar', words: [{ ar: 'فِى', en: 'in', type: 'p' }] },
        { role: 'mudaf', hint: 'genitive because of فِى only', words: [{ ar: 'خَلْقِ', en: 'the creation of' }] },
        { role: 'mudafIlayh', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ', en: 'the heavens' }] },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A word can sit in two roles at once. In خَلْقِ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ, the word خَلْقِ is the first word of an idafah. It also comes after فِى.

Learners sometimes say “it is genitive for two reasons”. That is not right. Each genitive word has **one** cause. For خَلْقِ the cause is فِى. Being the first word of an idafah says what role it has toward the next word. It does not give it a case.

The same is true of the preposition. The preposition is not genitive, and it does not become genitive by pulling. It just pulls.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-same" title="Mistake 3: mixing up the accusative and the genitive">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      reference="Made-up example"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'رَأَيْتُ', en: 'I saw', type: 'v' }] },

      en={['I saw the two men.']}
    />
    <Sentence
      reference="Made-up example"
      groups={[
        { words: [{ ar: 'مَعَ', en: 'with' }] },
        { words: [{ ar: 'ٱلرَّجُلَيْنِ', en: 'the two men' }] },

The same word ٱلرَّجُلَيْنِ is accusative in the first line and genitive in the second. Nothing in its spelling changes.

In the first, it is the thing seen, the object, so it is accusative. In the second, it follows مَعَ, so it is genitive. For a dual or a sound masculine plural, only the sentence tells you which.

<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: find the cause and the mark">

<Exercise n={1} q="Find the two genitive words, and say why each is genitive: مِنَ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ ٱلرَّجِيمِ (An-Nahl 16:98, end). Meaning: “from the cursed devil.”">

- ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ, “the devil”: genitive, **-i**. The preposition مِنَ pulls it.
- ٱلرَّجِيمِ, “the cursed”: genitive, **-i**. It is an adjective of ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ, so it copies its case. (Al-Jadwal: “a naʿt of الشيطان, genitive”.)

مِنَ itself has no case.

</Exercise>

- أُو۟لِى, “people of”: genitive. The preposition لِ pulls it. Its mark is **yāʾ**: it works like a sound masculine plural, and al-Jadwal says the same: “joined to the sound masculine plural”. It is also the first word of an idafah.
- ٱلْأَلْبَٰبِ, “the minds”: genitive, **-i**. It is the second word of an idafah (muḍāf ilayh).

- (e) **-in**: no *al-*, so two small kasras.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Put the word ٱلْكِتَابُ (the book) into three sentences, one for each case, and say what causes the ending. These are made-up examples.">

1. ٱلْكِتَابُ جَدِيدٌ, “The book is new.” **Nominative** (-u). It is the mubtadaʾ.
2. قَرَأْتُ ٱلْكِتَابَ, “I read the book.” **Accusative** (-a). It is the object of the verb.
3. فِى ٱلْكِتَابِ, “In the book.” **Genitive** (-i). The preposition فِى pulls it.

The same noun, three endings. The ending follows the job of the word.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Which words are genitive in كِتَابُ صَدِيقِ ٱلطَّالِبِ, and which are not? Then add فِى in front. This is a made-up example.">

- كِتَابُ, “the book of”: **nominative** (-u). It is the first word of an idafah, and the case comes from the sentence.
- صَدِيقِ, “the friend of”: **genitive**. It is the second word of the first idafah, and the first word of the second.
- ٱلطَّالِبِ, “the student”: **genitive**. It is the second word of the second idafah.

With فِى in front, كِتَابِ becomes genitive as well: فِى كِتَابِ صَدِيقِ ٱلطَّالِبِ. Now all three are genitive. كِتَابِ because of فِى, صَدِيقِ and ٱلطَّالِبِ because they are second words.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Say the case of ٱلرَّجُلَيْنِ in each: (a) رَأَيْتُ ٱلرَّجُلَيْنِ (b) ذَهَبْتُ إِلَى ٱلرَّجُلَيْنِ (c) كِتَابُ ٱلرَّجُلَيْنِ. These are made-up examples.">

- (a) **Accusative**: it is what I saw, the object.
- (b) **Genitive**: it follows the preposition إِلَى.
- (c) **Genitive**: it is the second word of an idafah.

The spelling is the same in all three. Only the sentence tells you.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Sort these genitive words by their cause (preposition, idafah, or a word that follows): (a) ٱلصَّبْرِ (b) ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ (c) ٱلْأَلْبَٰبِ (d) ٱلرَّجِيمِ (e) ٱلْأُنثَيَيْنِ. They come from 2:153, 3:190, 16:98 and 4:11.">

- (a) ٱلصَّبْرِ: **preposition** (بِ, 2:153).
- (b) ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ: **a word that follows**, joined with “and” to ٱلصَّبْرِ (2:153).
- (c) ٱلْأَلْبَٰبِ: **idafah**, the second word after أُو۟لِى (3:190).
- (d) ٱلرَّجِيمِ: **a word that follows**, an adjective of ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ (16:98).
- (e) ٱلْأُنثَيَيْنِ: **idafah**, the second word after حَظِّ (4:11).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="One of these phrases is wrong. Find it and fix it. (a) مِنَ ٱلْمَسْجِدِ ٱلْقَرِيبِ (b) فِى ٱلْبَيْتُ (c) كِتَابُ ٱلْمُعَلِّمِ. These are made-up examples.">

(b) is wrong. فِى is a preposition, so the noun after it must be genitive: فِى ٱلْبَيْتِ, “in the house”.

(a) is right: ٱلْمَسْجِدِ and its adjective ٱلْقَرِيبِ are both genitive after مِنَ, “from the near mosque”.

(c) is right: ٱلْمُعَلِّمِ is the second word of an idafah, so it is genitive. كِتَابُ can be nominative here, for example as the mubtadaʾ of a sentence.

</Exercise>

  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="a word that pulls plus a noun equals a genitive noun"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'a word that pulls', small: 'a little word, or the first word of an idafah' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'a noun', small: 'the word it pulls' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'genitive', small: 'جَرّ, usually -i' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- The genitive is the third case. Its usual ending is **-i**.
- A noun is genitive for one of three causes: after a **preposition**, as the **second word of an idafah**, or because it **follows** a genitive word (an adjective, or a word joined with “and”).
- A preposition has no case. The first word of an idafah takes the case its own place needs.
- The marks: **-i** and **-in**, a **yāʾ** for the dual and the sound masculine plural, a **hidden kasra** on a word ending in a long ā, and **-a** on a diptote. A pronoun counts as genitive without showing it.
- The dual and the sound masculine plural look the same in the accusative and the genitive. Read the sentence.
