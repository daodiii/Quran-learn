In 22:45 the mubtadaʾ is هِىَ, “it”. It stands for a town, قَرْيَة, which is feminine in Arabic. So the khabar is feminine too. It ends in -atun, ـَةٌ, the usual mark of a feminine word.

Some feminine words do not end in ـَة. ٱلشَّمْسُ, “the sun”, is feminine, so its khabar is مُنِيرَةٌ. ٱلْقَمَرُ, “the moon”, is masculine, so its khabar is مُنِيرٌ. You learn which words are feminine as you meet them.

<Note kind="tip">A khabar that ends in ـَةٌ tells you the mubtadaʾ is feminine, even when the mubtadaʾ itself does not end in ـَة.</Note>

<Block id="dual" title="Two of something: the dual" level={2}>
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Hajj 22:19 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'هَٰذَانِ', en: 'these two', say: 'hadhani' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'خَصْمَانِ', en: 'two opponents', say: 'khasmani' }] },
      ]}
      en={['These two ', { gap: 'are' }, ' two opposing sides.']}
    />
  </Stage>

Arabic has a special form for exactly two: the **dual**, مُثَنَّى (*muthanná*). In the nominative it ends in **-āni**: ـَانِ, an alif and a nūn.

هَٰذَانِ means “these two”. خَصْمَانِ means “two opponents”. Both are dual, and both end in -āni. So the khabar matches the mubtadaʾ in number as well as in gender.

<Note kind="know">The alif of the dual takes the place of the -u. It is the nominative mark of a dual word. L2.04 shows every mark of the nominative.</Note>

</Block>

<Block id="sound-masc" title="Many men: the sound plural in -ūna" level={2}>
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Imran 3:102 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'وَأَنتُم', en: 'and you (all)', say: 'wa-antum' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'مُّسْلِمُونَ', en: 'Muslims', say: 'muslimuna' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And you ', { gap: 'are' }, ' Muslims.']}
    />
  </Stage>

A **sound plural**, جَمْع سَالِم, is a plural made by adding an ending. The word stays whole inside. For men, the nominative ending is **-ūna**: ـُونَ. So مُسْلِم, “a Muslim”, becomes مُسْلِمُونَ.

Here the mubtadaʾ is أَنتُم, “you all”, a plural pronoun for men. The khabar مُسْلِمُونَ is a masculine plural too.

In L2.01 you saw the same ending in هُمْ فِيهَا خَٰلِدُونَ.

<Note kind="know">This sentence describes a state: “while you are Muslims”. A nominal sentence can do that. L4.01 teaches it.</Note>

For women, the sound plural ends in **-ātun**: ـَاتٌ. ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتُ is “the good women”. Its two khabars are both feminine plurals: قَٰنِتَٰتٌ and حَٰفِظَٰتٌ. This is also a mubtadaʾ with two khabars, as in L2.01.

<Note kind="warn">Do not mix up -atun and -ātun. The singular feminine ends in a short -atun (ظَالِمَةٌ). The plural has a long ā before the tāʾ (قَٰنِتَٰتٌ).</Note>

Here the mubtadaʾ is two words joined by وَ, “and”. Both end in -u. The khabar is a whole sentence. You will see how that works in the last steps.

For now, look at the little word -هُمْ in بَعْضُهُمْ. It is masculine plural, “them”, although the group includes women. When men and women are together, Arabic uses the masculine plural.

Not every plural is made by adding an ending. A **broken plural**, جَمْع تَكْسِير, changes the inside of the word. شَدِيد, “firm”, becomes أَشِدَّاء. رَحِيم, “kind”, becomes رُحَمَاء.

The mubtadaʾ ٱلَّذِينَ means “those who”, so it is plural. Its two khabars are plural in meaning too, although they do not end in -ūna. For people, the khabar is a plural: a sound plural or a broken plural.

<Note kind="know">أَشِدَّآءُ and رُحَمَآءُ end in -u, not -un. A small group of words never takes the extra n sound. You do not need that rule yet.</Note>

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal gives a second reading: ٱلَّذِينَ is joined to مُّحَمَّدٌ, and أَشِدَّآءُ is the khabar of both. *Al-Durr al-Maṣūn* also lists this possibility. This lesson follows al-Jadwal's main reading.</Note>

Look back at 22:45: هِىَ ظَالِمَةٌ, “it was unjust”, about one town. In 11:102 the words are almost the same, but هِىَ now stands for many towns, ٱلْقُرَىٰ, a plural of قَرْيَة. The khabar is still ظَٰلِمَةٌ, a feminine **singular**.

That is the usual rule. When the mubtadaʾ is a plural of things, not people, the khabar is a feminine singular. In 79:9, أَبْصَٰر (“eyes”) is a broken plural of a thing. The khabar خَٰشِعَةٌ is feminine singular.

<Note kind="remember">A plural of people takes a plural khabar. A plural of things usually takes a feminine singular khabar. L2.09 teaches the same rule for adjectives.</Note>

<Block id="not-matching" title="A plain noun does not have to match">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Al-Anʿam 6:128 (part)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلنَّارُ', en: 'the Fire', say: 'an-naru' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'مَثْوَىٰكُمْ', en: 'your home', say: 'mathwakum' }] },
      ]}
      en={['The Fire ', { gap: 'is' }, ' your home.']}
    />
  </Stage>

ٱلنَّارُ is feminine in Arabic. But the khabar مَثْوَىٰ, “home, resting place”, is a masculine noun. It does not change.

The difference is the kind of khabar. ظَالِمَةٌ and خَٰشِعَةٌ are **describing words**. They say what the mubtadaʾ is like, so they match it. مَثْوَىٰ is a plain noun. It names a place, so it keeps its own gender.

مَثْوَىٰ ends in a long ā, so its -u is hidden, as you saw in L2.01.

<Note kind="know">A few describing words never take ـَة. One is رَمِيم, “crumbled”, in وَهِىَ رَمِيمٌ (Ya-Sin 36:78, end). Al-Jadwal says that ـَة is left off either because the word has the sense “made to crumble”, or because it behaves more like a noun.</Note>

<Step id="definite" label="Definite or not" title="Definite and indefinite mubtadaʾ">
<Block id="definite-kinds" title="A mubtadaʾ is usually definite">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence

A **definite** word, مَعْرِفَة, points to something both people already know. An **indefinite** word, نَكِرَة, does not: “a book”. News about something unknown tells the listener little. So the mubtadaʾ is usually definite.

| Kind of definite word | Arabic | In this lesson |
|---|---|---|
| A name | عَلَم | مُّحَمَّدٌ (48:29) |
| A word with *al-* | مُعَرَّف بِأَلْ | ٱلنَّارُ (6:128) |
| A pronoun | ضَمِير | أَنتُم (3:102) |
| A pointing word: “this” | اِسْم إِشَارَة | هَٰذَانِ (22:19) |
| A “who” word | اِسْم مَوْصُول | وَٱلَّذِينَ (48:29) |
| The first word of an idafah with a definite second word | مُضَاف | أَبْصَٰرُهَا (79:9) |

<Note kind="know">A “who” word like ٱلَّذِينَ needs a few more words to finish its meaning. In the previous step it was مَعَهُۥٓ, “with him”. The whole group is the mubtadaʾ.</Note>

Most khabars so far were indefinite: عَلِيمٌ, “all-knowing”. Here both parts of each sentence are definite.

A definite khabar says exactly **who or what** the mubtadaʾ is: Allah is the Rich One. An indefinite khabar says **what kind** it is: وَٱللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ, “Allah is all-knowing”.

L2.01 said that two definite words often make a phrase, like ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلْجَدِيدُ. That was a first guide, not a law. When the first word is a name, a pronoun or a pointing word, two definite words can be a sentence. Here the verse sets two sentences side by side, and the meaning makes it clear.

Both mubtadaʾs here are indefinite: عَيْنَانِ and كُتُبٌ. L2.01 showed the first reason an indefinite mubtadaʾ is allowed (2:10): the khabar is a phrase or a place word, and it comes first.

In 55:50 the khabar is فِيهِمَا. The mubtadaʾ عَيْنَانِ comes later, and it is dual (-āni). The next word, تَجْرِيَانِ, is a dual verb that describes the springs.

In 98:3 the mubtadaʾ كُتُبٌ is a broken plural of things, so the adjective قَيِّمَةٌ is feminine singular, as you saw in the last step.

The khabar is خَيْرٌ, “better”. It has one form for both genders, so it is the same in both sentences. The adjective, however, matches its noun: مُؤْمِنَةٌ with أَمَةٌ, مُؤْمِنٌ with عَبْدٌ.

The little لَ at the start (the “beginning lām”) only adds emphasis. Al-Jadwal says this about both verses.

The little word أَ at the front asks a question. After a question, an indefinite word can start the sentence. Here the mubtadaʾ إِلَٰهٌ, “a god”, comes first, and the khabar follows: مَعَ ٱللَّهِ, “with Allah”. مَعَ is a place word, as in L2.01.

Compare this with the question in Ibrahim 14:10, أَفِى ٱللَّهِ شَكٌّ, “Is there any doubt about Allah?” There the khabar فِى ٱللَّهِ comes first and the mubtadaʾ شَكٌّ comes later. Al-Jadwal reads it this way.

<Step id="long-khabar" label="Long khabars" title="A khabar that is a phrase or a sentence">

L2.01 showed a phrase as khabar (7:128). Here are three more points.

First, a phrase has no ending of its own, because it is not one word. Al-Jadwal says it is tied to a word that is left out, here something like “belongs”: “to Allah (belongs) …”.

Second, the khabar is first. It does not have to be: the mubtadaʾ here is definite. The verse puts it first to stress who owns everything.

Third, look at the mubtadaʾ. It is مَا, “what”, a short word that never changes. It shows no -u. It is a “what” word (*ism mawṣūl*), and like هُوَ in L2.01 it counts as nominative “in its place”. The phrase after it (فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ) finishes its meaning.

<Note kind="tip">The tip from L2.01, “look for the -u”, works for nouns. When the mubtadaʾ is a short word like مَا or ٱلَّذِينَ, look for the word that means “what” or “who”.</Note>

Phrases can follow each other. In لَهُمْ فِى ٱلدُّنْيَا خِزْىٌ only the first phrase, لَهُمْ, is the khabar. The second phrase, فِى ٱلدُّنْيَا, adds more about the shame. Al-Jadwal does not call it a second khabar.

This is the same mistake as the one in L2.01 (“stopping at the phrase”), but turned around: do not call every phrase the khabar.

Both mubtadaʾs are indefinite (خِزْىٌ, عَذَابٌ), so the first phrase has to come first. The second sentence also has an adjective: عَظِيمٌ, “great”, which copies the ending of عَذَابٌ.

L2.01 showed khabars that are verbal sentences (2:15). Now look inside the verb.

يَعْلَمُ means “he knows”. The doer, “he”, is hidden inside the verb, and it is Allah. تَعْلَمُونَ means “you all know”. The letter و is the doer, “you all”, and it is the same group as أَنتُمْ.

That word inside the verb points back to the mubtadaʾ. It is the **link word**, رَابِط (*rābiṭ*). A khabar that is a whole sentence needs one, so the listener knows the sentence is about the mubtadaʾ.

There is no pronoun that points back. Instead the same noun is said again. Saying it again ties the question to the word it is about.

The khabar must match the mubtadaʾ. These are the common slips (all made-up examples):

- Wrong: ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ صَادِقٌ. A plural mubtadaʾ with a singular khabar. Right: ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ صَادِقُونَ.
- Wrong: ٱلْمُؤْمِنَاتُ صَادِقُونَ. Women need the ending for women. Right: ٱلْمُؤْمِنَاتُ صَادِقَاتٌ.
- Wrong: ٱلْأَبْصَارُ خَاشِعُونَ. The ending -ūna is for people. Right: ٱلْأَبْصَارُ خَاشِعَةٌ.

<Check n={22} q="Which one is right (made-up example)?" options={['ٱلْأَبْصَارُ خَاشِعُونَ', 'ٱلْأَبْصَارُ خَاشِعَةٌ']} answer={1} why="Eyes are things, so the khabar is a feminine singular." />
</Block>

<Block id="m-kinds" title="Mistake 2: thinking the khabar is one word">
  <Stage label="Five kinds of khabar, from this lesson">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Single:</b> ٱلْغَنِىُّ</li>
      <li><b>A phrase:</b> وَلِلَّهِ</li>
      <li><b>A place word:</b> مَعَ ٱللَّهِ</li>
      <li><b>A verbal sentence:</b> يَعْلَمُ</li>
      <li><b>A nominal sentence:</b> بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءُ بَعْضٍ</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

A habit from English is to look for one word that says what the mubtadaʾ “is”. In Arabic the khabar may be a phrase, a place word with its noun, or a whole sentence with its own link word.

Ask: “What is the news about the mubtadaʾ?” Then read to the end of the news before you name its kind.

<Check n={23} q="In أَءِلَٰهٌ مَّعَ ٱللَّهِ, what kind of khabar is مَعَ ٱللَّهِ?" options={['A single word', 'A place word with its noun', 'A verbal sentence']} answer={1} why="مَعَ is a place word, and ٱللَّهِ completes it." />
</Block>

  <Stage>

ٱلرَّجُلُ غَنِيٌّ has an indefinite khabar. It says what the man is like: “The man is rich.” It does not say that he is the rich one.

Compare وَٱللَّهُ ٱلْغَنِىُّ (47:38). The definite khabar says who the Rich One is. Do not translate the two the same way, and do not put *al-* on every khabar.

It is easy to read قُلُوبٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاجِفَةٌ as “Hearts on that day are trembling”, with وَاجِفَةٌ as the khabar.

Ask of any word after the mubtadaʾ: does it describe the mubtadaʾ, or is it news about it? If the sentence still feels unfinished, the news has not come yet.

<Exercise n={9} q="What kind of khabar does each one have? - (a) **A phrase**: وَلِلَّهِ, put first.
- (b) **A verbal sentence**: يَعْلَمُ, with a hidden “he”.
- (c) **A nominal sentence**: بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءُ بَعْضٍ.
- (d) **A place word**: مَعَ ٱللَّهِ.
- (e) **Single**: إِخْوَةٌ, a plural noun. إِنَّمَا means “only” and does not change the endings here (al-Jadwal calls it *kāffa*, “holding back”). L2.10 teaches *inna*.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={10} q="Find the link word that ties the khabar back to the mubtadaʾ. (a) فَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ قُطِّعَتْ لَهُمْ ثِيَابٌ مِّن نَّارٍ (Al-Hajj 22:19, part) (b) وَٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتُ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءُ بَعْضٍ (At-Tawbah 9:71, start) (c) قُلُوبٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاجِفَةٌ (An-Naziat 79:8), then أَبْصَٰرُهَا خَٰشِعَةٌ (An-Naziat 79:9)">

- (a) The -هُمْ in لَهُمْ points back to ٱلَّذِينَ.
- (b) The -هُمْ in بَعْضُهُمْ points back to the believing men and women.
- (c) The -هَا in أَبْصَٰرُهَا points back to قُلُوبٌ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={11} q="Give the role and case of every word: فِيهَا كُتُبٌ قَيِّمَةٌ (Al-Bayyinah 98:3). Meaning: “In them are right and true books.”">

- فِيهَا, “in them”: the **khabar, put first**. It is a phrase, so it has no ending of its own. It is tied to a word that is left out.
- كُتُبٌ, “books”: the **mubtadaʾ, put later**. It is indefinite and nominative (-un), a broken plural of things. The khabar comes first because the mubtadaʾ is indefinite.
- قَيِّمَةٌ: an **adjective** of كُتُبٌ, nominative (-un). It is feminine singular, because كُتُبٌ is a plural of things.

Al-Jadwal confirms the first two roles.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={12} q="Make two made-up sentences with a phrase khabar. (a) “Knowledge is in the heart.” ٱلْعِلْمُ and فِى ٱلْقَلْبِ (b) “A light is in the heart.” نُورٌ and فِى ٱلْقَلْبِ">

- (a) ٱلْعِلْمُ فِى ٱلْقَلْبِ. The mubtadaʾ is definite (-u). The khabar is a phrase, and ٱلْقَلْبِ after فِى is genitive (-i).
- (b) فِى ٱلْقَلْبِ نُورٌ. The mubtadaʾ نُورٌ is indefinite (-un), so the khabar comes first.

</Exercise>

        { kind: 'k', text: 'khabar', small: 'matches it' },

      ]}

- The khabar matches the mubtadaʾ: feminine -atun, two -āni, men -ūna, women -ātun.
- A plural of people takes a plural khabar. A plural of things takes a feminine singular. A plain noun keeps its own gender.
- A mubtadaʾ is usually definite: a name, a word with *al-*, a pronoun, a pointing word, a “who” word, or the first word of an idafah.
- An indefinite mubtadaʾ needs a reason: the khabar comes first, an adjective describes it, a question comes before it, or it is a wish or prayer.
- A definite khabar says who or what. An indefinite khabar says what kind.
- A phrase khabar belongs to a word that is left out. A khabar that is a sentence needs a link word: a pronoun, or the same noun said again.
