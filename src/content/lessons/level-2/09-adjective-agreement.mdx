It is the end of a verse about guidance. Read it from right to left.

**Two words describe one thing.** صِرَٰطٍ is “a path”. مُّسْتَقِيمٍ is “straight”. The second word tells you what kind of path it is.

English puts the describing word first: “a **straight** path”. Arabic puts it second: “a path **straight**”.

The adjective usually comes straight after its noun. Now and then a short phrase or a time word stands between them; you will see one case in the next step.

<Note kind="remember">Arabic says the noun first and the adjective after it. Read “a path, straight”.</Note>

- **Naʿt**, نَعْت: “a description”. This is the adjective.
- **Manʿūt**, مَنْعُوت: “the one described”. This is the noun.

Together they are one phrase. They are not a sentence. Nothing is said about the path yet; it is only described.

<Note kind="know">Some books call the adjective *ṣifah*, صِفَة. This course says naʿt, as *al-Ajurrumiyyah* and al-Jadwal do. *Al-Ajurrumiyyah* calls this kind of word a *tābiʿ*, تَابِع, “a follower”, because it follows its noun.</Note>
</Block>

    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* has a short chapter on the naʿt. It says:

- “The naʿt follows the manʿūt in its nominative, its accusative and its genitive, and in being definite and in being indefinite.”
- Then it gives three sentences, each with the name Zayd and the adjective ٱلْعَاقِلُ (“the wise”): “Wise Zayd stood up”, “I saw wise Zayd”, “I passed by wise Zayd”.

Look at the endings of ٱلْعَاقِلُ in the three sentences. They are -u, -a and -i. The adjective copies the case of Zayd each time.

The book names two matches: **case** and **definiteness**. Gender and number are the other two. You can see them in the shape of the words, and the next step covers them.
</Block>

      en={['… from one self.']}

The phrase مِن نَّفْسٍ وَٰحِدَةٍ means “from one single self”. The adjective وَٰحِدَةٍ matches the noun نَّفْسٍ in four ways:

| What matches | نَّفْسٍ | وَٰحِدَةٍ |
|---|---|---|
| Case | genitive, -in (after مِن) | genitive, -in |
| Definiteness | indefinite (-in, no *al-*) | indefinite (-in, no *al-*) |
| Gender | feminine | feminine (ends in ـَة) |
| Number | singular | singular |

Al-Jadwal says وَٰحِدَةٍ is the naʿt of نَفْس, “genitive like it”.

This is a nominal sentence (L2.01). The khabar لِلَّهِ comes first. The mubtadaʾ is ٱلْأَسْمَآءُ, with -u.

The adjective ٱلْحُسْنَىٰ must be nominative too. You cannot see its -u, because the word ends in a long ā (ىٰ), which cannot carry a vowel mark. You met this in L2.01. Al-Jadwal says it is nominative with the ḍamma “understood” on the last letter.

      ]}
      en={['Enter the holy land.']}

ٱلْأَرْضَ is the object of the verb (L2.05), so it is accusative, with -a. The adjective copies it: ٱلْمُقَدَّسَةَ also ends in -a.

Al-Jadwal: ٱلْمُقَدَّسَةَ is “the naʿt of ٱلْأَرْض, accusative like it”.

Look at the noun. ٱلْأَرْض has no ة at the end, but it is feminine in Arabic, so its adjective ends in ـَةَ. Gender is in the meaning of the noun, not only in its spelling.

<Check n={4} q="Why does ٱلْمُقَدَّسَةَ end in -a?" options={['It copies ٱلْأَرْضَ, the object', 'Every adjective ends in -a', 'It is a place word']} answer={0} why="The adjective takes the case of its noun. The noun is the object, so both are accusative." />
</Block>

<Block id="genitive" title="Case 3: genitive">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence

        { words: [{ ar: 'وَٱلْيَوْمِ', en: 'and the day', say: 'wal-yawmi' }], role: 'manut', hint: 'joined to “Allah”, after بِ' },

The full line is “he does not believe in Allah and the Last Day”. The preposition بِ (L2.07) makes ٱللَّهِ genitive, and وَ joins ٱلْيَوْمِ to it, so ٱلْيَوْمِ is genitive too.

The adjective ٱلْءَاخِرِ copies it with -i. Al-Jadwal: “naʿt of ٱلْيَوْم, genitive like it”.

<Note kind="tip">To find the case of an adjective, find the case of its noun. The adjective always has the same one.</Note>

</Block>

<Block id="definite" title="Definiteness: what counts as definite">
  <Stage label="From al-Ajurrumiyyah">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote lx-quote-list" lang="ar" dir="rtl">
      <p>الْمَعْرِفَةُ خَمْسَةُ أَشْيَاءَ:</p>
      <p>الِاسْمُ الْمُضْمَرُ نَحْوُ أَنَا وَأَنْتَ</p>
      <p>وَالِاسْمُ الْعَلَمُ نَحْوُ زَيْدٌ وَمَكَّةَ</p>
      <p>وَالِاسْمُ الْمُبْهَمُ نَحْوُ هَذَا وَهَذِهِ وَهَؤُلَاءِ</p>
      <p>وَالِاسْمُ الَّذِي فِيهِ الْأَلِفُ وَاللَّامُ نَحْوُ الرَّجُلُ وَالْغُلَامُ</p>
      <p>وَمَا أُضِيفَ إِلَى وَاحِدٍ مِنْ هَذِهِ الْأَرْبَعَةِ</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>
  </Stage>

The book lists **five kinds of definite noun**, مَعْرِفَة (*maʿrifa*):

| Kind | In the book | In the Quran |
|---|---|---|
| A pronoun | أَنَا، أَنْتَ | هُوَ |
| A name | زَيْدٌ، مَكَّةَ | ٱللَّهُ |
| A pointing word | هَٰذَا، هَٰذِهِ، هَٰؤُلَاءِ | ذَٰلِكَ، تِلْكَ |
| A noun with *al-* | ٱلرَّجُلُ، ٱلْغُلَامُ | ٱلْكِتَٰبِ |
| A noun joined to one of these four (an idafah, L2.08) | any such noun | رَبُّ ٱلْعَرْشِ، رَسُولِهِ |

Everything else is **indefinite**, نَكِرَة (*nakira*). The book gives a handy test: if you can put *al-* in front of the noun, it is indefinite.

So a noun can be definite **without** *al-*. رَبُّ in رَبُّ ٱلْعَرْشِ has no *al-*, but it is definite because it is joined to ٱلْعَرْشِ. A word like رَسُولِهِ is definite because of the pronoun on its end.
</Block>

    />
    <Sentence

In the first pair both words are indefinite: no *al-*, and tanwīn (-an) on each. In the second pair both are definite: *al-* on each.

The adjective copies the definiteness of its noun. It does not mix:

- an indefinite noun with an indefinite adjective: كَلِمَةً طَيِّبَةً, “a good word”;
- a definite noun with a definite adjective: ٱلْأَرْضَ ٱلْمُقَدَّسَةَ, “the holy land”.

Al-Fatiha shows the definite form: ٱلصِّرَٰطَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ (1:6, part), “the straight path”. The phrase in 2:142 that you saw first is the indefinite form: صِرَٰطٍ مُّسْتَقِيمٍ.

<Note kind="warn">If the noun has *al-*, the adjective needs *al-* too. If the noun has tanwīn, the adjective has tanwīn too.</Note>

You know from L1.07 that a feminine word usually ends in ة (the tāʾ marbūṭa). The adjective of a feminine noun must be feminine too:

- كَلِمَةً (feminine, ة) + طَيِّبَةً (feminine, ة);
- ٱلْأَرْضَ (feminine, no ة) + ٱلْمُقَدَّسَةَ (feminine, ة).

The second pair is the one to watch. The noun ٱلْأَرْض is feminine although it has no ة. You learn this by meeting the word. The adjective then shows it.

A masculine noun takes a masculine adjective, with no ـَة: صِرَٰطٍ مُّسْتَقِيمٍ.

The adjective also matches the number of its noun.

| Noun | Adjective | Nominative ending |
|---|---|---|
| one (singular) | one | -u / -un |
| two (dual) | two | -āni |
| many people, masculine | masculine plural | -ūna |

- **Dual.** جَنَّتَانِ is “two gardens”, with the dual ending -āni. Its adjective مُدْهَآمَّتَانِ is dual and feminine, also with -āni. They are in two different verses. Al-Jadwal says that what comes between them is a side remark, and that مُدْهَآمَّتَانِ is the naʿt of جَنَّتَانِ, nominative.
- **Plural of people.** ٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةُ, “the angels”, is plural. Its adjective ٱلْمُقَرَّبُونَ is a masculine plural with -ūna. Al-Jadwal: nominative, “with the letter و as its mark”.

The case endings for dual and plural nouns are in L2.04 to L2.06. The adjective uses the very same endings as its noun.

<Note kind="tip">The adjective ends like the noun. If the noun ends in -āni or -ūna, so does the adjective.</Note>

A plural of **things** is not like a plural of people. أَيَّامًا (“days”) is plural, but its adjective is **feminine**, not plural masculine.

The Quran shows two forms:

- 2:80 uses a feminine **singular** adjective: مَّعْدُودَةً.
- 2:184 uses a feminine **plural** adjective: مَّعْدُودَٰتٍ.

Al-Jadwal reads both as the naʿt of أَيَّامًا, accusative like it. In 2:184 the mark is a kasra, because that is how a feminine plural shows the accusative (L2.05).

The same rule is behind 7:180. ٱلْأَسْمَآءُ (“the names”) is a plural of things, and its adjective ٱلْحُسْنَىٰ is a feminine singular.

<Note kind="know">The pattern here is read from these three verses. L1.08 introduced plurals; this lesson only shows how an adjective treats them. Remember it as: a group of things behaves like one feminine thing.</Note>

Sometimes two nouns come before the adjective. Which one does it describe? The adjective has to match its noun in every way, so only one noun can fit.

**12:1.** The candidates are ءَايَٰتُ and ٱلْكِتَٰبِ.

| Test | ءَايَٰتُ | ٱلْكِتَٰبِ | ٱلْمُبِينِ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Case | nominative | genitive | genitive |
| Gender | feminine | masculine | masculine |
| Number | plural | singular | singular |

ٱلْمُبِينِ matches ٱلْكِتَٰبِ in all three. Al-Jadwal: “naʿt of ٱلْكِتَاب, genitive”. The verses are not “clear”; the Book is.

**9:129.** The candidates are رَبُّ (nominative) and ٱلْعَرْشِ (genitive). ٱلْعَظِيمِ is genitive, so it describes ٱلْعَرْشِ: the Throne is great. Al-Jadwal says exactly this.

One noun can have many adjectives. They stand in a row after it, and each one matches the noun on its own.

Here the noun is رَسُولٌ, “a messenger”. It is indefinite, masculine, singular and nominative. Every adjective is the same: indefinite, masculine, singular, with -un. Al-Jadwal calls عَزِيزٌ the naʿt of رَسُول, and then حَرِيصٌ “another naʿt”. For رَءُوفٌ and رَحِيمٌ it says: “and likewise”.

The phrase مِّنْ أَنفُسِكُمْ, “from among yourselves”, also describes the messenger. A phrase can be a describing word too.

In the same way, Bismillah has two adjectives of ٱللَّهِ: ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ and ٱلرَّحِيمِ, both genitive (see exercise 4).

<Note kind="warn">The words after رَسُولٌ are all adjectives of it. Do not look for a khabar here. The sentence is a verbal sentence: the verb جَآءَ, and رَسُولٌ is its doer.</Note>

<Block id="khabar" title="An adjective or a khabar?">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles

        { role: 'khabar2', words: [{ ar: 'حَكِيمٌ', en: 'all-wise', say: 'hakimun' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And Allah ', { gap: 'is' }, ' All-Knowing, All-Wise.']}
    />
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="An-Nisa 4:13 (end)"
      groups={[
        { role: 'mubtada', words: [{ ar: 'وَذَٰلِكَ', en: 'and that', say: 'wa-dhalika' }] },
        { role: 'khabar', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْفَوْزُ', en: 'the success', say: 'al-fawzu' }] },
        { role: 'naat', words: [{ ar: 'ٱلْعَظِيمُ', en: 'the great', say: 'al-azimu' }] },
      ]}
      en={['And that ', { gap: 'is' }, ' the great success.']}
    />
  </Stage>

L2.01 showed that definiteness is a clue. Now you have the whole rule. Ask three questions about the word after the noun:

1. **Does it match the noun in definiteness?** If the noun is definite and the next word is indefinite, it is a khabar. In 4:26 ٱللَّهُ is definite (a name) and عَلِيمٌ is indefinite. So عَلِيمٌ and حَكِيمٌ are two khabars, not adjectives.
2. **Is the first word a pronoun or a pointing word?** A pronoun never takes an adjective, and the word after a pointing word is often its khabar (or a badal, as in the last block of this step). In 4:13 ذَٰلِكَ is the mubtadaʾ, and ٱلْفَوْزُ is its khabar. The word after, ٱلْعَظِيمُ, describes ٱلْفَوْزُ. Al-Jadwal reads it this way.
3. **Does the whole thing make a statement?** “Allah is all-knowing” says something. “A kind messenger” does not.

<Note kind="know">A khabar can be a noun with its adjective: here ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ, “the great success”, is what is said about ذَٰلِكَ. The same shape can be a mubtadaʾ, as in عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ in L2.01.</Note>

  <Stage label="Two definite nouns side by side">
    <Tiles
      label="ءَايَٰتُ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ: an idafah. ٱلْكِتَٰبِ ٱلْمُبِينِ: a noun and its adjective"

Two definite words in a row can be an **idafah** (“X of Y”, L2.08) or a **noun and its adjective**. Here are the differences:

| Test | Idafah | Noun + adjective |
|---|---|---|
| First word | no *al-*, no tanwīn | matches the next word: *al-* with *al-*, tanwīn with tanwīn |
| Second word | always genitive | copies the first word's case |
| Meaning | “of”: ءَايَٰتُ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ, “verses of the Book” | “who or which is”: ٱلْكِتَٰبِ ٱلْمُبِينِ, “the clear Book” |

In 12:1 you get both at once: ءَايَٰتُ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ is an idafah, and then ٱلْمُبِينِ describes the second noun.

  <Stage>

Three words in a row, all genitive, all definite. Which are adjectives? Al-Jadwal reads them like this:

  <Stage>

ٱلْأَسْمَآءُ is a plural. A learner may add a plural masculine adjective, with -ūna. The Quran uses a feminine singular adjective for a plural of things. Remember: **people** get a masculine plural, **things** get a feminine singular (or, as in 2:184, a feminine plural).

If the noun is feminine, its adjective must have ـَة. Do not look at the noun's ending to decide the gender. ٱلْأَرْض has no ة, but its adjective ٱلْمُقَدَّسَةَ does. Say ٱلْأَرْضَ ٱلْمُقَدَّسَةَ, never ٱلْأَرْضَ ٱلْمُقَدَّسَ.

Al-Jadwal also reads some words as a badal (7:158). Use the three questions in the third step before you write “naʿt”.

<Step id="practice" label="Practice" title="Practice">
<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: matching and telling apart">
  <Stage label="The four checks">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Case:</b> the same as the noun.</li>
      <li><b>Definiteness:</b> *al-* with *al-*, tanwīn with tanwīn.</li>
      <li><b>Gender:</b> ـَة with a feminine noun.</li>
      <li><b>Number:</b> one, two or many; a plural of things is feminine singular.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Work out each answer first. Then open it.

<Exercise n={1} q="Check all four matches in ٱلصِّرَٰطَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ (Al-Fatiha 1:6, part). Meaning: “the straight path”.">

- **Case:** both accusative, with -a. The noun is the second object of the verb ٱهْدِنَا (“guide us”), and al-Jadwal says ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ is “the naʿt of ٱلصِّرَاط, accusative like it”.
- **Definiteness:** both definite, with *al-*.
- **Gender:** both masculine, with no ة.
- **Number:** both singular.

All four match.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Sentence or phrase? (a) ٱلْبَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ (b) ٱلْبَيْتُ ٱلْكَبِيرُ (c) عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:10, part) (d) وَٱللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ (An-Nisa 4:26, end). (a) and (b) are made-up examples.">

- (a) **Sentence**: “The house is big.” A definite word, then an indefinite one. كَبِيرٌ is a khabar.
- (b) **Phrase**: “the big house”. Both have *al-*. ٱلْكَبِيرُ is a naʿt.
- (c) **Phrase**: “a painful punishment”. Both are indefinite, with -un. أَلِيمٌ is a naʿt of عَذَابٌ.
- (d) **Sentence**: ٱللَّهُ is definite and عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ are indefinite, so they are two khabars.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Give the correct form of كَبِير (big) for each noun: (a) ٱلْبَيْتُ (nominative) (b) رَأَيْتُ ٱلْبَيْتَ (accusative) (c) فِى ٱلْبَيْتِ (genitive). These are made-up examples.">

- (a) ٱلْبَيْتُ ٱلْكَبِيرُ: “the big house”. Both nominative, with -u.
- (b) رَأَيْتُ ٱلْبَيْتَ ٱلْكَبِيرَ: “I saw the big house”. Both accusative, with -a.
- (c) فِى ٱلْبَيْتِ ٱلْكَبِيرِ: “in the big house”. Both genitive, with -i.

In each one the adjective keeps *al-* (the noun has it), is masculine singular, and takes the noun's case. Only its last vowel changes.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Find the adjectives and say what they describe: بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ (Al-Fatiha 1:1). Meaning: “In the name of Allah, the Most Kind, the Most Merciful.”">

ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ is the first naʿt of ٱللَّهِ. ٱلرَّحِيمِ is the second naʿt of ٱللَّهِ. Al-Jadwal reads them this way.

Both match ٱللَّهِ in four ways: genitive (-i), definite (*al-*), masculine, singular. The genitive of ٱللَّهِ comes from the idafah بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ. The adjectives copy it.

They are not khabars. There is no sentence here with a mubtadaʾ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="What is the adjective, and what does it describe? كَلِمَةً طَيِّبَةً (Ibrahim 14:24, part). Meaning: “a good word”. Check all four matches.">

طَيِّبَةً is the naʿt, and كَلِمَةً is the manʿūt.

- **Case:** both accusative, with -an. Al-Jadwal says it is the naʿt of كَلِمَة, accusative like it.
- **Definiteness:** both indefinite, with tanwīn.
- **Gender:** both feminine, with ة.
- **Number:** both singular.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} q="Which noun does ٱلْعَظِيمِ describe, and how can you tell? وَهُوَ رَبُّ ٱلْعَرْشِ ٱلْعَظِيمِ (At-Tawbah 9:129, end). Meaning: “and he is the Lord of the great Throne.”">

It describes ٱلْعَرْشِ, “the Throne”. ٱلْعَظِيمِ is genitive. رَبُّ is nominative (it is the khabar), so it cannot be the noun described. ٱلْعَرْشِ is genitive (the second word of the idafah), so the adjective matches it.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Choose the right adjective: (a) ٱلْمُعَلِّمُونَ ___ (the good teachers; people, masculine plural) (b) ٱلْكُتُبُ ___ (the new books; things) (c) ٱلْمَدْرَسَتَانِ ___ (the two big schools; dual feminine). Use صَالِح (good), جَدِيد (new) and كَبِير (big). These are made-up examples.">

- (a) ٱلْمُعَلِّمُونَ ٱلصَّالِحُونَ: people take a masculine plural adjective, with -ūna. (The made-up pair uses صَالِح, “good”, in place of جَدِيد.)
- (b) ٱلْكُتُبُ ٱلْجَدِيدَةُ: things take a feminine **singular** adjective, with ـَةُ.
- (c) ٱلْمَدْرَسَتَانِ ٱلْكَبِيرَتَانِ: dual feminine, with -āni.

In all three, the adjective keeps *al-* and the case of its noun.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Find and correct the mistake in each made-up example: (a) ٱلْمَرْأَةُ ٱلْكَبِيرُ (b) فِى ٱلْبَيْتِ ٱلْكَبِيرُ (c) ٱلْكِتَابُ كَبِيرٌ meaning “the big book” (d) رَأَيْتُ ٱلْبَيْتَ كَبِيرًا meaning “I saw the big house”. All four are made-up examples.">

- (a) The noun is feminine, so the adjective needs ـَة: ٱلْمَرْأَةُ ٱلْكَبِيرَةُ.
- (b) The noun is genitive, so the adjective must be genitive: فِى ٱلْبَيْتِ ٱلْكَبِيرِ.
- (c) This is a sentence, “the book is big”. For “the big book” both words need *al-*: ٱلْكِتَابُ ٱلْكَبِيرُ.
- (d) The noun has *al-*, the adjective does not. Write ٱلْبَيْتَ ٱلْكَبِيرَ. (Without *al-*, كَبِيرًا would be read as a state, a ḥāl.)

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="the noun described plus its adjective equals one phrase"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'manʿūt', small: 'the noun described' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'naʿt', small: 'the adjective' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'noun and adjective', small: 'مَنْعُوت وَنَعْت' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- The adjective (**naʿt**) comes right after its noun (**manʿūt**).
- It matches the noun in **case**, **definiteness**, **gender** and **number**. *Al-Ajurrumiyyah* names the first two.
- A definite noun has a definite adjective. A noun can be definite without *al-*: it can be a name, a pronoun, a pointing word, or joined to one of these.
- A feminine noun takes an adjective with ـَة. A plural of things takes a feminine singular adjective (or a feminine plural).
- A noun can have several adjectives in a row, and a “which” clause can be one too.
- When two nouns come before an adjective, check which one fits. An idafah is not a noun and adjective.
- Not every following word is a naʿt. It can be a khabar or a badal.
