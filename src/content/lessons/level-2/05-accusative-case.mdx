Read it from right to left. There is a verb, a doer and a receiver.

The doer, ٱللَّهُ, ends in **-u**. You know this from L2.04. The receiver, قَوْلَ, ends in **-a**. That is the new ending.

      en={['And establish the prayer, and give the charity.']}
    />

This case is called **accusative**, نَصْب (*naṣb*). Its usual sign is a **fatḥa**, the short vowel **-a**.

The most common accusative word is the **object**, مَفْعُول بِهِ (*mafʿūl bihi*): the thing or person the action falls on. To find it, ask the verb “what?” or “whom?”. Establish what? The prayer. Give what? The charity.

English keeps a trace of this in pronouns: “I saw **him**”, not “I saw **he**”. Arabic does it with every noun.

In these two commands the doer is the wāw at the end of the verb, “you all”. It is part of the verb, so no separate doer word is written.

</Block>

In English the object comes after the verb. In Arabic it can come first, as here: “a group you called liars”.

You do not need to count positions. Look at the ending. فَرِيقًا ends in **-an**, so it is accusative: a receiver. The doers here are inside the verbs: -tum and -ūna both mean “you”.

Al-Jadwal reads فَرِيقًا as an object that was put first.

A small pronoun joined to the end of a verb is the object: خَلَقَ**كُمْ**, “created **you**”. Other ones are -ka (you), -hu (him), -hum (them) and -nī (me).

These pronouns never change their ending. The grammar books say a pronoun like this stands **in the place of** the accusative (*fī maḥall naṣb*), as هُوَ stood in the place of the nominative in L2.01.

Look at رَبَّكُمُ. It has the same -kum, but here it means “your”. It hangs on the noun رَبَّ, and it is not an object. The noun رَبَّ is the object, with a fatḥa.

</Block>

<Step id="marks" label="The marks" title="How -a looks on each kind of word">
<Block id="five-signs" title="The five signs, as the first grammar book says it">
  <Stage label="From al-Ajurrumiyyah">
    <blockquote class="lx-quote" lang="ar" dir="rtl">
      <p>وَلِلنَّصْبِ خَمْسُ عَلَامَاتٍ: الْفَتْحَةُ، وَالْأَلِفُ، وَالْكَسْرَةُ، وَالْيَاءُ، وَحَذْفُ النُّونِ</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p class="lx-quote-note">Vowel marks added by Quran Learn.</p>
  </Stage>

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah*, the short grammar book from L2.01, says: “The accusative has five signs: the fatḥa, the alif, the kasra, the yāʾ, and the dropping of the nūn.”

- The **fatḥa** is the main sign. It is used on singular words and on broken plurals (plurals that change the shape of the word, like رُسُل from رَسُول).
- The **alif**, **kasra** and **yāʾ** stand in for it on some words. The next blocks show each one.
- The **dropping of the nūn** belongs to verbs, not nouns. L3.04 teaches it.

<Check n={5} q="Which sign belongs to verbs only?" options={['The kasra', 'The yāʾ', 'The dropping of the nūn']} answer={2} why="The first four can end a noun. Dropping the nūn is for a group of verbs (Level 3)." />
</Block>

A word without *al-* takes tanwīn. In the accusative it is **-an**: two fatḥas, written ـً.

Most words also get an **alif** after it: عَجُولًا is *ʿajūlan*. The alif is only part of the spelling. You say -an.

A word that ends in tāʾ marbūṭa (ة) has no alif: رَحْمَةً is *raḥmatan*. Both words in the second line are accusative. Al-Jadwal reads them as joined to an accusative word before them in the verse.

<Check n={6} q="Why does رَحْمَةً have no alif after the tanwīn?" options={['It ends in tāʾ marbūṭa (ة)', 'It is not accusative']} answer={0} why="After ة the -an is written on the letter itself. It is still accusative." />
</Block>

The **dual** is the form for exactly two. Its accusative ending is **-ayni**: the yāʾ is the sign. دَآئِبَيْنِ means “two going on steadily”, the sun and the moon. Al-Jadwal says its mark is the yāʾ.

In L2.04 the nominative dual ended in -āni. Now the alif has become a yāʾ.

The two words before it, ٱلشَّمْسَ and ٱلْقَمَرَ, are objects with a plain **-a**. The last word is a **ḥāl**, a word that says how things were while the action happened. Level 4 teaches it (L4.01). Here you only need to see that it is accusative.

</Block>

      ]}

The **sound masculine plural** (for men: -ūna in the nominative) ends in **-īna** in the accusative. The yāʾ is the sign. Al-Jadwal says exactly this about both words.

رُسُلًا is a broken plural, so it takes the plain **-an**. The two describing words copy its case. The describing word is called a **naʿt**, نَعْت (L2.09).

<Note kind="remember">Nominative sound plural: -ūna. Accusative: -īna. A dual is the same: nominative -āni, accusative -ayni.</Note>
</Block>

ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ and ٱلرَّٰكِعِينَ look the same. One is accusative and one is **genitive**, the case with the **-i** ending (L2.06).

The ending cannot tell you which. The job does. ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ is what Allah loves, so it is the object. ٱلرَّٰكِعِينَ comes after مَعَ, as the second word of “with those who bow”, so it is genitive. Al-Jadwal reads both this way.

The dual works the same way: -ayni is both accusative and genitive.

  <Stage>

The verb ءَاتَيْنَا (“We gave”) takes two objects: the one who receives (عِيسَى) and the thing given (ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ).

ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ is a **sound feminine plural**, the plural that ends in -āt. In the accusative it ends in a **kasra** (-i), not a fatḥa. Al-Jadwal says: its mark is the kasra, because it is a sound feminine plural.

So this accusative word looks just like a genitive word. You tell them apart by the job, as in the last block.

<Note kind="warn">The same verse has a word that ends in -a and is not accusative: مَرْيَمَ is genitive. Some words cannot take -in, so they take -a instead. Al-Jadwal says this about مَرْيَمَ. L2.06 explains.</Note>

</Block>

  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles

مُوسَىٰ ends in a long **ā**, written ى. A long ā cannot carry a vowel, so the -a cannot be written.

The word is still accusative: Moses is who Allah spoke to. The grammar books say its fatḥa is **hidden**: *muqaddara*, “understood but not seen”. Al-Jadwal says exactly this. The same happens with عِيسَى in the last block.

This is the accusative twin of L2.01, where مَوْلَىٰ had a hidden -u.

The last word, تَكْلِيمًا, repeats the verb's meaning to make it strong: “spoke a real speaking”. It is called a **mafʿūl muṭlaq** (L4.07). It is accusative with -an and an alif.

</Block>

<Block id="five-nouns" title="A few family words: the alif">
  <Stage>
    <Sentence
      roles
      reference="Yusuf 12:16"

“Father” is أَبٌ. It is *abū-hum* when it is the starting point, *abī-him* after a little word like *li-*, and **أَبَاهُمْ** *abā-hum* as an object. The long **ā** is the accusative sign.

*Al-Ajurrumiyyah* gives this alif to a small group of five nouns, among them أَبٌ (father), أَخٌ (brother) and ذُو (owner of). They take it when they are joined to a following word, here “their”.

Al-Jadwal reads أَبَاهُمْ as an object, in the accusative. Its text for this word is short and does not name the alif, so the alif here comes from *al-Ajurrumiyyah*.

      <li><b>Sound feminine plural:</b> a kasra (ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ, 2:87)</li>
      <li><b>Five nouns:</b> an alif (أَبَاهُمْ, 12:16)</li>
      <li><b>Ends in ى or ا:</b> a hidden fatḥa (مُوسَىٰ, 4:164)</li>
      <li><b>Attached pronoun:</b> no mark, “in the place of” the accusative (خَلَقَكُمْ, 2:21)</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Compare with L2.04. The nominative used **-u** for singular, **-āni** for the dual, **-ūna** for the sound masculine plural, and a ḍamma for the broken plural. The accusative changes the vowel to **-a** and turns the long letters into yāʾ: **-ayni**, **-īna**.

<Check n={12} q="Which noun shows its accusative case with a kasra?" options={['A dual', 'A sound masculine plural', 'A sound feminine plural']} answer={2} why="Only the sound feminine plural (-āt) uses a kasra in the accusative." />
</Block>
</Step>

  <Stage>

A word that says **when** is a **time word**, ظَرْف زَمَان (*ẓarf zamān*). It is accusative because it means “at that time”. *Al-Ajurrumiyyah* says a time word is accusative with an unwritten “in”, فِي, understood before it. لَيْلًا ends in -an.

It is not an object. Ask “took what?” and it does not fit. Ask “took when?” and it does: by night. Al-Jadwal reads لَيْلًا as a time word tied to the verb. You also saw عِشَآءً (“in the evening”) in 12:16.

A word that says **where** is a **place word**, ظَرْف مَكَان. حَوْلَ (around) ends in **-a**. Others are مَعَ (with, 2:43), فَوْقَ (above), تَحْتَ (under) and عِنْدَ (at).

Al-Jadwal calls حَوْلَهُۥ a place word, accusative, tied to the verb بَٰرَكْنَا. The pronoun -hu after it means “it” and is genitive: “around it”. The word after a place word is genitive, as you saw with مَعَ ٱلرَّٰكِعِينَ.

In L2.01 a place word could also be a whole khabar (عِندَهُۥ مَفَاتِحُ ٱلْغَيْبِ). It is accusative there too.

<Step id="practice" label="Practice" title="Practice">
<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: find the accusative words">
  <Stage label="For each word, say">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Role:</b> object, ism inna, khabar kāna, time or place word, or something else?</li>
      <li><b>Case:</b> nominative, accusative or genitive?</li>
      <li><b>Mark:</b> -a, -an, -ayni, -īna, a kasra, an alif, or hidden?</li>
      <li><b>Reason:</b> why does it have this case?</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Find the accusative noun, and say what the other nouns are. This is a made-up example: رَأَيْتُ ٱلْقَمَرَ فِى ٱلسَّمَاءِ. Meaning: “I saw the moon in the sky.”">

ٱلْقَمَرَ, “the moon”, is the **object** of رَأَيْتُ. It ends in **-a**. Ask “saw what?”: the moon.

The ending -tu on the verb is the doer, “I”. It is part of the verb.

ٱلسَّمَاءِ, “the sky”, comes after the little word فِى, so it is **genitive** (-i). It is not accusative.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Find every accusative word, and say what job and what mark each has: وَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَٱرْكَعُوا۟ مَعَ ٱلرَّٰكِعِينَ (Al-Baqarah 2:43). Meaning: “And establish the prayer, give the charity, and bow with those who bow.”">

- ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ and ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ: **objects**, accusative, with **-a**.
- مَعَ: a **place word**, accusative, with **-a**.
- ٱلرَّٰكِعِينَ ends in -īna, but it is **genitive**: it is the second word of “with those who bow”. Al-Jadwal says so.
- The three verbs are not nouns, so they have no case.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Add إِنَّ to this made-up sentence: ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ ظَلُومٌ, “Man is unfair.” Then say which words change. Compare with إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَظَلُومٌ كَفَّارٌ (Ibrahim 14:34, end).">

With inna: **إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ ظَلُومٌ**.

- ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ becomes ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ: the **ism inna**, now with **-a**.
- ظَلُومٌ stays **-un**: the khabar of inna is still nominative.

In the verse, لَظَلُومٌ has an extra لَ for emphasis, and a second khabar, كَفَّارٌ, also with -un.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Find the accusative words in وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا (An-Nisa 4:96, end), and say why. Then say what the sentence looks like without kāna. Meaning: “And Allah has always been Forgiving, Merciful.”">

- غَفُورًا is the **khabar of kāna**: accusative, with -an and an alif.
- رَحِيمًا is a **second khabar**: accusative, with -an and an alif. Al-Jadwal says so.
- ٱللَّهُ is the **ism of kāna** and keeps its **-u**.

Without kāna, both would be nominative: ٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ (a made-up sentence). The -an becomes -un.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Three nouns, three cases. Give the case, the job and the mark of each noun in this made-up example: جَاءَ رَسُولُ ٱللَّهِ يَوْمًا. Meaning: “The Messenger of Allah came one day.”">

- رَسُولُ: **nominative**. It is the doer of جَاءَ. Mark: **-u**.
- ٱللَّهِ: **genitive**. It is the second word of “Messenger of Allah”. Mark: **-i**.
- يَوْمًا: **accusative**. It is a **time word**, and it says when he came. Mark: **-an**.

يَوْمًا is a time word, not a ḥāl: it answers “when?”, not “how?”.

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Exercise n={6} q="Name the accusative mark on each word: (a) ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ (2:43) (b) دَآئِبَيْنِ (14:33) (c) مُبَشِّرِينَ (4:165) (d) ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ (2:87) (e) مُوسَىٰ (4:164) (f) أَبَاهُمْ (12:16)">

- (a) **-a** (fatḥa): a singular noun with *al-*.
- (b) **-ayni**: a dual.
- (c) **-īna**: a sound masculine plural.
- (d) **A kasra**: a sound feminine plural.
- (e) **A hidden fatḥa**: it ends in a long ā.
- (f) **An alif**: one of the five nouns.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="What job does each accusative word do? (a) قَوْلَ in سَمِعَ ٱللَّهُ قَوْلَ (3:181) (b) ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ in إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَظَلُومٌ (14:34) (c) عَجُولًا in وَكَانَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ عَجُولًا (17:11) (d) لَيْلًا in أَسْرَىٰ بِعَبْدِهِۦ لَيْلًا (17:1) (e) حَوْلَهُۥ in ٱلَّذِى بَٰرَكْنَا حَوْلَهُۥ (17:1)">

- (a) **Object** (mafʿūl bihi): what Allah heard.
- (b) **Ism inna**: the first part after inna.
- (c) **Khabar kāna**: the news part after kāna.
- (d) **Time word**: when the journey happened.
- (e) **Place word**: where the blessing is.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Which words in وَءَاتَيْنَا عِيسَى ٱبْنَ مَرْيَمَ ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ (Al-Baqarah 2:87, part) are accusative? Give the mark of each, and say which word looks accusative but is not.">

- عِيسَى: object. Mark: a **hidden fatḥa**, because it ends in a long ā.
- ٱبْنَ: accusative, with **-a**. It follows عِيسَى and copies its case. Al-Jadwal reads it as a naʿt or badal of عِيسَى.
- ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ: the second object. Mark: a **kasra**, because it is a sound feminine plural.
- **مَرْيَمَ** ends in -a but is **genitive**. It is the second word of “son of Mary”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Say the case and the mark of the object in each made-up sentence: (a) كَتَبَ ٱلطَّالِبُ ٱلْكِتَابَيْنِ, “The student wrote the two books.” (b) قَرَأَ ٱلطَّالِبُ ٱلْكَلِمَاتِ, “The student read the words.” (c) أَكْرَمَ ٱلْمُعَلِّمُ ٱلطَّالِبِينَ, “The teacher honoured the students.”">

In all three the object is accusative. The doer ends in **-u**.

- (a) ٱلْكِتَابَيْنِ: a dual. Mark: **-ayni**.
- (b) ٱلْكَلِمَاتِ: a sound feminine plural. Mark: a **kasra**.
- (c) ٱلطَّالِبِينَ: a sound masculine plural. Mark: **-īna**.

None of the three ends in -a. They are still objects.

</Exercise>

  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="the doer takes -u, the receiver takes -a"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'doer', small: 'nominative: -u' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'receiver', small: 'accusative: -a' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'two cases', small: 'رَفْع and نَصْب' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- The accusative, نَصْب, is the case of the **object** and of a few other jobs. Its main sign is **-a**.
- The marks: **-a** or **-an** (singular, broken plural), **-ayni** (dual), **-īna** (sound masculine plural), a **kasra** (sound feminine plural), an **alif** (five nouns), a **hidden fatḥa** (words ending in ى or ا), and no mark on an attached pronoun.
- The jobs for this lesson: object, ism inna (L2.10), khabar kāna (L2.11), time word, place word. Ḥāl, tamyīz, mafʿūl muṭlaq and mafʿūl li-ajlih come in Level 4.
- A -a on a verb is not a case. A kasra, -īna or -ayni can be genitive too: check the job.

**Next:** L2.06 teaches the genitive case, the third case, with the -i ending. Before you go on, review L2.04 (the nominative case) if the marks felt new.
