Each half of this verse is a nominal sentence. The khabar comes first: لَهُ, “to Him”. The mubtadaʾ comes second: ٱلْمُلْكُ, “the kingdom”. L2.02 taught that this order is allowed when the mubtadaʾ is definite. So the plain order, ٱلْمُلْكُ لَهُ (a made-up example), is correct too.

Grammar stops there, because both orders pass. Balagha asks the next question: why did the verse choose this one? Al-Jadwal answers in its note on 64:1. Putting لَهُ first points to Allah alone. The kingdom and the praise belong to Him, and not to anyone else. Any other king rules because Allah gave him power, and praise of another person is really praise for a favour that came through that person from Allah.

**Balāgha** (بَلَاغَة) comes from the root ب ل غ, “to reach”. The usual classroom definition is muṭābaqat al-kalām li-muqtaḍā l-ḥāl (مُطَابَقَةُ ٱلْكَلَامِ لِمُقْتَضَى ٱلْحَالِ): the wording matches what the situation asks for.

A situation is who is listening, what they already believe, and what the speaker wants. In 64:1 the speaker wants to say that all rule belongs to Allah, and the word order says it.

Balagha adds no new case endings and no new verb forms. It works on the choices that grammar leaves open. Which word comes first? Is the sentence strengthened or plain? Is the doer named or left out? Is the thing said plainly or by a picture?

<Note kind="know">This definition is a teaching summary. It is the one balagha manuals give, and Quran Learn has not checked it in an approved source for this course. The lesson uses it as a working idea. Where the lesson says why a verse chose a form, the reason comes from al-Jadwal’s note on that verse, and the lesson says so.</Note>

In 2:2 the noun comes first: لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ, “no doubt in it”. In 37:47, about the drink of Paradise, the phrase comes first: لَا فِيهَا غَوْلٌ, “no harm in it”. Both are correct Arabic.

Al-Jadwal explains the difference in its note on 2:2. Had that verse said لَا فِيهِ رَيْبٌ, the order would hint that some other book does hold doubt. That is how لَا فِيهَا غَوْلٌ says that the drink of Paradise has no harm, unlike the drinks of this world. With رَيْبَ first, the verse simply denies doubt.

| Looks at | Grammar asks | Balagha asks |
|---|---|---|
| Order | Is this order allowed? | Why this order among the allowed ones? |
| A little word | Which case does it give? | Why a strengthener here and none there? |
| A missing word | Is the sentence still whole? | Why was it left out? |

Your earlier lessons answered the middle column. This lesson starts on the right-hand column.

Balagha has three branches. The names are old, and al-Jadwal uses them. In its note on 11:44 it calls ʿilm al-bayān and ʿilm al-maʿānī the two bases of balagha. It says ʿilm al-maʿānī looks at the point of each word and at the reason for each fronting and delaying. It says ʿilm al-bayān looks at figures such as majāz, istiʿāra and kināya. In the same note it passes on a list of devices from the third branch, ʿilm al-badīʿ.

| Branch | Arabic | The question it asks | A verse in this lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maʿānī | عِلْم الْمَعَانِي | Why this order, this strengthener, this left-out word? | 64:1, 36:16, 11:44 |
| Bayān | عِلْم الْبَيَان | Why a picture instead of a plain statement? | 25:27 |
| Badīʿ | عِلْم الْبَدِيع | What pattern makes it beautiful? | 53:43 |

The one-line questions are a teaching summary. Books do not all file every device in the same branch, and one verse can use all three. Step 3 shows 11:44, which does.

Al-Jadwal is a book on the grammar of the Quran, and it prints a balagha note after the parse of many verses. On 16:40 it ends its note by saying the verse has a grandeur that leaves minds amazed.

The Quran also challenges its listeners directly. In 10:38 it says: فَأْتُوا۟ بِسُورَةٍ مِّثْلِهِۦ, “then bring a surah like it”. In 11:13 the call is for ten surahs. In 17:88 the verse says that if people and jinn joined together to bring the like of this Quran, they could not.

The word for this is iʿjāz (إِعْجَاز), “inimitability”. The three verses name no branch of balagha, and Quran Learn has no approved source in this course for how scholars connect style to the challenge. So the lesson stops at what the verses say.

The first half is a verbal sentence. Its verb زُيِّنَ is passive (L3.09), so the doer is not named, and the sentence goes on with another verb, وَيَسْخَرُونَ, “they mock”. The second half switches. It starts with a noun, so it is a nominal sentence (L2.03): ٱلَّذِينَ ٱتَّقَوْا۟ is the mubtadaʾ, and the place word فَوْقَهُمْ does the work of the khabar.

Al-Jadwal notices the switch. It says the verse puts the decoration of this life, and the mockery, in verbal sentences, which point to something that happens and passes. It puts the high place of the mindful in a nominal sentence, which points to something that lasts.

L2.03 warned you that “nominal means lasting” is not a rule of grammar. That still holds. Here it is al-Jadwal’s reading of this one verse, drawn from the meaning of the verse. Al-Jadwal makes a similar remark on 1:2, where ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ is a nominal sentence for praise that goes on. Do not carry the reading to every sentence of the same kind.

**Fronting** (taqdīm, تَقْدِيم) means putting a word earlier than its usual place. You have now met three kinds. In 64:1 the khabar goes before the mubtadaʾ. In 1:5 the object إِيَّاكَ goes before its verb, where the plain order is verb then object. In 2:3 the phrase that holds the object, مِمَّا رَزَقْنَٰهُمْ, goes before يُنفِقُونَ.

Al-Jadwal gives a reason for each. For 64:1 it says the kingdom and the praise belong to Allah alone. For 1:5 it says the fronting limits the worship and the asking for help to Allah. It adds that إِيَّاكَ is said a second time so that each verb keeps its own limited object. If the second one were dropped, the benefit of fronting would be lost. For 2:3 it gives a different reason: the thing they spend is put first because it matters more.

So fronting has more than one reading in these verses. In 64:1 and 1:5 it limits. In 2:3 it points to importance. The verse and its meaning decide, and a book that names the reason is how you know which.

First the news is given plainly: أَرْسَلْنَآ إِلَيْهِمُ ٱثْنَيْنِ. The people deny the two. Then comes إِنَّآ إِلَيْكُم مُّرْسَلُونَ, with two strengtheners. Al-Jadwal explains the silence in its note on 11:44. These are huge events, and only One with unmatched power could do them. The verse does not name Him, so nobody can wonder whether another did it. Al-Jadwal calls this a way of kināya, an indirect way of speaking. It says the same about the unnamed speaker of the commands to the earth and the sky at the start of the verse.

The grammar of a passive only tells you that the doer is absent. Why a verse leaves it out is a balagha reading, and in these verses it comes from al-Jadwal.

**Qaṣr** (قَصْر) is restriction: tying one thing to one other thing and denying it everything else. The frame وَمَا … إِلَّا does this, and you met it in L4.05. Without the frame, the first sentence would be مُحَمَّدٌ رَسُولٌ (made-up), which only says what he is. With the frame, he is tied to being a messenger.

Al-Jadwal names 3:144 a qaṣr and says what it answers. Some people had turned back, as if Muhammad would not pass away as the earlier messengers had. **Badīʿ** (بَدِيع) is the branch of patterns. In 53:43 two opposite actions sit side by side: made laugh and made weep. 53:44 does the same with died and lived (أَمَاتَ وَأَحْيَا), and 53:45 with the male and the female. Putting opposites together is ṭibāq (طِبَاق).

Al-Jadwal names it ṭibāq in its note on these verses. It adds that the pairs repeat, so the note files the whole run under muqābala (مُقَابَلَة), and that the matching sounds at the ends of the verses make the effect more beautiful.

The grammar of 53:43 is plain. هُوَ is the mubtadaʾ, and the clause أَضْحَكَ is its khabar, as in L2.03 and L3.01.

The question of 55:13 comes back 31 times in the surah, from 55:13 to 55:77. We counted it in the Corpus text, and every one of the 31 is in surah 55. L4.06 met this repeating as tawkīd lafẓī.

Al-Jadwal calls it takrīr (تَكْرِير), “repeating”. It says the repeat fits because the surah counts many different favours, and after each favour the question rebukes the denial of it. It compares this to a person who asks: “Did I not do this good to you? Did I not do that?” Its footnote says the refrain comes after passages on the wonders of creation, after passages on the Fire, and after the description of the gardens.

The books file devices like this in different branches. Al-Jadwal names takrīr here and does not say which branch it belongs to. This lesson does not say either.

Al-Jadwal reads 11:44 from several sides. It says the verse can be looked at through ʿilm al-bayān and ʿilm al-maʿānī. It also passes on Ibn Abī al-Iṣbaʿ, who found twenty kinds of badīʿ in the seventeen words of the verse. Some of what al-Jadwal says:

| What you see in 11:44 | What al-Jadwal says | Branch (teaching map) |
|---|---|---|
| يَٰٓأَرْضُ comes before ٱبْلَعِى | The call stands before the command, as when one speaks to someone who can hear and obey | Maʿānī |
| ٱبْلَعِى, “swallow”, for water sinking into the ground | A borrowed picture (istiʿāra): the earth is treated as one that swallows | Bayān |
| يَٰٓأَرْضُ and يَٰسَمَآءُ, earth and sky | A pair of opposites, which Ibn Abī al-Iṣbaʿ counts as ṭibāq | Badīʿ |
| The doers are not named | An indirect way of speaking (kināya): only Allah could do these things | Step 2 |

So one verse can use all three branches, and one feature can be read in more than one. The right-hand column is our sorting. The middle column is al-Jadwal’s reading.

<Step id="care" label="Reading with care" title="Reading a verse with care">
<Block id="method" title="Four questions for any verse">
  <Stage label="Ask in this order">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>The grammar:</b> what is each word doing? (Levels 2 to 4)</li>
      <li><b>The other ways:</b> what else could the verse have said in correct Arabic?</li>
      <li><b>The source:</b> does a named book say why the verse chose this one?</li>
      <li><b>The stop:</b> if no book says, show the choice and stop.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Use the four in this order. Most people go from the second question straight to a reason, and the reason is the part that needs a source. In this lesson, every reason comes from al-Jadwal, and the lesson names the verse where it says so.

Al-Jadwal prints a balagha paragraph after the parse of many verses, and not after every verse. When it prints none, you still have the grammar and the other ways. You do not have a reason.

<Block id="mistake-1" title="Mistake 1: a reason with no source behind it">
  <Stage label="Sort each statement">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Grammar:</b> in 64:1, لَهُ is tied to a khabar that is left out and put first.</li>
      <li><b>A named reading:</b> al-Jadwal says the fronting in 64:1 shows the kingdom is Allah’s alone.</li>
      <li><b>No named source:</b> the refrain of 55:13 makes the reader feel thankful.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Three kinds of statement appear in books and lessons about the Quran. The first is grammar: the parse. The second is a reading with a name behind it: a book says why. The third is a reading with no name behind it, such as a claim about what a listener feels. The third kind sounds the same as the other two, and it is common.

When you cannot name the book that says it, treat the statement as an opinion. This lesson uses only the first two kinds. For the refrain of 55:13, al-Jadwal says the repeat confirms each favour and rebukes the denial. It says nothing about the reader’s feelings, so the lesson says nothing either.

</Block>

<Block id="mistake-2" title="Mistake 2: one label for each feature">
  <Stage label="One feature, three readings">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Grammar:</b> غِيضَ is a passive verb, and ٱلْمَآءُ takes the doer’s place (L3.09).</li>
      <li><b>Maʿānī:</b> the doer is left out (ḥadhf).</li>
      <li><b>Bayān:</b> al-Jadwal calls leaving the doers unnamed an indirect way of speaking (kināya).</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

The branches overlap, as the table for 11:44 showed. The passive in وَغِيضَ ٱلْمَآءُ is a grammar fact, a left-out word, and, in al-Jadwal’s words, a way of kināya. All three are true at once.

So do not hunt for the one correct label. Name each thing the verse does, and give the source for each. Where books file a device in different branches, as with the repeat of 55:13, say that they differ.

<Block id="mistake-3" title="Mistake 3: turning a reading into a rule">
  <Stage label="Three readings that look like rules">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Fronting means “only”?</b> In 64:1 and 1:5, yes. In 2:3, al-Jadwal says importance.</li>
      <li><b>Nominal means lasting?</b> Al-Jadwal says so for 2:212 and 1:2. L2.03 says it is no rule of grammar.</li>
      <li><b>Strength shows doubt?</b> Al-Jadwal says so for 36:14 to 36:16: the strength follows what the listeners believe.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Each of these is a reading of particular verses. It comes from the meaning around the verse, and a book names it. None of them is a rule that works on every verse.

If you read a new verse and see a fronted word, ask the four questions. The answer may be “only”, “importance”, or nothing a named book says.

Some lessons list لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ as a restriction. Al-Jadwal does not. In its parse, لَا is the little word that denies a whole kind (L4.05, L4.10), and رَيْبَ is its noun. In its balagha note on 2:2 the first figure it names is fronting, the one you met in the first step.

In the verses of this lesson, al-Jadwal names qaṣr where the frame وَمَا … إِلَّا stands, as in 3:144 and 36:17. A denial of a whole kind with no “only” in it is a different thing.

- **Balagha** asks why a verse chose one correct wording over another. The usual definition is the match of the wording to the situation (a teaching summary).
- **Three branches:** maʿānī (the choices inside a sentence), bayān (pictures) and badīʿ (patterns). One verse, such as 11:44, can use all three.
- **Maʿānī:** fronting (64:1, 1:5, 2:3), two kinds of sentence in one verse (2:212), strengtheners that follow the listeners (36:14 to 36:16), a left-out doer or object (11:44, 36:14) and restriction (3:144, 36:17).
- **Bayān:** kināya (25:27), istiʿāra (14:1) and tashbīh (2:17), named by al-Jadwal. L4.18 teaches them.
- **Badīʿ:** ṭibāq in 53:43 to 53:45. The refrain of 55:13 is takrīr, and al-Jadwal does not file it.
- **Grammar and balagha:** grammar says a sentence is correct. Balagha asks why this correct sentence was chosen. A reason needs a named source, and a reading of one verse is not a rule.
