Read each verse from right to left. The English under each verse is our own plain meaning, not a translation. A marked word is one that English needs and the Arabic leaves out.

The surah has **11 verses** and **40 written words**, as the Quranic Arabic Corpus counts them. Verses 1 to 8 end in the sound ā, written ىٰ. Verses 9, 10 and 11 end in a letter with a sukūn.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal gives three reports about why the surah came down. They tell of a pause in revelation and of what people said about it. It calls the first, the report of Jundub ibn Sufyān, the strongest. This lesson teaches grammar and does not choose between the reports.</Note>

Verse 1 is one written word with two pieces. **وَ** is the oath letter (L4.06). It looks like the wāw that means “and”. Check the noun after it: it is genitive, and something is being sworn by.

**ٱلضُّحَىٰ** is the noun. It ends in an alif maqṣūra, so its genitive kasra is there but cannot show (L4.13). Al-Jadwal says the phrase belongs to a verb that is not written: **أُقْسِمُ**, “I swear”. The sentence “I swear by the morning light” opens the surah.

Verse 2 swears a second time. إِذَا means “when”. L4.04 listed it among the words that do **not** cut a verb short, and al-Jadwal goes one step further here: it calls إِذَا a time word with the condition taken away. Nothing is being made conditional. The oath is “by the night, at the time it grows still”. The verb after it is a plain past verb.

The clause سَجَىٰ is what إِذَا is added to, the way the first word of an idafah is added to the second. So al-Jadwal puts the clause in the place of the genitive.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal parses the first oath of this surah in words, and it does not parse the wāw of وَٱلَّيْلِ on its own. In 95:1 it reads the later oath-nouns as joined to the first, for example هَٰذَا, “joined to ٱلتِّينِ”. We read the wāw here the same way. This does not change what you see: ٱلَّيْلِ ends in a kasra.</Note>

L4.06 taught that an oath has two parts. The first names what you swear by: a letter and a noun in the genitive. The second is the **answer** (جَوَاب الْقَسَم), the sentence that is sworn to.

Here the oath is spoken in verses 1 and 2, and al-Jadwal calls the sentence of verse 3 its answer. It says no, in two clauses: مَا وَدَّعَكَ and وَمَا قَلَىٰ. Verses 4 and 5 go on, and al-Jadwal joins both to the answer. Each of them carries a لَ. Al-Jadwal calls this لَ the lām of the oath in both verses, and its footnote says why it appears: the speech is now affirming something, after a negative.

So the answer of the oath is three sentences long, and the فَ of verse 5 joins one more clause to it.

<Note kind="know">L4.06 called the لَ on a noun that starts a sentence “the beginning lām”, for example in 59:13. In 93:4 al-Jadwal calls the same letter on the same kind of noun the oath lām, because here it sits in the answer of an oath. Both names describe a لَ that strengthens a sentence. This lesson follows al-Jadwal’s reading of this surah.</Note>

يَتِيمًا ends in -an, and a ḥāl (L4.01) ends in -an too. The ending alone does not tell the two apart. L4.01 gave a test: **take the word out**. If a full sentence is left, the word was a ḥāl.

Try it. “The student left” is a full sentence, so ضَاحِكًا is a ḥāl. Now take يَتِيمًا out of verse 6: “Did He not find you?” The sentence asks “find you what?” and stops. The word was needed. Al-Jadwal says it is the second object of يَجِدْ, and L4.14 reads it the same way in 93:6 and in 93:7.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal states the second object in words for يَتِيمًا in 93:6. It gives no separate note for ضَآلًّا and عَآئِلًا. We read them the same way, because the frame of the three verses is the same.</Note>

Verses 9 to 11 use one frame three times. **أَمَّا** names a topic. After the topic comes a **فَ**, and then the answer. Al-Jadwal calls أَمَّا a word of condition and detail. It sits near the condition words of L4.04, but it behaves differently in two ways.

In verses 9 and 10 the topic was an object. In verse 11 the topic is a **phrase**: a preposition, بِ, and its noun. Al-Jadwal says the phrase belongs to the verb حَدِّثْ, and the فَ between them does not prevent that. English puts it after the verb: “speak of the favour”.

**حَدِّثْ** is a command of Form II (L3.12): the middle letter is doubled. A command built from a sound verb ends in a sukūn (L3.08).

Al-Jadwal adds two style notes of its own on this surah.

- It marks ضَآلًّا فَهَدَىٰ in verse 7 as a metaphor (الاستعارة التصريحية) and explains the two words in its own note. What “lost” means in this verse is a matter for the books of tafsir. This lesson does not decide it.

<Block id="m1" title="Mistake 1: reading the time from the negative word">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="two negative words, two verb forms, one kind of meaning"
      items={[
        { ar: 'مَا وَدَّعَ', en: 'مَا + a past verb: did not', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'لَمْ يَجِدْ', en: 'لَمْ + jazm: did not', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'مَا يَنۢبَغِى', en: 'مَا + a present verb (36:69): does not', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

It is tempting to say “مَا is for the past and لَمْ is for the present”. The surah shows both in the past, and L4.10 shows more.

- **مَا** stands before a past verb (93:3), before a present verb (36:69) and before nouns. It changes nothing. The verb sets the time.
- **لَمْ** stands before a present verb and puts it in jazm. The form is present and the meaning is past.

In verse 3, مَا وَدَّعَكَ and, in verse 6, لَمْ يَجِدْكَ both say “did not”. They are two ways to say it.
</Block>

<Block id="m2" title="Mistake 2: calling يَجِدْكَ a hollow verb">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="where the weak letter is, in three roots"
      items={[
        { ar: 'و ج د', en: 'weak letter first: assimilated (L4.14)', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ق و ل', en: 'weak letter in the middle: hollow (L4.12)', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'ه د ي', en: 'weak letter last: defective (L4.13)', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

The root of يَجِدْكَ is و ج د. The و is the **first** letter. Hollow verbs (L4.12) have their weak letter in the middle, like قَالَ, root ق و ل.

يَجِدْ looks short, and a short verb makes some learners think of hollow verbs, which also lose a letter in jazm (L4.12). The test is the root: where does the weak letter sit?

The surah has no hollow verb at all.
</Block>

<Block id="m3" title="Mistake 3: calling يَتِيمًا a ḥāl">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="three words in -an after وَجَدَ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'يَتِيمًا', en: 'second object (93:6)' },
        { ar: 'ضَآلًّا', en: 'second object (93:7)' },
        { ar: 'عَآئِلًا', en: 'second object (93:8)' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

An ending in -an is a mark of the accusative, and the accusative has many jobs (L2.05). A ḥāl is one of them (L4.01). It comes after a complete sentence, and you can take it out.

يَتِيمًا, ضَآلًّا and عَآئِلًا cannot be taken out of their sentences: “He found you …” is unfinished. They are second objects of وَجَدَ, as al-Jadwal reads يَتِيمًا in verse 6 and L4.14 reads all three. This surah has no ḥāl in it.
</Block>

<Block id="m4" title="Mistake 4: reading the root from the spelling">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="four verbs that end in ىٰ"
      items={[
        { ar: 'سَجَىٰ', en: 'root س ج و', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'تَرْضَىٰ', en: 'root ر ض و', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'قَلَىٰ', en: 'root ق ل ي', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'هَدَىٰ', en: 'root ه د ي', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

All four verbs end in ىٰ, and they are not from one kind of root. Two have و as their last root letter and two have ي, if you go by the Corpus. The usual spelling gives ىٰ to roots with ي and an alif to roots with و, but the Quran has exceptions (L4.13). سَجَىٰ is one of them. تَرْضَىٰ is another case: its past is رَضِيَ, and the root has و.

Do not decide the root from the last letter. Use the “I” form or the present, and check the root in the Corpus (L4.13).
</Block>

<Block id="m5" title="Mistake 5: three different لَ in one verse">
  <Stage>
    <Tiles
      label="the lām in verse 4"
      items={[
        { ar: 'وَلَلْءَاخِرَةُ', en: 'the lām of the oath, then the lām of ٱل', type: 'n' },
        { ar: 'لَّكَ', en: 'the preposition li- with -ka: for you', type: 'p' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Verse 4 has three lāms. The first belongs to the oath: it strengthens the sentence and changes no ending (L4.06). The second belongs to the article ٱلْ. The third, in لَّكَ, is the preposition li-, “for”, joined to the pronoun كَ, and it does pull its noun into the genitive (L2.07).

L4.06 also showed a لَ that only ties the answer of لَوْ. When you see a لَ, ask what stands before it and what it is joined to.
</Block>
