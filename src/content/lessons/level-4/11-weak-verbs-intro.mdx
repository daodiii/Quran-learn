Read the verse from right to left. The Corpus tags ten of its words as verbs, and the verbs are coloured. Look at their shapes.

- **يَخْلُقُ**, “he creates”: root خ ل ق. All three root letters are there, in order. This is a sound verb, like the ones in L3.04.
- **قَالَتْ, قَالَ, يَقُولُ**: root ق و ل. Where the root has و, you read a long ā or a long ū. No plain و.
- **يَكُونُ, كُن, فَيَكُونُ**: root ك و ن. The same. In كُن the و has gone altogether.
- **يَشَآءُ**: root ش ي ء. The ي shows as a long ā, and the last root letter is a hamza.
- **قَضَىٰٓ**: root ق ض ي. The last root letter ي shows as ىٰ.
- **يَمْسَسْنِى**: root م س س. One letter, س, stands twice.

Nine of the ten verbs are not sound. Each of the shapes above is one kind of verb, and each kind has its own lesson, L4.12 to L4.15. This lesson is the map. The last block of this step counts how common each kind is.

Compare the two rows. In every form of كَتَبَ you can read ك, then ت, then ب. The root stays in view.

Now read the second row. قَالَ has no و, only a long ā. يَقُولُونَ shows the و as a long ū. قُلْ has no و at all. قِيلَ has a ي that is not in the root. The letter و does not hold its place. That is what **weak** means: the letter gives way.

The three weak letters are **و**, **ي** and the long **ā** (ا). They are called حُرُوف الْعِلَّة (*ḥurūf al-ʿilla*). In a root, as the Corpus writes it, the weak letters are only **و** and **ي**. The ā is a long vowel that stands in for one of them: in قَالَ it stands for the و of ق و ل.

The **hamza** (ء) is not one of the three. A verb with a hamza also changes shape, mostly in how it is written. Many books place it near the weak verbs, and this lesson does the same. L4.15 teaches it.

Every verb root in the Quran falls into one of six kinds. The kind depends on where the special letter sits in the root.

| Kind | Arabic | The root has | Example root | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sound | صَحِيح | no weak letter, no hamza, no repeat | خ ل ق | يَخْلُقُ |
| Hollow | أَجْوَف | a weak letter in the middle | ق و ل | قَالَ |
| Defective | نَاقِص | a weak letter at the end | ق ض ي | قَضَىٰٓ |
| Assimilated | مِثَال | a weak letter at the start | و ق ع | وَقَعَتِ |
| Hamzated | مَهْمُوز | a hamza as one of the letters | أ م ر | يَأْمُرُ |
| Doubled | مُضَعَّف | the last two letters the same | م س س | مَسَّ |

Hollow, defective and assimilated verbs have a weak letter. They are the **weak verbs**, الْأَفْعَال الْمُعْتَلَّة. Hamzated and doubled verbs have no weak letter, but their letters also change shape, so they are taught in the same run of lessons. Some roots belong to two kinds: جَآءَ, from ج ي ء, has a weak middle letter and a hamza. The next step shows how to read them.

<Note kind="know">The Arabic names are the ones the classical books use. English books use other names for the same kinds, so keep the Arabic name beside the English one.</Note>

Quran Learn counted the kinds again for this lesson, from the Quranic Arabic Corpus. **The method:** the Corpus gives a root for every word. We took every word with a verb part (19,356) and read its root. The Corpus writes a hamza in a root as the letter A. A root is hollow if its second letter is و or ي, defective if its third is, assimilated if its first is, hamzated if it has a hamza, and doubled if its second and third letters are the same strong letter. A word can fall in more than one kind, so the shares below add up to more than 58.4%.

| Kind | Verb words | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Sound | 8,030 | 41.5% |
| Hollow | 5,162 | 26.7% |
| Defective | 3,481 | 18.0% |
| Hamzated | 2,955 | 15.3% |
| Assimilated | 999 | 5.2% |
| Doubled | 872 | 4.5% |

Four of these counts match the earlier Quran Learn curriculum review (hollow, defective, hamzated and assimilated). The review gave 945 for the doubled verbs. That count also holds 73 words from the roots ح ي ي and ع ي ي, whose last letter is a ي, so this lesson counts them as defective and leaves them out here.

Two more numbers. Verb words with a weak letter (و or ي) in the root are 9,021, or 46.6%. Words that fall in two or more kinds are 2,144, or 11.1%. Two roots alone, ق و ل (1,620 words) and ك و ن (1,358), make 2,978 verb words, or 15.4%.

Is ٱهْدِنَا a weak verb? Four steps tell you.

1. **Peel it.** The ending -نَا is “us”. What is left is ٱهْدِ, “guide!”. The ٱ in front is the helping alif of a command (L3.08), not a root letter.
2. **Count the letters.** Two are left: ه and د. A root has three, so one has dropped.
3. **Find the letter.** The Corpus lists the root as ه د ي. The missing letter is the last one, ي.
4. **Name the kind.** The ي is the third root letter, so ٱهْدِنَا is a **defective** verb.

Al-Jadwal says the same about the missing letter. It gives the matching present verb, تَهْدِينَا, “you guide us”, with the ي in it. The command ٱهْدِ has lost that ي already, before the ending -nā comes on. The ي does not drop because of -nā.

<Note kind="tip">Do not learn the kind of every verb one by one. Learn the four steps. If you can peel a verb down to its root and compare the letters with the three of a sound verb, you will find the weak letter.</Note>

A word often shows a clue before you look up its root. Here are six.

| What you see | What to suspect | Example, with its root |
|---|---|---|
| A long ā, ū or ī in the middle, with only two other letters | Hollow | قَالَ, يَقُولُ: ق و ل |
| A long ā (ىٰ or ا) at the end, after two letters | Defective | قَضَىٰٓ: ق ض ي |
| A command or a verb after لَمْ with only two letters, the last one gone | Defective | ٱهْدِ: ه د ي. يَخْشَ: خ ش ي |
| A present verb with only two letters after the prefix, the first one gone | Assimilated | يَعِدُ: و ع د |
| A hamza on ا, و or ي, or by itself, inside the verb | Hamzated | يَأْمُرُ: أ م ر |
| A shadda on the last root letter | Doubled | مَسَّ: م س س |

Only the root decides. When a clue is not enough, look at the root in the Corpus. It lists the full root even when the word shows less (L1.05, L3.03).

<Note kind="know">The clues are a teaching summary. They fit most verbs, and L4.12 to L4.15 give the cases where they do not. No approved book states the weak-verb rules as one list.</Note>

The verb وَقَعَتِ has the root و ق ع. The first letter is a weak و, so this is an assimilated verb. But look at it: in the past the و stands in plain view, and you could take the verb for a sound one.

The trouble comes in the present. The present of وَعَدَ, “he promised”, is يَعِدُ, “he promises”. The و has gone. In Al-Baqarah 2:268 the verse has يَعِدُكُمُ ٱلْفَقْرَ, “he promises you poverty”. Al-Jadwal says the first letter is dropped in the present, and gives the pattern of يَعِدُكُمْ as يَعِلُكُمْ.

| Root | Past | Present |
|---|---|---|
| و ع د | وَعَدَ, “he promised” | يَعِدُ, “he promises” |
| و ج د | وَجَدَ, “he found” | يَجِدْ, “he finds” (after لَمْ, Al-Baqarah 2:196) |

The kind of an assimilated verb is easy to miss in the past and easy to see in the present. L4.14 teaches when the و drops.

Four verbs in this verse, four kinds.

- **ءَامَنَ**, root أ م ن: hamzated.
- **أَقَامَ**, root ق و م: hollow.
- **ءَاتَى**, root أ ت ي: it has a hamza at the start and a weak ي at the end, so it belongs to **two kinds**: hamzated and defective.
- **يَخْشَ**, root خ ش ي: defective. The plain present is يَخْشَىٰ. After لَمْ the weak last letter drops. Al-Jadwal says the sign of jazm here is the dropping of the weak letter.

Many roots hold two kinds. A root with two weak letters is called لَفِيف (*lafīf*). Here are the most common roots with two kinds, with their counts from the Corpus.

| Word | Root | Kinds | Verb words from the root |
|---|---|---|---|
| أَتَىٰٓ | أ ت ي | hamzated and defective | 535 |
| رَءَا | ر أ ي | hamzated and defective | 315 |

<Step id="change" label="What changes" title="What happens to the weak letter">
<Block id="three" title="Three things can happen">
  <Stage label="Three kinds of iʿlāl">
    <Tiles
      label="The three changes, in the words of al-Jadwal"
      items={[
        { ar: 'إِعْلَال بِالْقَلْب', en: 'it turns into another letter', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'إِعْلَال بِالتَّسْكِين', en: 'it gets a sukūn', type: 'x' },
        { ar: 'إِعْلَال بِالْحَذْف', en: 'it drops out', type: 'x' },

A weak letter is hard to say with a short vowel on it. The books say the vowel is “heavy” on it, and the letter gives way. This happens in three ways. Al-Jadwal names each way in its notes on word shapes (*ṣarf*), and calls the change *iʿlāl*.

| The change | What you see | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The weak letter turns into another letter (قَلْب) | a different letter stands where it was | قَضَىٰٓ: the ي became a long ā |
| The weak letter gets a sukūn (تَسْكِين) | its vowel moves to the letter before | نَسْتَعِينُ |
| The weak letter drops out (حَذْف) | the word is shorter | قُلْ, ٱهْدِنَا, يَعِدُ |

Often two changes meet in one word. The next three blocks give an example of each, with the note of al-Jadwal that names it.

<Note kind="know">The three-way split is how al-Jadwal names the changes. No approved book gives one full list of rules for weak verbs, so this lesson teaches the kinds of change and leaves the rules to L4.12 to L4.15. Where the lesson applies a rule from al-Jadwal to a verb the book does not name, it says so.</Note>

In the second half of Al ʿImran 3:47, the verb قَضَىٰٓ began as قَضَيَ. Al-Jadwal says the ي had a fatḥa and came after a fatḥa, so it turned into an alif. The letter is still a ي in the root, and the Corpus lists ق ض ي.

The same book explains قِيلَ (Al-Baqarah 2:11). The verb began as قُوِلَ. The و cannot carry the kasra, so the kasra moved to the ق and the و turned into a ي. That is two changes in one word: a sukūn and a turn.

In ٱتَّقَوْا۟ and تَتَّقُونَ the root is و ق ي, and the first letter, a weak و, turned into a ت. Al-Jadwal explains it on Al-Baqarah 2:21: the verb began as ٱوْتَقَىٰ, and the و turned into ت because it stood before the ت of Form VIII. The two ت merged.

The same turn explains قَالَ: its و, with a fatḥa after a fatḥa, became a long ā. Al-Jadwal gives this rule for the ي of قَضَىٰٓ. This lesson applies it to the و of قَالَ, and the book does not say it for that verb.

<Note kind="tip">For a verb of three root letters, the way a final ā is written tells you the weak letter: ا stands for و (دَعَا, from د ع و) and ىٰ stands for ي (قَضَىٰٓ, from ق ض ي). This is a teaching summary.</Note>

<Block id="sukun" title="It gets a sukūn">
  <Stage label="Three steps in one word">
    <Tiles
      label="From the starting shape to the word you read"
      items={[
        { ar: 'نَسْتَعْوِنُ', en: 'starting shape: the و carries a kasra', type: 'x' },

The verb نَسْتَعِينُ, “we ask for help”, comes from ع و ن. Al-Jadwal gives the steps on Al-Fatiha 1:5, and they give a good picture of iʿlāl.

1. The verb begins as نَسْتَعْوِنُ, with a kasra on the و.
2. A kasra is heavy on a weak letter, so it **moves back** to the ع. The و is left with a sukūn. The book calls this *iʿlāl bi-t-taskīn*.
3. A و with a sukūn after a kasra turns into ي. The book calls this *iʿlāl bi-l-qalb*.

The same thing happens in يَقُولُ. The starting shape يَقْوُلُ has a ḍamma on the و. The ḍamma moves back to the ق. The و is left with no vowel of its own after a ḍamma, and that is a long ū (L1.02). This is the standard account and a teaching summary for this verb. The book does not spell it out for يَقُولُ.

This kind of change is why a hollow verb seems to have a long vowel in the middle. The root letter is still there, with a sukūn.

A doubled verb has no weak letter. Its second and third root letters are the same strong letter: م س س, ض ل ل. When the second one carries a vowel, the two letters merge into one, written with a shadda. This is مَسَّ. The merging is called إِدْغَام (*idghām*).

When the last root letter has a sukūn, the two letters cannot merge, and they stay apart. This is يَمْسَسْكُمْ after إِن (jazm), and ضَلَلْتُ, where the ending -tu puts a sukūn on the last root letter (L3.05). Al-Baqarah 2:217 shows the same pair: يَرُدُّوكُمْ is merged, and يَرْتَدِدْ is apart.

L3.03 gave رَبِّكَ (An-Nasr 110:3) as an example of a doubled root. It is a noun, from ر ب ب. The shadda in رَبِّ is the same merging. Doubled roots are 872 verb words in the Corpus (4.5%).

<Note kind="warn">A shadda on a verb does not always mean a doubled root. In كَذَّبَ the shadda is Form II (L3.12), and the root ك ذ ب has three different letters. Count the root letters first.</Note>

<Block id="mistake-root" title="Mistake 1: reading the root off the word">
  <Stage label="The same verb, two readings">
    <Tiles
      label="What you see and what the root is"
      items={[
        { ar: 'قَالَ', en: 'you see ق ا ل, the root is ق و ل', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'قَضَىٰٓ', en: 'you see ق ض ىٰ, the root is ق ض ي', type: 'v' },
      ]}

The root of قَالَ is not ق ا ل. The alif is never a letter of the root: in the Corpus every root with a weak letter is written with **و** or **ي**. The ā in the word stands for one of them. Likewise ىٰ at the end of قَضَىٰٓ stands for ي.

So when a verb has a long ā, do not write it into the root. Ask which weak letter it stands for. Check with the Corpus.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-aa" title="Mistake 2: a long ā always means a weak letter">
  <Stage label="Two verbs with a long ā">
    <Tiles

قَاتَلَ has a long ā in the middle, like قَالَ. But قَاتَلَ is Form III (L3.17): the ā was added by the form, and the root ق ت ل is sound. The test is the one from L3.04: take the extra letter away and count what is left. قَاتَلَ leaves three root letters. قَالَ leaves two, so a root letter is hidden.
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-kinds" title="Mistake 3: mixing up the hamza, the doubled letter and the weak letter">
  <Stage label="Three letters, three kinds">
    <Tiles
      label="Which one is not a weak letter?"
      items={[

The hamza is not a weak letter, and a repeated letter is not a weak letter. A hamzated verb and a doubled verb are not weak verbs, even though they are taught in the same run of lessons.

The mistake in the other direction is to think a root belongs to one kind only. جَآءَ is hollow and hamzated. أَتَىٰٓ is hamzated and defective. When you name a verb, check every letter of the root.
</Block>

- **Six kinds:** sound, hollow (weak middle letter), defective (weak last letter), assimilated (weak first letter), hamzated and doubled. A root can have two.
- **Weak letters:** و and ي in the root. The ā stands for one of them. The hamza and a repeated letter are not weak, but their words also change shape.
- **Find the kind:** peel off what was added, count the root letters, look up the root, and name the kind from where the special letter sits.
- **Three changes:** the weak letter turns into another letter (قَضَىٰٓ), drops out (قُلْ, يَعِدُ) or gets a sukūn (نَسْتَعِينُ). Often two meet in one word.
- **Counts:** 41.5% of verb words in the Corpus are sound. Hollow verbs are 26.7%, defective 18.0%, hamzated 15.3%, assimilated 5.2% and doubled 4.5%.
- The clues in this lesson are a teaching summary. The root, in the Corpus, tells you what is true.
