A wind destroyed the people of ʿĀd. The verse says how long it blew. Read the line from right to left.

Look at the two numbers. سَبْعَ, “seven”, has no round ة at the end. ثَمَٰنِيَةَ, “eight”, has one.

Now look at what they count. لَيَالٍ is “nights”, the plural of لَيْلَة, a feminine word. أَيَّامٍ is “days”, the plural of يَوْمٌ, a masculine word.

So the number for the feminine word looks masculine, and the number for the masculine word looks feminine. The number turns around. English has nothing like it, and most of this lesson is about this turn and about the places where it stops.

The number word is the **ʿadad** (عَدَد). The thing it counts is the **maʿdūd** (مَعْدُود), “the counted”. Every number phrase raises three questions.

- **The form.** What form does the number take? Does it end in ة or not?
- **The counted noun.** Which case is it in, and is it singular or plural?
- **Which comes first?** Usually the number, but not always.

L4.02 answered the second question for 11 to 99: the counted word is a singular accusative tamyīz. This lesson answers the first question for every number, and the second for the other numbers.

The 61 words for “one” include the name of Allah ٱلْوَٰحِدُ, which occurs 6 times. Four things stand out.

- **Seven** is the busiest number from 3 to 10: 24 of the 84 words.
- From 11 to 19 only **11, 12 and 19** occur. Twelve occurs five times, eleven once (12:4) and nineteen once (74:30).
- **21 to 98** never occur. 99 occurs once, in 38:23.
- Of the ordinals, the Corpus has the 2nd to the 6th and the 8th. It has no 7th, 9th or 10th.

<Note kind="know">The Corpus tags a number word as feminine (F) when its own form ends in ة, and as masculine (M) when it does not. The tag follows the form of the number, not the gender of what it counts. That is why you will see an F tag on the number for a masculine noun.</Note>

One and two are the easy numbers. They come **after** the noun, like an adjective, and agree with it in gender and case. In 16:51 al-Jadwal reads both ٱثْنَيْنِ and وَٰحِدٌ as a naʿt, a describing word.

For a feminine noun, one takes the round ة: نَّفْسٍ وَٰحِدَةٍ, “one soul” (4:1 and 39:6). Two has only dual endings: ٱثْنَيْنِ for a masculine noun, ٱثْنَتَيْنِ for a feminine one. The Corpus has 61 words for “one” (30 masculine, 31 feminine) and 20 for “two” (13 masculine, 7 feminine). The made-up form ٱثْنَتَانِ is regular but the Quran does not use it.

Why add a number at all? Al-Jadwal’s note on this verse answers. A singular noun already says “one” and a dual noun already says “two”. The number is added only to stress it. Without وَٰحِدٌ, the words إِنَّمَا هُوَ إِلَٰهٌ could sound like a statement that He is a god. With it, the point is that He is one.

Here is the rule for 3 to 10. The number goes against the counted noun.

- With a **masculine** noun the number takes the form **with ة**: ثَلَٰثَةُ أَشْهُرٍ, “three months”.
- With a **feminine** noun the number takes the form **without ة**: ثَلَٰثُ عَوْرَٰتٍ, “three times of exposure”.

Al-Jadwal states the rule in a note on 6:160, and says the number from three to ten goes against the counted noun. It gives the same reason in notes on 69:7, 2:196 and 56:7: the number has ة because the counted noun is masculine.

The table shows a verse for each form. Two forms are made up: the Quran never uses five or six without ة. Five occurs three times and six seven times, always with ة.

**Which gender counts?** The gender of the **singular** word. Al-Jadwal says this in a note on 27:48: with a plural, take the gender of its singular. سِنِينَ ends like a masculine plural, but it is the plural of سَنَة, a feminine word. أَيَّامٍ is the plural of يَوْمٌ, a masculine word.

<Note kind="know">This is a teaching summary. Al-Jadwal states the rule in its notes (on 6:160, 7:160 and 27:48), but none of the other books in this project gives the number rules. So the lesson checks the rule against the verses and gives Quran Learn’s own counts.</Note>

Zakariyya asks for a sign, and the sign is three nights without speaking. لَيْلَة, “night”, is feminine, so ثَلَٰثَ has no ة. Al-Jadwal parses ثَلَٰثَ as a time word (ẓarf) and لَيَالٍ as its muḍāf ilayh.

Salih says this to his people after they killed the she-camel. يَوْمٌ, “day”, is masculine, so the number is ثَلَٰثَةَ, with ة. Put the two phrases next to each other: three **nights** has no ة, three **days** has one.

Days is the noun counted most often with ة: 13 of the 28 places where a masculine noun is written. “Six days” (سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ) is how seven verses count the creation (7:54, 10:3, 11:7, 25:59, 32:4, 50:38 and 57:4), and all seven have ة. Other masculine nouns follow the same pattern: أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ, “four months” (9:2), عَشَرَةِ مَسَٰكِينَ, “ten poor people” (5:89) and ثَلَٰثَةَ قُرُوٓءٍ, “three qurūʾ”, the waiting time after divorce (2:228).

An ordinal says the place in a line: third, fourth, fifth. It has the pattern **فَاعِل**: ثَالِث, رَابِع, خَامِس, سَادِس, ثَامِن. Al-Jadwal says, in a note on 18:22, that a number takes this pattern when it shows order (ٱلتَّرْتِيب).

The Quran joins an ordinal to a number in two ways.

- **Ordinal + its number:** ثَالِثُ ثَلَٰثَةٍ, “the third of three”. It is an idafah (5:73). Al-Jadwal reads ثَالِثُ as a khabar and ثَلَٰثَةٍ as the muḍāf ilayh. The verse reports what some people say, and the Quran calls them unbelievers for it.
- **Ordinal + a pronoun:** رَابِعُهُمْ, “the fourth of them”, in the sense “the one who makes them four” (58:7). The verse says that whenever three people whisper together, He is the fourth, and with five, the sixth.

**No turn-around.** An ordinal agrees with its noun like any describing word: masculine with a masculine noun, feminine with a feminine one. The number it comes from still turns around: ثَلَٰثَةٍ has ة in both verses, as it does with a masculine noun that is not written.

        { ar: 'ثَلَاثَةُ نِسَاءٍ', en: 'feminine noun, with ة: wrong', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A common mistake is to match: to put ة on the number because the noun is feminine. From 3 to 10 the number goes the other way. The same pair appears in al-Jadwal’s note on 27:48 as its own example: three men, three women.

A quick test. See the number, look at the noun’s singular, and say the opposite. A number with ة goes with a masculine noun, and a number without ة goes with a feminine one.

<Block id="m-plural" title="Mistake 2: judging the noun by its plural">
  <Stage label="Plurals that fool the eye">
    <Tiles

        { ar: 'ثَلَٰثَةَ قُرُوٓءٍ', en: 'qarʾ is masculine (2:228)', type: 'x' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

A plural can hide the gender. سِنِينَ ends in -īna, like a masculine plural, but it is the plural of سَنَة, a feminine word, so the number is سَبْعَ. حِجَجٍ looks like a masculine word and is the plural of حِجَّة, feminine, so the number is ثَمَٰنِىَ. قُرُوءٍ is the plural of قَرْء, masculine, so the number is ثَلَٰثَةَ.

Al-Jadwal gives the rule in the note on 27:48: with a plural, the gender is taken from the singular. Do not judge by how the plural looks.

From 13 to 19 the two parts do not match each other. The first part turns around, the second part agrees. With a masculine noun the first part has ة and the second does not: تِسْعَةَ عَشَرَ. With a feminine noun the first part has no ة and the second does: تِسْعَ عَشْرَةَ. Putting ة on both is wrong.

For 11 and 12 both parts agree with the noun and with each other: أَحَدَ عَشَرَ and إِحْدَىٰ عَشْرَةَ, ٱثْنَا عَشَرَ and ٱثْنَتَا عَشْرَةَ.

The Quran uses only 11, 12 and 19, so the forms of 13 to 18 and the feminine of 19 are known from the grammar notes and not from verses.

<Step id="practice" label="Practice" title="Practice">
<Block id="practice-1" title="Practice: forms and agreement">
  <Stage label="For each number, ask">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Which number is it?</b> One or two, 3 to 10, 11 to 19, a ten, a hundred or a thousand.</li>
      <li><b>The noun’s gender?</b> Judge a plural by its singular.</li>
      <li><b>Which form?</b> From 3 to 10 the opposite of the noun. One, two and ordinals agree.</li>
      <li><b>Which case?</b> For the counted noun: plural genitive, singular accusative or singular genitive.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Right or wrong? (a) سَبْعَ سَمَٰوَٰتٍ (Al-Mulk 67:3, part) (b) ثَلَٰثَةِ أَيَّامٍ (Al-Baqarah 2:196, part) (c) خَمْسَةِ لَيَالٍ (made up) (d) ثَلَاثُ رِجَالٍ (made up)">

- (a) **Right.** سَمَٰوَٰتٍ is the plural of سَمَاء, a feminine word, so سَبْعَ has no ة. The counted noun is plural and genitive.
- (b) **Right.** أَيَّامٍ is the plural of يَوْمٌ, a masculine word, so ثَلَٰثَةِ has ة.
- (c) **Wrong.** لَيْلَة is feminine, so the number has no ة: **خَمْسِ لَيَالٍ**.
- (d) **Wrong.** رَجُلٌ is masculine, so the number has ة: **ثَلَاثَةُ رِجَالٍ**.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Write the number and its counted noun (made up). (a) five men: رَجُلٌ, plural رِجَالٌ (b) seven nights: لَيْلَةٌ, plural لَيَالٍ (c) eleven stars: كَوْكَبٌ (d) a thousand years: سَنَةٌ">

- (a) **خَمْسَةُ رِجَالٍ.** Men is masculine, so “five” has ة. The counted noun is plural and genitive.
- (b) **سَبْعُ لَيَالٍ.** Nights is feminine, so “seven” has no ة.
- (c) **أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا.** This is the Quran’s own phrase in 12:4. Both parts agree with the masculine noun, and كَوْكَبًا is a singular accusative tamyīz.
- (d) **أَلْفُ سَنَةٍ.** The counted noun is singular and genitive. (In 2:96 the Quran has it in the accusative: أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ.)

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Explain the form of the number. (a) أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ وَعَشْرًا (Al-Baqarah 2:234, part) (b) ثَلَٰثَ لَيَالٍ (Maryam 19:10, part) (c) فَلَهُۥ عَشْرُ أَمْثَالِهَا (Al-Anʿam 6:160, part)">

- (a) أَرْبَعَةَ has ة because أَشْهُرٍ is the plural of شَهْر, masculine. وَعَشْرًا has no ة because the unwritten noun is nights, a feminine word, in al-Jadwal’s reading.
- (b) ثَلَٰثَ has no ة because لَيَالٍ is the plural of لَيْلَة, feminine.
- (c) This is the one place that breaks the rule. أَمْثَالٌ is the plural of مِثْل, masculine, so the rule expects عَشَرَةُ. Al-Jadwal says the verse goes against the rule and reads the noun by its meaning, “ten good deeds”, which is feminine.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Read each number phrase: the number, the counted noun and its gender, and the form. (a) وَلَيَالٍ عَشْرٍ (Al-Fajr 89:2) (b) تِلْكَ عَشَرَةٌ كَامِلَةٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:196, part) (c) ٱثْنَتَىْ عَشْرَةَ أَسْبَاطًا (Al-Aʿraf 7:160, part) (d) ٱثْنَا عَشَرَ شَهْرًا (At-Tawbah 9:36, part)">

- (a) The number comes **after** the noun. لَيَالٍ is feminine (from لَيْلَة), so عَشْرٍ has no ة. The Corpus tags it as an adjective.
- (b) عَشَرَةٌ has ة because the counted noun, days, is masculine. كَامِلَةٌ describes it and agrees with it in form.
- (c) This is twelve with a feminine form: both parts are feminine. أَسْبَاطًا is a **plural**, so it is not a singular tamyīz. Al-Jadwal reads it as describing an omitted noun, فِرْقَة or أُمَّة, which is feminine.
- (d) ٱثْنَا is nominative with an alif and عَشَرَ is built on fatḥa. Both parts are masculine because شَهْر is masculine, and شَهْرًا is a singular accusative tamyīz.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Find every number, its counted noun, and say whether the number turns around. (a) سَخَّرَهَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَبْعَ لَيَالٍ وَثَمَٰنِيَةَ أَيَّامٍ حُسُومًا (Al-Haqqah 69:7, part) (b) وَبَعَثْنَا مِنْهُمُ ٱثْنَىْ عَشَرَ نَقِيبًا (Al-Ma'idah 5:12, part) (c) وَسَبْعَ سُنۢبُلَٰتٍ خُضْرٍ (Yusuf 12:43, part)">

- (a) **Two numbers.** سَبْعَ counts لَيَالٍ (feminine) and has no ة. ثَمَٰنِيَةَ counts أَيَّامٍ (masculine) and has ة. Both counted nouns are plural and genitive.
- (b) **One number, 12.** ٱثْنَىْ عَشَرَ agrees with the masculine noun, and it ends in -ay because it is the object. نَقِيبًا is a singular accusative tamyīz. The number does not turn around, because 11 and 12 agree.
- (c) سَبْعَ counts سُنۢبُلَٰتٍ, the plural of سُنْبُلَة, feminine, so it has no ة. خُضْرٍ is a genitive describing word.

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Exercise n={6} q="Which noun is understood after the number? Use the form of the number. (a) وَسَبْعَةٍ (Al-Baqarah 2:196, part) (b) سَبْعٌ شِدَادٌ (Yusuf 12:48, part) (c) ثَلَٰثَةٌ رَّابِعُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ (Al-Kahf 18:22, part) (d) عَلَيْهَا تِسْعَةَ عَشَرَ (Al-Muddaththir 74:30)">

- (a) **Days.** The number has ة, so the unwritten noun is masculine. Al-Jadwal says عَشَرَةٌ later in the verse has its form because its noun is days.
- (b) **Years.** The number has no ة, so the noun is feminine. The story in 12:47 speaks of years (سِنِينَ).
- (c) **Men.** The number has ة, so the noun is masculine. Al-Jadwal reads ثَلَٰثَةٌ as the khabar of an omitted “they are”.
- (d) **Angels.** تِسْعَةَ has ة. Al-Jadwal gives “nineteen angels” as the understood phrase, and 74:31 names angels.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Say the case and the number (singular or plural) of the counted noun. (a) خَمْسِينَ عَامًا (Al-ʿAnkabut 29:14, part) (b) مِا۟ئَةَ جَلْدَةٍ (An-Nur 24:2, part) (c) أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا (Yusuf 12:4, part) (d) سَبْعَ سِنِينَ (Yusuf 12:47, part)">

- (a) عَامًا is **singular accusative**: the tamyīz of fifty.
- (b) جَلْدَةٍ is **singular genitive**: the muḍāf ilayh of a hundred.
- (c) كَوْكَبًا is **singular accusative**: the tamyīz of eleven.
- (d) سِنِينَ is **plural genitive**: the muḍāf ilayh of seven. The yāʾ is the genitive ending of the plural.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="Look at وَلَبِثُوا۟ فِى كَهْفِهِمْ ثَلَٰثَ مِا۟ئَةٍ سِنِينَ وَٱزْدَادُوا۟ تِسْعًا (Al-Kahf 18:25). Why has ثَلَٰثَ no ة? Why has تِسْعًا no ة? Which noun is understood after تِسْعًا?">

- ثَلَٰثَ has no ة because مِا۟ئَةٍ, “hundred”, is a feminine word. The number before it turns around.
- تِسْعًا has no ة because the understood noun is feminine: years (سِنِينَ, the plural of سَنَة).
- The unwritten noun is **years**. سِنِينَ, three words earlier, supplies it. L4.02 goes through the two readings of سِنِينَ and تِسْعًا.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Read the ordinals. For each: say the ordinal, its meaning, and whether it agrees with its noun or turns around. (a) ثَلَٰثَةٌ رَّابِعُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ (Al-Kahf 18:22, part) (b) إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ ثَالِثُ ثَلَٰثَةٍ (Al-Ma'idah 5:73, part) (c) وَٱلْخَٰمِسَةُ أَنَّ لَعْنَتَ ٱللَّهِ عَلَيْهِ (An-Nur 24:7, part)">

- (a) **رَابِعُهُمْ**, “the fourth of them”. It is masculine and agrees with the unwritten men.
- (b) **ثَالِثُ**, “the third”, in an idafah with ثَلَٰثَةٍ: “the third of three”. It is masculine and nominative here, as the khabar after the accusative ٱللَّهَ.
- (c) **ٱلْخَٰمِسَةُ**, “the fifth”. It is feminine, agreeing with an unwritten feminine noun, and it is nominative as the mubtadaʾ.

No ordinal turns around. They agree with their noun.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={10} q="Why do these two number phrases look different from the usual 3 to 10 pattern? (a) فَخُذْ أَرْبَعَةً مِّنَ ٱلطَّيْرِ (Al-Baqarah 2:260, part) (b) تِسْعَةُ رَهْطٍ (An-Naml 27:48, part)">

- (a) The counted noun, ٱلطَّيْر, is a **collective noun** (a word for a group), so it comes with **min**: “four of the birds”.
- (b) رَهْط, “a band of men”, is a collective noun too, so it follows the number in an idafah with a **singular** genitive. It does not take a plural.

Al-Jadwal’s note on 27:48 explains both patterns. The number has ة in both, because the group is spoken of as masculine.

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="a number plus a counted noun equals a counted phrase"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'a number', small: 'the form depends on the noun' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'the counted noun', small: 'case and number depend on the number' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'a counted phrase', small: 'سَبْعَ لَيَالٍ' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- **One and two** follow the noun and agree with it: وَٰحِدٌ and وَٰحِدَةٌ, ٱثْنَيْنِ and ٱثْنَتَيْنِ.
- **Three to ten turn around.** A masculine noun takes the number with ة, and a feminine noun takes it without ة. The counted noun is plural and genitive. In the Quran the turn holds in 55 of the 56 places with a written noun, and the 56th (6:160) has an explanation.
- **Eleven to nineteen** are two number parts, built on fatḥa (the first part of twelve is like a dual). 11 and 12 agree with the noun. From 13 on the first part turns around and the second agrees. The counted noun is a singular accusative tamyīz. Only 11, 12 and 19 occur.
- **The tens** end in -ūna or -īna and have one form for both genders. The counted noun is a singular accusative tamyīz.
- **A hundred and a thousand** take a singular genitive counted noun.
- **Ordinals** have the pattern فَاعِل and agree with their noun.
- When a number stands alone, its form tells you the gender of the noun that is left out.

<Note kind="tip">Recognise first, produce later. When you see a number from 3 to 10, ask: is the noun masculine? Then the number should end in ة. Look at the singular of a plural.</Note>
