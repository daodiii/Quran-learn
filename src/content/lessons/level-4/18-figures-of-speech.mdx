The verse is about hearts that became hard. It does two things in a few words. First it compares them: **كَٱلْحِجَارَةِ**, “like the stones”. Then it goes past the comparison: **أَوْ أَشَدُّ**, “or harder”.

This is a **simile** (tashbīh). Al-Jadwal names it so. It names a second figure in the words just before, ثُمَّ قَسَتْ قُلُوبُكُم, “then your hearts became hard”. There the word for hardness is borrowed for hearts that turn away from every warning without being moved. That is a metaphor of the hidden kind, which Step 2 explains.

A simile has up to four parts. All four are in the verse you just saw.

| Part | Arabic | In فَهِىَ كَٱلْحِجَارَةِ أَوْ أَشَدُّ قَسْوَةً |
|---|---|---|
| The thing compared | مُشَبَّه | هِىَ, the hearts |
| The thing compared with | مُشَبَّه بِهِ | ٱلْحِجَارَةِ, the stones |
| The little word of comparison | أَدَاة التَّشْبِيه | كَ |
| The point they share | وَجْه الشَّبَه | hardness, said in قَسْوَةً |

Put together: the hearts are like stones in hardness. The point is said at the end of the verse, after “harder”. That word, قَسْوَةً, is a tamyīz (L4.02). Al-Jadwal calls this simile mursal: the little word is there.

Many similes leave a part out. The next blocks show which parts, and what al-Jadwal calls them.

In 101:5 the little word and both things are there. The point is not said. Al-Jadwal calls this a simile with the point left out (mujmal): the mountains come apart and their pieces fly about, as fluffed wool does.

In 54:7 the point is said, in the last word: the people are like locusts, and the locusts are spread out. Al-Jadwal calls this one mufaṣṣal, with the point spelled out. It also shows that the little word is not always كَ. Here it is كَأَنَّ, “as if”.

There is no كَ in this verse. The people are called deaf, dumb and blind. Both sides of the comparison are there: the people, and the three words for people who cannot hear, speak or see.

Al-Jadwal stops to ask whether this is a metaphor. It says the books differ. The careful ones call it a **tashbīh balīgh** (تَشْبِيه بَلِيغ), a simile with its little word left out, and not a metaphor. Its reason: the thing described is named, which is the people. A metaphor is a case where the thing described is not named. Step 2 uses this test again.

The two sides are clear: **أَعْمَٰلُهُمْ** and **سَرَابٍ**. The point is not one word. Al-Jadwal names this a **tashbīh tamthīlī** (تَشْبِيه تَمْثِيلِيّ), a comparison taken from a whole scene. Nothing in 36:37 says “like”. The verb **نَسْلَخُ** is what you do to a sheep: you take off its skin. Al-Jadwal says the word is borrowed for taking the light off the night, so the dark shows as the flesh shows when the skin comes off. The word of skinning is there. The thing it describes, taking the light away, is not named. That is a **metaphor** (istiʿāra).

Al-Jadwal names this kind **taṣrīḥiyya** (تَصْرِيحِيَّة), the open kind: the borrowed word is named, and the thing described is dropped. When the borrowed word is a verb, as here, it adds **tabʿiyya**. In 19:4, وَٱشْتَعَلَ ٱلرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا (“and the head blazed with white hair”), it says the verb was taken from the noun for burning, and so it is tabʿiyya too.

In 14:1 al-Jadwal finds metaphors in two nouns. It reads **ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ** as going astray and **ٱلنُّورِ** as guidance, and it allows the whole phrase to be read as one extended picture. The same book gives 111:4, **حَمَّالَةَ ٱلْحَطَبِ** (“carrier of the firewood”), as the word for someone who carries tales between people: the wood stands for the talk.

Humility has no wing. Al-Jadwal reads the verse like this. Humility is likened to a bird, but the bird is **not named**. Only something that belongs to a bird is named: its wing. The verb “lower” fits a bird too.

|---|---|---|---|

Al-Jadwal finds the hidden kind in 2:74 too, in ثُمَّ قَسَتْ قُلُوبُكُم. It calls it makniyya and tabʿiyya (the borrowed word is a verb), and says the word for hardness is used for hearts that turn away from warnings.

<Note kind="know">Al-Jadwal calls the naming of the wing takhyīl (تَخْيِيل) and the verb “lower” tarshīḥ (تَرْشِيح), two helpers of the hidden metaphor. It also records another view: the wings are lowered as a bird does when it gathers its chicks to care for them.</Note>

        { ar: 'فَيَكُونُ', en: 'and it is', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'ٱلْقَرْيَةَ', en: 'the town (12:82)', type: 'n' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Three common slips.

First, **كُن فَيَكُونُ** (2:117). It can look unfinished: “Be what?” Al-Jadwal reads كُن as a complete verb, “exist”, with a hidden “you” as its doer, so nothing is left out. For the figure, it names a picture: a thing that is willed comes to be with no delay, like an order that is obeyed at once by the one who obeys. It calls that an istiʿāra tamthīliyya, a metaphor drawn from a whole scene.

Second, **ٱلْقَرْيَةَ** in 12:82 is not a gap. Al-Jadwal calls it majāz mursal.

Third, a nominal sentence has no “is”. English adds one. In Arabic nothing is left out. Only count a gap when the grammar asks for a word: a khabar with no mubtadaʾ, a verb with no object it needs, a condition with no answer. In 39:73, حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا جَآءُوهَا وَفُتِحَتْ أَبْوَٰبُهَا, al-Jadwal says the answer of “when” is left out, and gives “they felt at ease” or “they were glad”.

<Check n={26} q="How does al-Jadwal read the verb كُنْ in 2:117?" options={['A complete verb, with nothing left out', 'A verb with a left-out noun', 'A noun']} answer={0} why="Al-Jadwal calls it a complete command verb (فعل أمر تام) with a hidden “you”." />
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-effect" title="Mistake 4: saying what a figure “achieves”">
  <Stage label="Two ways to say it">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Say what the words do:</b> “The khabar is put before the mubtadaʾ.”</li>
      <li><b>Say what a source says:</b> “Al-Jadwal says it limits the kingdom to Allah.”</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

It is easy to write “this figure creates a deep sense of awe” or “this makes the verse more powerful”. Those sentences sound like balagha. They say what the writer feels, and they need a source.

In this lesson, each reason given for a figure is al-Jadwal’s: the ellipsis in 2:216 is for brevity, the fronting in 64:1 limits the kingdom, the fronting in 35:28 limits the fear to the knowers, the yā in 26:78 is dropped for the endings. Where al-Jadwal gives no reason, this lesson shows the figure and stays silent about why.

<Check n={27} q="In 35:28, what reason does al-Jadwal give for ٱللَّهَ being put first?" options={['It limits the fear to the knowers', 'It makes the verse more powerful', 'It is to rhyme']} answer={0} why="Al-Jadwal reads the order as limiting the fear to the knowers, so the lesson gives that reading and adds nothing." />
</Block>

<Block id="mistake-sound" title="Mistake 5: mixing up opposites and matching sounds">
  <Stage label="Which is which">
    <Tiles
      label="Opposites, but not the same sound"
      items={[
        { ar: 'أَيْقَاظًا', en: 'awake', type: 'n' },
        { ar: 'رُقُودٌ', en: 'asleep', type: 'n' },
      ]}
    />
    <Tiles
      label="The same sound, but not opposites"
      items={[
        { ar: 'يَسْقِينِ', en: 'gives me drink', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'يَشْفِينِ', en: 'heals me', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

Ṭibāq is about **meaning**: two opposites in one sentence. Sajʿ is about **sound**: the same ending. The two can meet in one verse, as al-Jadwal says they do at 53:43 to 53:45, but each one needs its own test. Ask “are these opposite in meaning?” for ṭibāq, and “do these end alike when I stop?” for sajʿ.

<Check n={28} q="Which pair is ṭibāq and not sajʿ?" options={['أَيْقَاظًا and رُقُودٌ', 'يَسْقِينِ and يَشْفِينِ', 'ٱلْقُرْءَانَ and ٱلْبَيَانَ']} answer={0} why="Awake and asleep are opposite in meaning. The other pairs only end alike." />
</Block>

<Block id="find" title="How to find the figure">
  <Stage label="Look in this order">
    <ol class="lx-card-list">
      <li><b>Little word:</b> كَ, كَأَنَّ, مَثَل. Check that it means likeness.</li>
      <li><b>Odd word:</b> one that cannot be meant plainly. Is the thing described named? Then a simile. If not, a metaphor.</li>
      <li><b>A sign:</b> a hand, a bite. Kināya. A place for its people: majāz mursal.</li>
      <li><b>Something missing:</b> a khabar with no mubtadaʾ, a verb with no object it needs.</li>
      <li><b>A word first:</b> taqdīm.</li>
      <li><b>A question:</b> one you are not meant to answer. Confirmation or denial.</li>
      <li><b>Contrast or sound:</b> ṭibāq, muqābala, jinās, sajʿ.</li>
    </ol>
  </Stage>

Use this order on every verse. The first four questions are about meaning. The last three are about order, questions and sound. A verse often carries several figures: 2:74 has a simile, a metaphor, a small ellipsis and a tamyīz in a few words.

Then name the source. When you write the name of a figure, add whose reading it is. This is the habit that keeps the figures honest.

<Note kind="tip">Write the figure like this: “Simile (tashbīh): the hearts are like stones, 2:74, al-Jadwal.” One line, with the verse and the source.</Note>
</Block>

  <Stage label="For each phrase, say">

      <li><b>The words that carry it.</b></li>
      <li><b>The source:</b> whose name is it?</li>

Work out each answer first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer.

<Exercise n={1} q="Name the figure in each. (a) مَثَلُهُمْ كَمَثَلِ ٱلَّذِى ٱسْتَوْقَدَ نَارًا (Al-Baqarah 2:17, part) (b) عَضُّوا۟ عَلَيْكُمُ ٱلْأَنَامِلَ مِنَ ٱلْغَيْظِ (Al ʿImran 3:119, part) (c) وَٱشْتَعَلَ ٱلرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا (Maryam 19:4, part) (d) وَٱمْرَأَتُهُۥ حَمَّالَةَ ٱلْحَطَبِ (Al-Masad 111:4) (e) فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ (Yusuf 12:18, part) (f) وَلَمَّا سُقِطَ فِىٓ أَيْدِيهِمْ (Al-Aʿraf 7:149, part)">

- (a) A simile of the kind al-Jadwal calls tamthīlī: a scene, with the word كَ. The mushabbah is the people the verse is about, and the mushabbah bihi is the man who kindled a fire.
- (b) Kināya. Biting the fingertips stands for rage and regret (al-Jadwal).
- (c) A metaphor, of the kind called tabʿiyya because the verb is borrowed from burning. The thing described is the spreading of white hair, and it is not named as such.
- (d) A metaphor. The firewood stands for the tales that set people against each other.
- (e) Ellipsis. The mubtadaʾ is left out: “my patience”.
- (f) Kināya, for deep regret (al-Jadwal).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} q="Find the four parts of the simile in وَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوٓا۟ أَعْمَٰلُهُمْ كَسَرَابٍۭ بِقِيعَةٍ يَحْسَبُهُ ٱلظَّمْـَٔانُ مَآءً (An-Nur 24:39). Which kind does al-Jadwal call it, and why can you not name one single point of likeness?">

- The thing compared: **أَعْمَٰلُهُمْ**, “their deeds”.
- The thing compared with: **سَرَابٍ**, “a mirage”.
- The little word: **كَ**.
- The point: there is no single word for it. Al-Jadwal calls this a tashbīh tamthīlī. The likeness is a whole scene. A man counts on his deeds to help him, as a thirsty man counts on a mirage. Each arrives, and finds nothing.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} q="Simile or metaphor? Say what is named and what is left out. (a) فَهِىَ كَٱلْحِجَارَةِ (Al-Baqarah 2:74, part) (b) صُمٌّۢ بُكْمٌ عُمْىٌ (Al-Baqarah 2:18, part) (c) نَسْلَخُ مِنْهُ ٱلنَّهَارَ (Ya-Sin 36:37, part) (d) وَٱخْفِضْ لَهُمَا جَنَاحَ ٱلذُّلِّ (Al-Isra 17:24, part) (e) وَتَكُونُ ٱلْجِبَالُ كَٱلْعِهْنِ ٱلْمَنفُوشِ (Al-Qariʿah 101:5)">

- (a) A simile. Both sides and the word كَ are named.
- (b) Both sides are named, with no little word. Al-Jadwal says the books differ: the careful ones call it tashbīh balīgh, a simile with the little word left out.
- (c) A metaphor of the open kind (taṣrīḥiyya). The borrowed word, “skin”, is named. The thing described, taking away the light, is not.
- (d) A metaphor of the hidden kind (makniyya). The bird is not named. Only its wing is.
- (e) A simile, with the point not said (al-Jadwal: mujmal).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Kināya, majāz mursal or metaphor? Say what the words point to. (a) فَأَصْبَحَ يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ (Al-Kahf 18:42, part) (b) وَسْـَٔلِ ٱلْقَرْيَةَ (Yusuf 12:82, part) (c) لِتُخْرِجَ ٱلنَّاسَ مِنَ ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ إِلَى ٱلنُّورِ (Ibrahim 14:1, part)">

- (a) Kināya. Turning the hands over points to regret and grief.
- (b) Majāz mursal. The town stands for the people in it (the place for what is in it).
- (c) A metaphor. In al-Jadwal’s reading the darkness is going astray and the light is guidance, and neither of the two is named. It also allows the whole phrase to be read as one extended picture.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="What is left out, and what does al-Jadwal say it is? (a) فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ (Yusuf 12:18, part) (b) وَٱللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ (Al-Baqarah 2:216, part) (c) ٱللَّهُ لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ (Al-Baqarah 2:255, part) (d) حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا جَآءُوهَا وَفُتِحَتْ أَبْوَٰبُهَا (Az-Zumar 39:73, part)">

- (a) The mubtadaʾ of صَبْرٌ: “my patience”, “my matter” or “my affair”.
- (b) The object of يَعْلَمُ: “what is good for you and what is bad”. Al-Jadwal says it is left out for brevity.
- (c) The khabar of لَا, which al-Jadwal supplies as “existing”: “there is no god existing except Him”. The whole sentence لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ is the khabar of ٱللَّهُ, so nothing is left out after ٱللَّهُ.
- (d) The answer of “when” is left out. Al-Jadwal supplies “they felt at ease” or “they were glad”.

</Exercise>
</Block>

<Exercise n={6} q="Put the words back in the usual order, then say what stays the same. (a) لَهُ ٱلْمُلْكُ وَلَهُ ٱلْحَمْدُ (At-Taghabun 64:1, part) (b) إِنَّمَا يَخْشَى ٱللَّهَ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ ٱلْعُلَمَٰٓؤُا۟ (Fatir 35:28, part) (c) لَكُمْ دِينُكُمْ وَلِىَ دِينِ (Al-Kafirun 109:6)">

- (a) ٱلْمُلْكُ لَهُ and ٱلْحَمْدُ لَهُ (made-up forms). The mubtadaʾ stays nominative: -u. Al-Jadwal says the order shows the kingdom and the praise belong to Allah alone.
- (b) إِنَّمَا يَخْشَى ٱلْعُلَمَٰٓؤُا۟ ٱللَّهَ (made-up). ٱللَّهَ keeps its fatḥa as the object, and ٱلْعُلَمَٰٓؤُا۟ keeps its ḍamma as the doer. The endings say who does what in either order. Al-Jadwal says the order limits the fear to the knowers: with the doer first, the meaning would be that they fear only Allah.
- (c) ٱلدِّينُ لَكُمْ would be the usual order for the first half (made-up). Al-Jadwal reads لَكُمْ as tied to a khabar that is put first, and gives no reason for the order.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={7} q="Which kind of question, and what is the listener led to? (a) أَلَمْ نَشْرَحْ لَكَ صَدْرَكَ (Ash-Sharh 94:1) (b) أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَٰبِ ٱلْفِيلِ (Al-Fil 105:1) (c) أَفَلَا يَنظُرُونَ إِلَى ٱلْإِبِلِ كَيْفَ خُلِقَتْ (Al-Ghashiyah 88:17)">

- (a) A question of confirmation (taqrīrī, al-Jadwal). The listener is led to say “yes”.
- (b) A question of confirmation (taqrīrī, al-Jadwal). The listener is led to say “yes, I saw”.
- (c) A question of denial or blame (inkārī, al-Jadwal). It does not ask for news. It blames them for not looking.

Rewrite (a) as a plain statement: “We opened up your chest for you.” The statement carries the same news and does not lead the listener anywhere.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={8} q="List the pairs of opposites. Which verse is also muqābala? (a) وَعَسَىٰٓ أَن تَكْرَهُوا۟ شَيْـًٔا وَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ (Al-Baqarah 2:216, part) (b) فَلْيَضْحَكُوا۟ قَلِيلًا وَلْيَبْكُوا۟ كَثِيرًا (At-Tawbah 9:82) (c) وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ (Al-Kahf 18:18, part)">

- (a) Dislike against love (in the full verse) and good against bad. Al-Jadwal names two pairs in 2:216.
- (b) Laugh against cry, and a little against a lot. Al-Jadwal names ṭibāq and also muqābala, since there are several pairs.
- (c) Awake against asleep. One pair, so ṭibāq only.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Write the four verse endings of Ash-Shuʿara 26:78 to 26:81 as al-Jadwal says they would be with the yā of “me”. Then say why the yā is dropped, and which nūn stays. Use the verbs يَهْدِينِ (26:78), يَسْقِينِ (26:79), يَشْفِينِ (26:80) and يُحْيِينِ (26:81).">

- With the yā, in full: يَهْدِينِى, يَسْقِينِى, يَشْفِينِى, يُحْيِينِى (teaching forms, not verse text).
- Al-Jadwal says the yā is dropped to keep the endings alike (murāʿāt al-fawāṣil).
- The nūn that stays is the nūn that guards the verb (nūn al-wiqāya).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={10} q="Grammar and figure together: وَيَوْمَ يَعَضُّ ٱلظَّالِمُ عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ (Al-Furqan 25:27, part). (a) What is the tense of يَعَضُّ and what is the ending? (b) What is the role of ٱلظَّالِمُ? (c) Why does يَدَيْهِ end in a yā? (d) Which figure does al-Jadwal name, and for what?">

- (a) A present verb, nominative. The ending is a ḍamma, -u, on the last root letter (the root is ع ض ض, with the doubled letter).
- (b) The doer of يَعَضُّ, nominative with a ḍamma.
- (c) يَدَيْهِ is a dual noun after the preposition عَلَىٰ, so it is genitive. A dual is genitive with a yā (L1.08).
- (d) Kināya. Al-Jadwal says biting the hands is a kināya for rage and regret.

</Exercise>

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="a figure of speech is a way of putting a meaning"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'a meaning', small: 'what is said' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'a way of putting it', small: 'compare, hint, leave out, move, ask, match' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'a figure of speech', small: 'name it, with the source' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- A **simile** (tashbīh) has up to four parts: the thing compared, the thing compared with, the little word and the point (2:74). Al-Jadwal names kinds: mursal, mujmal, mufaṣṣal, balīgh and tamthīlī.
- A **metaphor** (istiʿāra) has no “like”. In the open kind the borrowed word is named (36:37, 14:1). In the hidden kind only something that belongs to it is named (17:24). If the thing described is named, it is a comparison.
- **Kināya** names a sign that goes with the meaning (18:42, 3:119). **Majāz mursal** uses a word for something close to it (12:82).
- **Ḥadhf** leaves a word out that the grammar asks for: a mubtadaʾ (12:18), an object (2:216). Not every gap in English is a gap in Arabic.
- **Taqdīm** puts a word first. Al-Jadwal gives its reading in 64:1, 1:5 and 35:28. The endings keep the roles clear.
- A question can be one of confirmation (94:1, 105:1) or of denial (88:17).
- **Ṭibāq** is opposites in one sentence (18:18, 2:216). **Muqābala** is several pairs together (9:82, 3:26).
- **Jinās** is two almost-alike words with different meanings (6:26, 18:104).
- **Sajʿ** is matching sound at the end of clauses, heard at the stop (26:78 to 26:81). **Tawāzī** is two clauses built the same way (2:216).
- Do not call a comparison a metaphor, do not read every كَ as “like”, do not find a gap where the grammar needs nothing, and do not write what a figure “achieves” without a source.
