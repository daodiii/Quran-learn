The verse is three commands in a row. Two of them have a hamza at the front, and one has none.

- **خُذِ** comes from أَخَذَ, “he took”. The root is أ خ ذ, so the hamza is a root letter. The command has lost it.
- **وَأْمُرْ** comes from أَمَرَ, “he ordered”. The root is أ م ر. This command keeps its hamza.
- **وَأَعْرِضْ** comes from أَعْرَضَ, “he turned away”. The root is ع ر ض. The hamza is an extra letter at the front (Form IV, L3.13). The root has no hamza.

One verse shows the three things this lesson is about: a hamza that drops, a hamza that stays, and a hamza that is not part of the root.

A **hamzated verb** (فِعْل مَهْمُوز) has a hamza as one of its three root letters. The roots of the three verbs above are أ خ ذ (first letter), س أ ل (middle) and ب د أ (last). The Corpus writes this root letter with an alif. This course writes it أ, as L3.03 did.

Compare it with the weak verbs of L4.11. A weak letter (و or ي) can turn into a long vowel or drop out. The hamza is a full consonant, and it usually stays through the forms. The past of سَأَلَ goes سَأَلَ, سَأَلْتُ, سَأَلُوا۟, like كَتَبَ in L3.05. The endings are the ones you know, so there is little new to build.

Two things are new. The first is the **seat** of the hamza: the letter it is written on changes with the vowels around it (Step 2). The second is a short list of verbs that drop the hamza in the command (Step 3).

Hamzated verbs are common. In the Corpus file, **2,955** of the **19,356** verb words (15.3%) are counted as hamzated, the same figure as in L4.11. Quran Learn counted every word with a verb stem and read its root letters. Four of the 2,955 are forms of هَاتُوا۟ (“bring!”). The Corpus writes its root with the same letter it uses for a hamza, but the word has a long ā and no hamza. The rest of this lesson leaves those four out and counts **2,951**.

</Block>

The place of the hamza gives the kind. In the Corpus, the hamza is the first root letter in 1,711 verb words, the middle one in 491 and the last one in 749. A few roots carry most of the words.

| Place | Root | Verb | Words in the Corpus |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | أ م ن | أَمِنَ “he felt safe”, ءَامَنَ “he believed” | 558 |
| First | أ خ ذ | أَخَذَ “he took” | 260 |
| First | أ ك ل | أَكَلَ “he ate” | 93 |
| First | أ م ر | أَمَرَ “he ordered” | 79 |
| Middle | س أ ل | سَأَلَ “he asked” | 115 |
| Middle | ب أ س | بِئْسَ “how bad is” | 42 |
| Last | ق ر أ | قَرَأَ “he read” | 17 |
| Last | ب د أ | بَدَأَ “he began” | 15 |
| Last | م ل أ | مَلَأَ “he filled” | 7 |

The count is every form of the root, with Form IV and the other forms included. The 558 words of أ م ن are mostly Form IV: 537 of them are ءَامَنَ and its relatives. 20 are Form I (أَمِنَ) and 1 is Form VIII.

The place matters for what you see. A hamza at the front meets the prefixes of the present (يَأْخُذُ). A hamza at the end meets the endings (قَرَأْتَ). A hamza in the middle sits between two vowels (سَأَلَ).

A fourth group comes in Step 3: roots with a hamza and a weak letter, like ج ي ء and أ ت ي. They are more than half of the verb words with a hamza.

        { ar: 'ؤ', en: 'on a wāw', type: 'x' },

      ]}
    />

L1.01 showed these five. The seat is only a way to write the hamza. The root letter is the hamza itself, and it does not change. What changes is the drawing.

The idea behind the seats is simple: the hamza sits on the letter that **matches a vowel** next to it.

- A **fatḥa** goes with the alif: أَ.
- A **ḍamma** goes with the wāw: ؤُ.
- A **kasra** goes with the yāʾ seat: ئِ. (The seat has no dots.)
- With no vowel to match, the hamza stands **alone**: ء.

Which vowel counts depends on where the hamza is: at the start of a word, at the end, or in the middle. The next three blocks take them one by one.

A hamza at the start of a word is **always on an alif**. A fatḥa or a ḍamma puts the hamza above it: أَخَذَ, أُمِرْتُ. A kasra puts it below: إِيمَٰنُكُمْ. This is the rule from L1.01.

It also holds after a one-letter word that is written joined to the verb, like وَ or فَ. In وَأْمُرْ the hamza is still the first root letter, so it is on an alif.

The present is different. The prefix يَ, تَ or نَ comes first, and the root hamza is now **inside** the written word: يَأْخُذُ. It follows the rules of the middle, in the block after next.

</Block>

        { ar: 'ٱمْرُؤٌا۟', en: 'after ḍamma: wāw', type: 'n' },
        { ar: 'شَىْءٍ', en: 'after sukūn: alone', type: 'n' },

At the end of a word the hamza looks at **one thing**: the vowel on the letter before it. Its own ending does not matter.

| The letter before has | The hamza goes on | Quran word |
|---|---|---|
| fatḥa | an alif | بَدَأَ |
| kasra | a yāʾ seat | قُرِئَ |
| ḍamma | a wāw | ٱمْرُؤٌا۟ |
| sukūn, or a long vowel | the line, alone | شَىْءٍ, جَآءَ |

Look at جَآءَ. The hamza has a fatḥa, but the letter before it is a long ā, so the hamza stands alone. In شَىْءٍ the letter before it has a sukūn, and the hamza is alone again.

An ending can move the hamza away from the end. Then it follows the rules of the middle. Block `many-seats` shows this with ق ر أ.

In the middle the hamza has two neighbours: its own vowel, and the vowel of the letter before it. Compare them. The **strongest** vowel gives the seat. From strongest to weakest the order is kasra, ḍamma, fatḥa, sukūn.

| Seat | Quran word | The hamza has | The letter before has |
|---|---|---|---|
| yāʾ seat | سُئِلَ | kasra | ḍamma |
| yāʾ seat | بِئْسَ | sukūn | kasra |
| wāw | يُؤْمِنُونَ | sukūn | ḍamma |
| wāw | بِسُؤَالِ | fatḥa | ḍamma |
| alif | سَأَلَ | fatḥa | fatḥa |
| alif | يَأْمُرُ | sukūn | fatḥa |

Read the first row. The hamza of سُئِلَ has a kasra and the سُ before it has a ḍamma. Kasra is stronger, so the seat is a yāʾ. In يُؤْمِنُونَ the hamza has a sukūn, so the vowel before it decides: the ḍamma of يُـ gives a wāw. This is the case to watch, because the hamza itself has no vowel.

A long ā before the hamza counts as no vowel. A kasra still wins, as in the doer word سَآئِلٌۢ (yāʾ seat). A fatḥa does not: after a long ā, or after a wāw or yāʾ with a sukūn, a hamza with a fatḥa stands **alone**. You see this in تَرَٰٓءَا and سَوْءَٰتُهُمَا.

The Tanzil text follows the old copies of the Quran. In some places it writes the hamza differently from ordinary print. It is the same letter and the same sound. Only the drawing is different.

| Quran text | Ordinary print | What differs |
|---|---|---|
| يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ | يَسْأَلُونَكَ | the hamza sits on a short stroke, not an alif |
| مَسْـُٔولًا | مَسْؤُولًا | a stroke, not a wāw |
| رُءُوسَكُمْ | رُؤُوسَكُمْ | the hamza is alone, not on a wāw |
| ٱلْقُرْءَانُ | ٱلْقُرْآنُ | the hamza is written out, then a long ā |
| ءَامَنَ | آمَنَ | the same: ءَا is the madda آ |

So learn the rules above for ordinary print, and expect these three habits in the Quran: a stroke after a letter with a sukūn (يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ), the hamza alone between ḍammas or before a wāw (رُءُوسَكُمْ, ٱقْرَءُوا۟), and ءَا for a hamza with a long ā. This course does not teach why the Quran is spelled this way.

L1.02 said that the wavy sign in إِنَّآ is not a hamza. That is true of that word. In ordinary print the madda آ in آمَنَ does stand for a hamza and a long ā together. The Quran text here writes the hamza out, so you see ءَا.

A past hamzated verb takes the endings of كَتَبَ (L3.05). Nothing about the endings changes. The hamza is the first letter of the written word in all the forms above, so it stays on an alif, with a fatḥa. The sukūn goes on the last root letter before -ta, -tum, -tu and -nā, as in كَتَبْتُ.

29:40 has two of the forms. In **أَخَذْنَا** the ending -nā is the doer. In **أَخَذَتْهُ** the ت is the tāʾ of “she” (L3.05), and -hu is the object (L3.02). The doer, ٱلصَّيْحَةُ, comes after its verb and takes a ḍamma.

The table shows eight of the 13 forms. They are teaching forms. The Quran has six of the eight: he, she, they (women), you all, I and we, for example وَأَخَذْنَ (they, women), وَأَخَذْتُمْ (you all) and أَخَذْنَا (we). It has no Form I word for “they (men)” or “you (one man)”. In the Corpus, Form I of أ خ ذ has 127 words.

In the present, the prefix يَ, تَ or نَ comes before the root. A hamza that was the first letter is now **inside** the written word. It has a sukūn, and the letter before it, the prefix, has a fatḥa. By the rule of the middle, the seat is an alif: يَأْخُذُ, تَأْخُذُ, نَأْخُذُ. L3.03 peeled وَيَأْمُرُكُم down to أ م ر. The seat of its hamza is the same: a sukūn after the fatḥa of يَـ.

The vowel in the middle is a ḍamma for أَخَذَ, أَكَلَ and أَمَرَ: يَأْخُذُ, يَأْكُلُ, يَأْمُرُ. The Corpus agrees: it has 10 verb words for “they eat”, with or without a prefix such as وَ, and 4:10 has two of them. The endings are those of L3.06: ـُونَ for “they”, ـْنَ for “they (women)”.

The row for “I” is different. The prefix أَ and the root hamza meet: أَأْخُذُ. They join into one long ā, written ءَا. The next steps show this with real verses.

The command (L3.08) is made from the present, and hamzated verbs go three ways.

**The hamza stays.** Many hamzated verbs keep it: ٱقْرَأْ (“read!”), وَأْمُرْ (“and order!”), ٱئْتِ (“bring!”, from أَتَى). Al-Jadwal explains وَأْمُرْ on 20:132. The full command is اُؤْمُرْ. The helper alif has a ḍamma, because the middle vowel of يَأْمُرُ is a ḍamma, and the root hamza sits on a wāw. After وَ the helper alif falls away, and the hamza is written on an alif. In ٱئْتِ the helper alif has a kasra (the middle vowel of تَأْتِي), and the hamza sits on a yāʾ seat.

**The hamza drops.** A few common verbs lose it. خُذْ comes from تَأْخُذُ, and كُلْ from تَأْكُلُ. L3.08 showed these two: the first hamza drops, so there is no helper alif either. **Both.** سَأَلَ has both: سَلْ and ٱسْأَلْ. The Quran has forms with the hamza, such as فَسْـَٔلْ, 14 times, and سَلْ twice.

| سَأَلَ | تَسْأَلُ | سَلْ or ٱسْأَلْ | 16 commands: 2 without a hamza (2:211), 14 with it (17:101) |
| أَمَرَ | تَأْمُرُ | وَأْمُرْ | 4 commands, all with a hamza (20:132) |
| قَرَأَ | تَقْرَأُ | ٱقْرَأْ | 6 commands, all with a hamza (17:14) |

The root أ م ن has a hamza as its first letter. Form IV puts another hamza in front (L3.13), so **two hamzas** meet. Al-Jadwal explains this on 12:64 and on 2:3. The first hamza has a fatḥa and the second has a sukūn, so they join into one long ā. The madda sign marks it. The pattern is أَفْعَلَ.

The same joining happens in two other places. In 12:64 the prefix أَ of “I” meets the root hamza of أَمِنَ: أَأْمَنُ becomes **ءَامَنُ**. In 12:32 the same happens to أَمَرَ: أَأْمُرُ becomes **ءَامُرُ** (ءَامُرُهُۥ, “I order him”). The first-person row in the table of the present is the same.

The second hamza does not always give a long ā. It follows the vowel of the hamza before it.

| What meets | Written first | The Quran shows | The rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form IV, past | أَأْمَنَ | ءَامَنَ | fatḥa, then a hamza with sukūn: a long ā |
| Form IV, command | أَأْمِنْ | ءَامِنْ | the same |
| “I”, present of أَمَرَ | أَأْمُرُ | ءَامُرُهُۥ | the same |

| Form IV, verbal noun | إِئْمَان | إِيمَٰنُكُمْ | kasra, then a hamza with sukūn: a long ī |
| Form IV of أَتَى, passive | أُؤْتُوا | أُوتُوا۟ | ḍamma, then a hamza with sukūn: a long ū |

The present of Form IV has one hamza only, يُؤْمِنُ. Al-Jadwal says on 2:3 that one hamza drops (three hamzas would meet in “I”), and that the drop stays for the other persons and for the participles. The root hamza that is left has a sukūn, and the ḍamma of يُـ comes before it, so it sits on a wāw. The participle is the same: مُؤْمِنٌ. The participles of L3.19 keep the hamza and follow the seat rules.

- **Active participle.** The pattern is فَاعِل. A first-letter hamza meets the long ā of the pattern: the hamza with a fatḥa and the ā are written together, ءَا, as in ءَاخِذٌۢ. This is a hamza and a long ā, not two hamzas. A middle hamza sits between the ā and a kasra, so it gets a yāʾ seat: سَآئِلٌ. Al-Jadwal calls السَّائِل on 51:19 an active participle of the three-letter verb سَأَلَ, on the pattern فَاعِل.
- **Passive participle.** The pattern is مَفْعُول. The hamza has a sukūn after a fatḥa in مَّأْكُولٍۭ: an alif. In مَسْـُٔولًا the hamza has a ḍamma after a sukūn: a wāw in ordinary print and a stroke in the Quran. Al-Jadwal gives both as passive participles on the pattern مَفْعُول.
- **Form IV.** The participle of Form IV drops its extra hamza, as in the present. The root hamza sits on a wāw: ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ.

Some roots have a hamza **and** a weak letter. They are common. In the Corpus, **1,513** of the 2,951 hamzated verb words (51.3%) belong to a root with a و or ي as well. Four roots carry most of them.

| Word | Root | Hamza | Weak letter | Words in the Corpus | Taught in |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| جَآءَ | ج ي ء | last | the middle ي | 278 | L4.12 |
| شَآءَ | ش ي ء | last | the middle ي | 236 | L4.12 |
| أَتَىٰ | أ ت ي | first | the last ي | 535 | L4.13 |
| رَءَا | ر أ ي | middle | the last ي | 315 | L4.13 |

In these roots the two letters do their own work. The hamza follows the seat rules of this lesson. The weak letter follows the rules of L4.12 or L4.13. In جَآءَ and شَآءَ the middle ي became a long ā, as in قَالَ, and the hamza stands alone after it (the rule of the end). In أَتَىٰ the first hamza stays on an alif, and the last ي shows as a long ā at the end.

When an ending with a sukūn follows, the long ā of جَآءَ and شَآءَ is cut, and the hamza has a sukūn after a kasra. It then sits on a yāʾ seat, as the rule of the middle says: جِئْتُكُم and شِئْتَ. L4.12 shows these forms.

A small root of the same kind is **ي ء س**, with a first ي and a middle hamza. The Corpus has 10 verb words from it. In يَئِسَ, “he despaired”, the hamza has a kasra after a fatḥa, so it sits on a yāʾ seat. In the present, 12:87 has يَا۟يْـَٔسُ. The hamza follows a ي with a sukūn, and the Quran draws it on a stroke, as in يَسْـَٔلُونَ. The text also has a silent alif there, marked ۟. L4.14 teaches the first ي.

These four roots add up to 1,364 words, nearly half of all the hamzated verb words.

L4.11 gave the map of the six kinds of verb: sound, hollow, defective, assimilated, hamzated and doubled. Four of them have a special letter in the root. Here they are side by side.

| Kind | Arabic | Where the letter is | Example and root | What happens | Taught in |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hollow | أَجْوَف | the middle | قَالَ (ق و ل) | the weak letter becomes a long vowel or drops: قُلْ | L4.12 |
| Defective | نَاقِص | the last | هَدَىٰ (ه د ي) | the last letter changes or drops: ٱهْدِ | L4.13 |
| Assimilated | مِثَال | the first | وَجَدَ (و ج د) | the و drops in the present: يَجِدُ | L4.14 |
| Hamzated | مَهْمُوز | any place | سَأَلَ (س أ ل) | the hamza stays and its seat moves; three verbs drop it in the command | L4.15 |

The hamza is different from the other three. It is a full consonant, not a weak letter, so it rarely drops or turns into a vowel. Grammar books treat hamzated verbs together with the weak verbs. One reason is that the shape of the word still changes: in the seat, in the command and in the two hamzas of ءَامَنَ. This explanation is a Quran Learn teaching summary.

To read any verb you meet, ask four questions.

1. **Root:** peel off the prefixes and endings (L3.03).
2. **Letters:** is there a و, a ي or a hamza among the three?
3. **Place:** is it first, middle or last?
4. **Kind:** what does that kind do? For a hamza, find the seat from the vowels (Step 2).

A root can have two such letters. جَآءَ has a middle ي and a last hamza, so it belongs to two kinds at once.

</Block>

  <Stage>

In قُلْ the middle و of قَالَ is gone. In سَأَلْتَهُم the hamza of سَأَلَ is in every form. A hamza is a full consonant, so expect it to stay.

The opposite mistake also happens: “a hamzated verb always loses its hamza in the command”. Only a few do. وَأْمُرْ and ٱقْرَأْ keep it, and خُذْ and كُلُوا۟ lose it. Learn the short list from the block on the command.

In يُؤْمِنُونَ the hamza has a sukūn. A learner who looks only at the hamza finds no vowel and picks an alif. The vowel before it, the ḍamma of يُـ, decides, and a ḍamma goes with the wāw.

In سُئِلَ the hamza has a kasra and the letter before it has a ḍamma. The kasra is stronger, so the seat is a yāʾ. Compare both vowels every time. The order, strongest first, is kasra, ḍamma, fatḥa, sukūn.

  <Stage>

A hamza at the start of a verb can come from four places. It can be the **first root letter** (أَخَذَ). It can be the **Form IV letter** added to a root (أَنزَلْنَآ, from ن ز ل). It can be the prefix for **“I”** in the present (أَكُونَ, from ك و ن). Or it can be the **question** hamza in front of a verb (أَتَقُولُونَ, “do you say?”).

Only the first makes a hamzated verb. Use the test from L3.13: take off the first أَ and count. Three letters left means the أَ was added. Two letters left means it was a root letter. For ءَامَنَ the answer is both: the first hamza was added and the second is the root letter.

<Block id="done" title="You have finished the lesson">
  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="a hamza in the root makes a hamzated verb"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'a root with a hamza', small: 'first, middle or last' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'a seat from the vowels', small: 'alif, wāw, yāʾ or alone' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'hamzated verb', small: 'فِعْل مَهْمُوز' },
      ]}
    />
  </Stage>

- A **hamzated verb** has a hamza as one of its three root letters. In the Corpus, 2,955 of 19,356 verb words (15.3%) are counted as hamzated. Without the four forms of هَاتُوا۟, 2,951 have a hamza: 1,711 first, 491 middle, 749 last.
- The hamza is a full consonant and stays through the forms. Its **seat** changes: at the start an alif, at the end by the letter before, in the middle by the strongest vowel (kasra, ḍamma, fatḥa, sukūn). The Quran text sometimes draws the hamza on a stroke or alone.
- The past and present take the usual endings: أَخَذْنَا, يَأْخُذُ. A hamza with a sukūn after a ḍamma sits on a wāw: يُؤْمِنُ, يُؤْخَذُ.
- In the command, **خُذْ**, **كُلُوا۟** and **سَلْ** lose the hamza. وَأْمُرْ and ٱقْرَأْ keep it.
- Two hamzas join into a long vowel: **ءَامَنَ** (ā), **إِيمَٰن** (ī), **أُوتُوا۟** (ū). The same happens in **ءَامُرُ**, “I order”.
- The passive (سُئِلَ, أُمِرْتُ) and the participles (ءَاخِذٌ, سَآئِلٌ, مَأْكُولٌ, مُؤْمِنٌ) follow the same seat rules.
- More than half of the hamzated verb words, 1,513 (51.3%), are in roots that also have a weak letter: جَآءَ, شَآءَ, أَتَىٰ, رَءَا.
- A hamza in a word is not always a hamza in the root (سَمَآء). Check the root.
