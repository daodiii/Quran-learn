The verse is about a person who calls on his Lord when harm touches him, and forgets Him when relief comes. It has three verbs, and each ends in a different way.

- **دَعَا** ends in an alif: “he called”.
- **نَسِىَ** ends in a ي with a fatḥa: “he forgot”.
- **يَدْعُوٓا۟** ends in a و: “he calls”, in the present.

دَعَا and يَدْعُوٓا۟ come from the same root, د ع و. Look for the و. In يَدْعُوٓا۟ it shows. In دَعَا it has turned into an alif. The root of نَسِىَ is ن س ي.

The small round mark over the alif after the و of يَدْعُوٓا۟ tells you not to say that alif. L3.05 showed it after the wāw of “they”. Here it follows a verb for one person (Mistake 3 comes back to this).

A **defective verb** (فِعْل نَاقِص, *fiʿl nāqiṣ*) has a weak letter, و or ي, as its **last** root letter. Count the root of دَعَا: د, ع, و. The third letter is a و.

The word نَاقِص means “lacking”. In many forms the last letter seems to be missing, as in دَعَا, which shows only two letters of the root.

L3.03 and L3.05 named the other weak kinds. In a **hollow** verb the middle letter is weak (قَالَ, L4.12). In an **assimilated** verb the first letter is weak (وَعَدَ, L4.14). A verb can have more than one weak letter, like و ق ي, whose first and last letters are both weak.

The weak last letter can do three things. This lesson shows when each happens.

- It can **stay**: يَدْعُو, with the و.
- It can turn into an **alif**: دَعَا.
- It can **drop**: لَمْ يَدْعُ, without the و.

</Block>

A sound verb keeps all three root letters in every form. A defective verb does not. The table puts كَتَبَ (root ك ت ب, “he wrote”) beside هَدَىٰ (root ه د ي, “he guided”).

| Form | Sound: كَتَبَ | Defective: هَدَىٰ | What is different |
|---|---|---|---|
| past, he | كَتَبَ | هَدَىٰ | the last root letter shows as an alif |
| past, I | كَتَبْتُ | هَدَيْتُ | the same: the last letter takes a sukūn |
| past, they (men) | كَتَبُوا | هَدَوْا | the alif has dropped, and a fatḥa stays before the و |
| present, he | يَكْتُبُ | يَهْدِي | a ḍamma that shows, and one that is hidden |
| jazm | لَمْ يَكْتُبْ | لَمْ يَهْدِ | a sukūn, and a dropped letter |
| command | ٱكْتُبْ | ٱهْدِ | a sukūn, and a dropped letter |

The sound verb needs no new rule. The defective verb has three things to learn: the alif in the past, the long vowel in the present, and the drop in jazm and the command. The last three are the same drop.

L3.07 showed this line. It says that jazm shows by **dropping a letter** in two kinds of present verb. One is the five verbs, which lose their nūn. The other is the present verb that is **الْمُعْتَلُّ الْآخِرِ**, “weak at the end”.

That is the verb of this lesson. The book names it by its weak end. Al-Jadwal uses the shorter name نَاقِص, for example on 3:151.

The book gives only the one rule: the weak letter drops in jazm. The rest of this lesson is a teaching summary made from the Quran text and al-Jadwal. No approved book states the other rules in this form, and the lesson says so where it matters.

L3.05 showed both words in Al-Anfal 8:17. The root is ر م ي. In رَمَىٰ you see ر and م, then an alif. In رَمَيْتَ the ي has come back.

A defective verb hides its last root letter in some forms and shows it in others. To find the letter, change the verb to “I” and look before the تُ: دَعَا gives دَعَوْتُ, and رَمَىٰ gives رَمَيْتُ.

This test works for verbs that end in an alif. Step 2 shows one pattern where it does not.

<Block id="three" title="Three patterns for “he”">
  <Stage label="Three past verbs">
    <Tiles
      label="The three patterns of the past verb"
      items={[
        { ar: 'دَعَا', en: '1: an alif from و (root د ع و)', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'رَمَىٰ', en: '2: an alif written ىٰ, from ي (root ر م ي)', type: 'v' },
        { ar: 'نَسِىَ', en: '3: no alif; the ي stays (root ن س ي)', type: 'v' },
      ]}
    />

The past verb for “he” has three patterns.

1. **An alif from و.** دَعَا, from د ع و.
2. **An alif from ي.** رَمَىٰ, from ر م ي. It is written with ىٰ, the alif maqṣūra, an alif that looks like a ي with no dots. It sounds the same as the alif of دَعَا.
3. **The -iya pattern.** نَسِيَ, from ن س ي. There is no alif. The ي shows, with a fatḥa.

Why does the weak letter turn into an alif in the first two? Al-Jadwal gives the reason for the present verbs يَخْشَىٰ (20:3) and لِتَشْقَىٰ (20:2): the weak letter has a vowel, and it comes after a fatḥa, so it turns into an alif. The same happens in دَعَا and رَمَىٰ. The letter before the last one has a fatḥa.

In نَسِيَ the letter before the last one, the س, has a **kasra**. So the ي does not turn into an alif. This part of the reason is a teaching summary. No approved book words it like this.

The patterns follow the doors of L3.04. دَعَا يَدْعُو is door 1 (al-Jadwal calls it the door of نَصَرَ, on 2:186). رَمَىٰ يَرْمِي is door 2. نَسِيَ يَنْسَىٰ is door 4, with a kasra in the past and a fatḥa in the present.

<Note kind="tip">The Quran text in this course (Tanzil) writes the last ي of the -iya pattern without dots: نَسِىَ, رَضِىَ. The tables below write it with the dots, as in the usual spelling: نَسِيَ.</Note>

The endings are the 13 endings of L3.05. The stem is what changes. The **After the middle letter** column starts with the vowel of the ع, the second root letter, and shows everything after it.

Look at the rows in three groups.

- **He** keeps the alif.
- **She**, the two women and they (men) have no alif: دَعَتْ, دَعَتَا, دَعَوْا. The fatḥa on the ع stays.
- **The two men** and every form with a consonant ending show the و: دَعَوَا, and from دَعَوْنَ to دَعَوْنَا.

The next two blocks explain the groups.

These forms are in the Quran text: دَعَا (39:8), دَعَوَا (7:189, written دَّعَوَا), دَعَوْا۟ (25:13), دَعَوْتُ (71:5) and, with an attached pronoun, أَدَعَوْتُمُوهُمْ (7:193), “did you call them?”. The other forms are teaching forms. They follow the endings of L3.05.

This table is the same as the last one, with ي in place of و. The he-form is written with ىٰ, and the three groups are the same.

- **He** keeps the alif, written ىٰ: رَمَىٰ.
- **She**, the two women and they (men) have no alif: رَمَتْ, رَمَتَا, رَمَوْا. Look at رَمَوْا: it has a و, and the root has none. The و is the doer, “they”.
- **The two men** and every form with a consonant ending show the ي: رَمَيَا, رَمَيْتُ.

In the Quran text رَمَىٰ and رَمَيْتَ (8:17) are the only Form I past forms of this root. Other verbs with a last ي show the other forms: فَأَتَتْ (19:27), أَتَيَآ (18:77), أَتَوا۟ (3:188), وَجَرَيْنَ (10:22), عَصَيْتُ (6:15) and هَدَيْنَا (6:84). The verb مَشَىٰ, “he walked”, is in the Quran text only as مَّشَوْا۟ (2:20), apart from its present and its command.

The weak letter comes back in two places.

- **The alif of -ā** (the two men): the letter returns with a fatḥa before it. دَعَوَا, رَمَيَا, أَتَيَآ in 18:77.

L3.05 taught that the last root letter takes a sukūn before these endings. In a defective verb the last root letter is the weak letter. The alif of the stem cannot carry a sukūn, so the root letter comes back and takes it. Say it slowly: *da-ʿaw-tu*. This is a teaching summary.

In 10:22 the doer -na is the feminine plural. It speaks of the ships, which Arabic treats as feminine. Al-Jadwal calls the nūn “the nūn of women”.

The usual spelling of a three-letter verb is an alif when the root letter is و (دَعَا, عَفَا, بَدَا) and ىٰ when it is ي (رَمَىٰ, هَدَىٰ). The Quran text has exceptions.

Quran Learn counted the Form I past “he” verbs with a three-letter stem in the Corpus (the -iya pattern left out).

- **Root with و:** 29 words are written with an alif (7 verbs), and 5 words with ىٰ (5 verbs): تَلَىٰ (91:2), طَحَىٰ (91:6), دَحَىٰ (79:30), سَجَىٰ (93:2) and زَكَىٰ (24:21).
- **Root with ي:** 171 words are written with ىٰ, and 20 with an alif: رَءَا (18 words, for example 6:76), طَغَا (69:11) and عَصَا (14:36).

So do not use the spelling to find the root. Use the “I” form, دَعَوْتُ or رَمَيْتُ, or the present (Step 3).

In the longer verbs, Forms II to X, the spelling is more regular. The Corpus tags 282 past “he” words as Forms II to X. 267 of them end in ىٰ, and 10 end in a plain alif: أَحْيَا (nine words, root ح ي ي) and تَوَلَّا (22:4). The other 5 are written with a small alif only, as in أَوْصَٰنِى (19:31).

Three verbs, three endings: **يَتْلُو** (-ū), **تَجْرِي** (-ī) and **يَخْشَىٰ** (-ā). In each one the last root letter is a long vowel, and no short vowel shows on it.

In the plain present (rafʿ) the books say a ḍamma is there, but **hidden**. Al-Jadwal says it of تَجْرِى in 2:25: a ḍamma hidden on the yāʾ. L3.06 introduced the idea with يَدْعُو. An alif cannot carry a vowel at all. Al-Jadwal says of يَتَوَلَّىٰ in 3:23 that its ḍamma is hidden on the alif.

Which ending does a verb take? Look at the vowel before the last letter.

- **ḍamma:** the ending is **ـُو**. Every one of these in the Corpus has a root with و.
- **kasra:** the ending is **ـِي**. Every one of these has a root with ي.
- **fatḥa:** the ending is **ـَىٰ**. Roots with و and with ي both have it.

The Corpus count is for the Form I present verbs that stand alone in the indicative (he, she, you (one), I, we): 67 words end in ـُو, all from a root with و. 236 end in ـِي, all from a root with ي. 22 end in ـَىٰ from a root with و, and 110 from a root with ي. This is a count by Quran Learn, and some tags are wrong.

So the present helps you find the root: يَدْعُو shows a و, and يَرْمِي shows a ي. Some verbs have a fatḥa in the present because of a throat letter in the root (door 3 of L3.04): سَعَىٰ يَسْعَىٰ and نَهَىٰ يَنْهَىٰ. They also end in ـَىٰ.

- **You (one woman):** تَدْعِينَ, with a kasra.

L3.09 made the passive. A defective verb follows it, with two special shapes.

- **Past passive:** the last letter shows as **ي**, whether the root has و or ي: دُعِيَ, رُمِيَ, هُدِيَ. The و of دَعَا turns into ي after the kasra. With “they” the ي drops: دُعُوٓا۟ (24:48).
- **Present passive:** the last letter is always an **alif**: يُدْعَىٰ, يُرْمَىٰ, يُهْدَىٰ (10:35). With “they” the alif drops and the fatḥa stays: يُدْعَوْنَ (3:23).

| Tense and person | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| past, he | دَعَا | دُعِيَ |
| past, they | دَعَوْا | دُعُوا |
| present, he | يَدْعُو | يُدْعَىٰ |
| present, they | يَدْعُونَ | يُدْعَوْنَ |

L3.09 gave يُوحَىٰٓ (18:110), يُفْتَرَىٰ (12:111) and يُجْزَىٰ (6:160). Al-Jadwal says of يُجْزَىٰ that the yāʾ turned into an alif because the verb is passive. The same is true of every defective verb in the present passive. On 3:23 he gives the pattern of يُدْعَوْنَ as يُفْعَوْنَ.

In the Corpus 274 of the 3,481 defective verb words are passive: 138 past and 136 present.

The doer word of Form I is on the pattern **فَاعِل** (L3.19). From دَعَا it would be دَاعِوٌ. A و at the end after a kasra turns into ي, as al-Jadwal says on 2:186 (the original is الداعو). That gives دَاعِيٌ. What happens next depends on the case.

- **With a tanwīn** (nominative or genitive): the ي drops: دَاعٍ, قَاضٍ, بَاقٍ. Al-Jadwal says the yāʾ and the tanwīn are two silent sounds that meet, so the yāʾ drops. The word is called **manqūṣ** (اسْم مَنْقُوص). Its ḍamma is hidden on the dropped yāʾ (16:96).
- **With ٱلْ:** the ي shows: ٱلدَّاعِي. The Quran text leaves it out of ٱلدَّاعِ in 2:186. Al-Jadwal says it drops in connected reading in some readings, so it was left out of the writing.
- **Accusative, feminine:** the ي shows: دَاعِيًا (33:46), رَاضِيَةً (89:28).

The done-to word has a doubled weak letter. In مَرْضِيَّة (89:28) al-Jadwal says the original is مرضوي: the و turned into ي and joined the other ي.

It is easy to keep the weak letter in jazm: لَمْ يَدْعُو, لَمْ يَرْمِي. Both are wrong. After لَمْ the weak letter drops (Step 3): لَمْ يَدْعُ, لَمْ يَرْمِ.

The opposite mistake is to add a visible ḍamma to the plain present: يَدْعُوُ, يَرْمِيُ. The weak letter is a long vowel and cannot carry the ḍamma. The grammar counts it as hidden.

And a naṣb fatḥa is not hidden on و and ي: يَدْعُوَ, يَرْمِيَ. It shows. Only on an alif is it hidden.

In رَمَتْ the last letter you see is ت. It is the ending of “she”, and the root is ر م ي. In رَمَوْا the و is the doer, “they”. In نَسُوا the same. The weak root letter is not there.

To find the root:

1. Change the verb to “I”: رَمَيْتُ, دَعَوْتُ (Step 1).
2. Look at the present: يَرْمِي shows a ي, and يَدْعُو shows a و (Step 3).
3. Check the Corpus root. Do not trust the spelling of the alif (Step 2).

The -iya pattern is the one case where the “I” form can mislead: رَضِيتُ shows a ي, and the root is ر ض و.

<Exercise n={1} q="Is the verb defective? If it is, say which letter is the weak one. (a) ق ت ل (b) د ع و (c) ه د ي (d) ن ص ر (e) ر م ي (f) ك ت ب (g) م ش ي (h) ع م ل (i) ق و ل (j) و ق ي">

- (b) **Yes:** the last letter is **و**.
- (c) **Yes:** the last letter is **ي**.

- (h) **No.** A sound root.

- (j) **Yes:** the last letter is **ي**. The first letter و is also weak (L4.14). The command is قِ, as in وَقِنَا (2:201).

<Exercise n={3} q="Write the past of هَدَىٰ, “he guided”, root ه د ي, for: (a) she (b) they (men) (c) I (d) we (e) the two (men)">

- (a) هَدَتْ (*hadat*). The alif drops before the ت.
- (b) هَدَوْا (*hadaw*). The alif drops, and the wāw is the doer.
- (c) هَدَيْتُ (*hadaytu*). The ي returns with a sukūn.
- (d) هَدَيْنَا (*hadaynā*). The ي returns with a sukūn. It is in 6:84.
- (e) هَدَيَا (*hadayā*). The ي returns with a fatḥa before the alif.

In هَدَوْا the و is not the ي changed. The alif has dropped, and the و is the doer.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} q="Write the past of خَشِيَ, “he feared”, root خ ش ي, for: (a) she (b) they (men) (c) I (d) you all (men)">

- (a) خَشِيَتْ (*khashiyat*). The ي keeps its fatḥa.
- (b) خَشُوا (*khashū*). The ي drops, and the kasra becomes a ḍamma.
- (c) خَشِيتُ (*khashītu*). The ي has a sukūn and reads as a long ī. It is in 20:94.
- (d) خَشِيتُمْ (*khashītum*).

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} q="Take the root د ع و. Write: (a) the past “he” (b) the present “he” (c) the jazm “he” after لَمْ (d) the command to one man (e) the command to many men">

- (a) دَعَا (*daʿā*). The و turns into an alif.
- (b) يَدْعُو (*yadʿū*). The long ū, with a hidden ḍamma.
- (c) لَمْ يَدْعُ (*lam yadʿu*). The و drops, and the ḍamma stays.
- (d) ٱدْعُ (*udʿu*). The same drop, and the helper ٱ.
- (e) ٱدْعُوا۟ (*udʿū*). The root و and the doer wāw are one, and the alif after it is written and not read.

</Exercise>

- (b) رَمَيْتُ (*ramaytu*). The ي returns with a sukūn.
- (c) يَرْمِي (*yarmī*). The long ī, with a hidden ḍamma.
- (d) يَرْمِيَ (*yarmiya*). The ي shows a fatḥa.
- (e) لَمْ يَرْمِ (*lam yarmi*). The ي drops, and the kasra stays.
- (f) يَرْمُونَ (*yarmūna*), as in 24:4. The ي drops, and the kasra becomes a ḍamma.

<Exercise n={7} q="Go through ٱهْدِنَا ٱلصِّرَٰطَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ (Al-Fatihah 1:6). (a) What is the root of ٱهْدِنَا? (b) What is the command before نَا is added? (c) What happened to the ي? (d) What does نَا mean? (e) What does the verb ask for?">

- (a) ه د ي, “guiding”.
- (b) ٱهْدِ (*ihdi*), “guide!”.
- (c) It dropped in the command, as in jazm. It did not merge with نَا.
- (d) “Us”, the object.
- (e) A guide to the straight path. Al-Jadwal calls it a command of supplication, said to Allah. هَدَىٰ takes its second object, ٱلصِّرَٰطَ, straight.

<Exercise n={8} q="Find the defective verb in فَمِنْهُم مَّن يَمْشِى عَلَىٰ بَطْنِهِۦ (An-Nur 24:45, part). Say the root, the tense, and why no ḍamma shows.">

The verb is **يَمْشِى**, root م ش ي, a present verb for “he”. It is in the plain present (rafʿ). The last letter is the long ī, and the ḍamma is hidden on it. The ي is the last root letter, and the kasra before it points to a root with ي.

In the same verse the verb comes two more times: *yamshi ʿalā rijlayn* and *yamshi ʿalā arbaʿ*.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={9} q="Why has the weak letter dropped? (a) وَلَا تَنسَ نَصِيبَكَ مِنَ ٱلدُّنْيَا (Al-Qasas 28:77, part) (b) ثُمَّ يَرْمِ بِهِۦ بَرِيٓـًٔا (An-Nisa 4:112, part) (c) يَخْلُ لَكُمْ وَجْهُ أَبِيكُمْ (Yusuf 12:9, part)">

- (a) **تَنسَ**: root ن س ي. The verb is in jazm after لَا of the ban. The ىٰ (alif) dropped, and the fatḥa stays.
- (b) **يَرْمِ**: root ر م ي. The verb is in jazm, joined to يَكْسِبْ after مَن. Al-Jadwal says its sign of jazm is the dropped weak letter, and its pattern is يَفْعِ. The kasra stays.
- (c) **يَخْلُ**: root خ ل و. Al-Jadwal says it is in jazm as the answer to a request: “throw him in a land, and your father’s face will be free for you”. The و dropped, and the ḍamma stays.

</Exercise>

- (a) **ٱدْعُ.** The و drops, and the helper has a ḍamma.
- (b) **ٱهْدِ.** The ي drops, and the helper has a kasra.
- (c) **ٱنْهَ.** The alif drops, and the fatḥa stays. It is in 31:17.
- (d) **ٱمْشُوا.** The nūn drops. It is in 67:15.

<Exercise n={11} q="Is يَعْفُونَ “they (men)” or “they (women)”? Read إِلَّآ أَن يَعْفُونَ أَوْ يَعْفُوَا۟ ٱلَّذِى (Al-Baqarah 2:237, part). Then say what يَعْفُوَا۟ is.">

**They (women).** The verse is about wives. The و is the root letter, and the nūn is the women’s -na. Al-Jadwal calls it a present verb built on a sukūn, in the place of naṣb after أَن.

**يَعْفُوَا۟** is “he forgives”, in naṣb after أَوْ, with a visible fatḥa on the و. Al-Jadwal says it is joined to the place of يَعْفُونَ.

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={12} q="Name each word: (a) دُعِىَ ٱللَّهُ وَحْدَهُۥ (Ghafir 40:12, part) (b) يُدْعَوْنَ إِلَىٰ كِتَٰبِ ٱللَّهِ (Al-Imran 3:23, part) (c) ٱلدَّاعِ (Al-Baqarah 2:186, part) (d) قَاضٍ (Ta-Ha 20:72, part)">

  <Stage label="The idea">
    <Formula
      label="what you see at the end depends on what follows"
      items={[
        { kind: 'm', text: 'root with a weak last letter', small: 'د ع و, ر م ي' },
        { kind: 'k', text: 'what follows', small: 'an ending, or a mood' },
        { kind: 's', text: 'what you see', small: 'an alif, the letter, or nothing' },
      ]}
    />

- A **defective verb** (نَاقِص) has a weak letter, و or ي, as its last root letter. It is 3,481 of the 19,356 verb words in the Corpus (18.0%). The weak letter can stay, turn into an alif, or drop.
- The past “he” has three patterns: an **alif from و** (دَعَا), an **alif from ي** written ىٰ (رَمَىٰ), and the **-iya pattern** (نَسِيَ). The spelling does not always show the root. Use the “I” form: دَعَوْتُ, رَمَيْتُ.
- In the 13 past forms, **he** keeps the alif. **She**, the two women and they (men) lose it: دَعَتْ, دَعَتَا, دَعَوْا. **The two men** and every consonant ending show the letter, with a fatḥa before the alif and a sukūn before the other endings: دَعَوَا, دَعَوْتُ. In the -iya pattern the ي stays and **they (men)** is نَسُوا.
- The present ends in **ـُو**, **ـِي** or **ـَىٰ**, with a hidden ḍamma. The women’s يَدْعُونَ is written like the men’s.
- **Naṣb:** the و and the ي show a fatḥa, and it is hidden only on an alif. **Jazm and the command:** the weak letter drops: لَمْ يَدْعُ, ٱدْعُ.
- **Passive:** دُعِيَ, يُدْعَىٰ. **Doer word:** دَاعٍ, a manqūṣ noun. **Place noun:** مَفْعَل, as in مَأْوَىٰ. The other verb forms change in the same ways.
- The rules of word shape in this lesson are teaching summaries made from the Quran text and al-Jadwal. No approved book states them in this form.
