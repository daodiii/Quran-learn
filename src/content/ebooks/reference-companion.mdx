<OrientationChapter number="01" title="Every Word Is a Noun, Verb, or Particle. Nothing Else." anchor="orientation">

Seven rules. Ninety percent of the Quran. Keep this orientation close while you study — each principle is a cheat-code that unlocks a whole category of meaning.

**The rule:** Every Arabic word — no exceptions — belongs to one of three categories: **ism** (noun), **fiʿl** (verb), or **ḥarf** (particle).

| Category | Arabic | What it does | Quick test |
|----------|--------|--------------|------------|
| **Noun** اِسْمٌ | Names things, qualities, people, ideas | Can it take ال or tanween (ـٌ)? → Noun |
| **Verb** فِعْلٌ | Shows action tied to time | Does it tell you WHEN? → Verb |
| **Particle** حَرْفٌ | Connects — meaningless alone | Is it glue? → Particle |

**Why it matters:** Once you know what *kind* of word you're reading, every other rule becomes easier — because rules apply to types, not random words.

**Examples:**

- **ٱللَّهُ** — noun (names Allah)
- **كَتَبَ** — verb (he wrote — past time)
- **فِي** — particle (in — meaningless without what follows)

**Cheat shortcut:** If a word has **ال** or **tanween**, it is definitely a noun. If it has a tense prefix (يَ, تَ, نَ, أَ), it is definitely a verb.

</OrientationChapter>

<OrientationChapter number="02" title="Every Verb Comes from a 3-Letter Root">

**The rule:** Every Arabic verb (and most nouns) is built from a **three-letter root** (جَذْر / jadhr). The root carries the core meaning. Everything else — prefixes, suffixes, doubled letters, inserted vowels — is a **costume** worn over that root.

**How to undress a verb:**

1. Remove any tense prefix (يَ, تَ, نَ, أَ, اِسْتَ...)
2. Remove any suffix (ـَ, ـُوا, ـْتُ, ـْنَا...)
3. Remove any doubled letter (shadda marks a doubled letter — count it once)
4. Remove any inserted long vowel (ا between letters = Form III)
5. What remains = the 3-letter root

**Example:** يَسْتَغْفِرُونَ

- Remove prefix يَ and prefix اِسْتَـ... wait — strip all to find: **غ-ف-ر** = forgiveness

Root ج-ذ-ر means "root." Root ك-ت-ب means "writing." Root ع-ل-م means "knowledge." Once you know the root, you can guess the meaning family of any word you encounter.

</OrientationChapter>

<OrientationChapter number="03" title="The Verb Changes; You Don't Need a Separate Pronoun">

**The rule:** Arabic packs WHO performed the action directly into the verb via **suffixes** (past tense) and **prefixes + suffixes** (present tense). You rarely need a separate pronoun.

**Past tense suffix key:**

| Suffix | Means | Example |
|--------|-------|---------|
| — (just fatha) | he | كَتَبَ (kataba) |
| ـَتْ | she | كَتَبَتْ (katabat) |
| ـُوا | they (men) | كَتَبُوا (katabū) |
| ـْنَ | they (women) | كَتَبْنَ (katabna) |
| ـْتَ | you (man) | كَتَبْتَ (katabta) |
| ـْتِ | you (woman) | كَتَبْتِ (katabti) |
| ـْتُ | I | كَتَبْتُ (katabtu) |
| ـْنَا | we | كَتَبْنَا (katabnā) |
| ـَا | they two | كَتَبَا (katabā) |

**Present tense prefix key — remember "ANAYTA" (أَنَيْتَ):**

| Prefix | Means |
|--------|-------|
| **أَ** | I |
| **نَ** | we |
| **يَ** | he / they (men) |
| **تَ** | you / she / they (women) |

**Cheat rule:** In past tense, if the suffix starts with **ت**, the actor is YOU or I. If it is **وا**, it is THEY (men). In present tense, the first letter of the verb tells you everything.

</OrientationChapter>

<OrientationChapter number="04" title="Nouns Change Endings Based on Their Job">

**The rule:** A noun's last vowel (or ending) tells you its **grammatical role** in the sentence. This is called **iʿrāb** (الْإِعْرَابُ) — case endings.

| Case | Job | Ending (indefinite) | Ending (definite) | Memory hook |
|------|-----|--------------------|--------------------|-------------|
| **Nominative** مَرْفُوع | The actor (subject) | ـٌ (un) | ـُ (u) | **U** — yoU are the doer |
| **Accusative** مَنْصُوب | Acted upon (object) | ـً (an) | ـَ (a) | **A** — hAs action done to it |
| **Genitive** مَجْرُور | After preposition or possession | ـٍ (in) | ـِ (i) | **In** — in, from, under |

**Cheat rule:** See ـُ or ـٌ → that word is the **actor**. See ـَ or ـً → it is **acted upon**. See ـِ or ـٍ → it follows a preposition or belongs to something.

</OrientationChapter>

<OrientationChapter number="05" title="Masculine Is the Default; Feminine Is Always Marked">

**The rule:** In Arabic, masculine is the **unmarked** form. Feminine is always signaled by a visible marker — usually **ة** (tāʾ marbūṭa) at the end of a noun, or **ـَتْ** on a past-tense verb.

| Form | Example | Meaning |
|------|---------|---------|
| Masculine noun | كِتَابٌ (kitābun) | a book |
| Feminine noun | مَدْرَسَةٌ (madrasatun) | a school (ة signals feminine) |
| Masc. verb | كَتَبَ (kataba) | he wrote |
| Fem. verb | كَتَبَتْ (katabat) | she wrote (ـَتْ suffix) |
| Masc. adjective | كَبِيرٌ (kabīrun) | big (masculine) |
| Fem. adjective | كَبِيرَةٌ (kabīratun) | big (feminine — ة added) |

**Cheat rule:** If you see **ة** on a noun, it is feminine. If you see **ـَتْ** on a past verb, the subject is female. No marker = masculine (or default).

**Exception:** Some nouns are feminine without ة — body parts that come in pairs (يَدٌ "hand," عَيْنٌ "eye") and names of certain things (شَمْسٌ "sun," أَرْضٌ "earth"). These must be memorized.

</OrientationChapter>

<OrientationChapter number="06" title="Definite = ال, Indefinite = Tanween. They Never Coexist.">

**The rule:** Every noun in Arabic is either **definite** ("the book") or **indefinite** ("a book"). The marker is always visible.

| State | Marker | Example | Meaning |
|-------|--------|---------|---------|
| **Definite** | ال at start | ٱلْكِتَابُ (al-kitābu) | **the** book |
| **Indefinite** | Tanween at end (ـٌ ـً ـٍ) | كِتَابٌ (kitābun) | **a** book |
| Both? | IMPOSSIBLE | ~~ٱلْكِتَابٌ~~ | Does not exist |

**Tanween is always THREE possible vowels — one for each case:**

- ـٌ (nominative indefinite) — subject + indefinite
- ـً (accusative indefinite) — object + indefinite
- ـٍ (genitive indefinite) — after preposition + indefinite

**Cheat rule:** If a word has ال, no tanween. If it has tanween, no ال. The moment you see ال, you know the noun is definite AND you know its grammatical case from the last vowel.

</OrientationChapter>

<OrientationChapter number="07" title="Word Order Is Flexible; Meaning Comes from Endings">

**The rule:** Arabic word order is NOT fixed the way English is. In English, word position determines meaning: "The dog bit the man" ≠ "The man bit the dog." In Arabic, the **case endings** carry the meaning — word order is for **emphasis**.

All three of these mean the same thing in Arabic:

1. **ضَرَبَ الرَّجُلُ الْكَلْبَ** — verb first (standard verbal sentence)
2. **الرَّجُلُ ضَرَبَ الْكَلْبَ** — subject first (nominal sentence, more emphatic)
3. **الْكَلْبَ ضَرَبَ الرَّجُلُ** — object first (maximum emphasis on "the DOG")

In all three: الرَّجُلُ has ـُ = it is the actor. الْكَلْبَ has ـَ = it receives the action. The case endings make it unambiguous no matter what order you put the words in.

**Why this matters in the Quran:** When Allah says إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ (iyyāka naʿbudu — "You alone we worship"), the object إِيَّاكَ is placed FIRST for maximum emphasis. In normal Arabic, the verb comes first. This inversion is the Quran saying: *"It is exclusively YOU we worship — no one else."*

**Cheat rule:** Don't assume the first word is the subject. Find the word with ـُ — that is the subject. Find the word with ـَ after a verb — that is the object. Position = emphasis, not role.

</OrientationChapter>

  <p class="orient-body" style="margin-bottom:14px;">The sound masculine plural applies to male human nouns (مُسْلِمُونَ "Muslims") and masculine adjectives (صَالِحُونَ "righteous ones"). Like the dual, it has only two case forms.</p>

{/* The five nouns */}
<section class="ebook-page ebook-page-folio" style="padding:0.6in 0.5in 0.5in;">
  <h3 style="font-family:'Geist',sans-serif;font-weight:500;font-size:16px;color:#efe7d4;margin:0 0 12px;">The five nouns (الْأَسْمَاءُ الْخَمْسَةُ)</h3>
  <p class="orient-body" style="margin-bottom:10px;">Five special nouns use <strong>long vowels</strong> instead of short vowel case endings when they are (1) singular, (2) indefinite or in iḍāfah, and (3) not followed by the first-person possessive suffix (ـي). The five are: أَبٌ (father), أَخٌ (brother), حَمٌ (father-in-law), فُو (mouth), ذُو (possessor of).</p>

  <Callout type="rule" title="The Causative Confusion — II vs IV">
    Both Form II (فَعَّلَ) and Form IV (أَفْعَلَ) can be translated as "to cause," and beginners mix them up constantly. The visible difference is decisive: Form II <strong>doubles the middle root letter</strong> (you will always see a shadda — ّ — on letter two). Form IV <strong>adds a hamza with fatḥa</strong> (أَ) before the root and leaves the middle letter alone. The nuance also differs: Form II tends to be intensive, repeated, or directed at an object (عَلَّمَ — "he taught"), while Form IV is purer causation — bring about a result (أَعْلَمَ — "he informed"; أَنْزَلَ — "He sent down").
  </Callout>
</section>

There is no rule; the pattern is a property of each individual verb and you must learn it together with the verb. Arabic dictionaries always list both the past and the present so you know which middle vowel to use.</p>

  <p class="orient-body" style="margin-bottom:14px;">A <strong>root</strong> (جَذْرٌ, <em>jadhr</em>) is a cluster of consonants — almost always three, occasionally four — that carries a single core meaning. Every word derived from a given root shares a family resemblance. The root <strong>ك ت ب</strong> (k-t-b), for example, carries the core idea of "writing," and from it the language builds an entire household of related words.</p>

  <p class="orient-body" style="margin-top:14px;">When you encounter an unfamiliar word in the Quran, identifying its root unlocks three things at once: a clue to its meaning, a way to look it up (Arabic dictionaries are organised by root, not alphabetically), and a thread that connects it to the other forms of the same root that appear elsewhere in the Book.</p>

  <h3 style="font-family:'Geist',sans-serif;font-weight:500;font-size:16px;color:#efe7d4;margin:0 0 12px;">Trilateral and Quadrilateral Roots</h3>
  <p class="orient-body" style="margin-bottom:14px;">About <strong>95% of Quranic vocabulary</strong> comes from <strong>trilateral</strong> (three-consonant) roots. Arabic grammarians use the template <strong>ف ع ل</strong> (f-ʿ-l) as a stand-in for any trilateral root: <strong>ف</strong> represents the first root letter, <strong>ع</strong> the second, and <strong>ل</strong> the third. The remaining 5% are <strong>quadrilateral</strong> (four-consonant) roots, often onomatopoeic or expressive — words like <strong>زَلْزَلَ</strong> (z-l-z-l, to shake / earthquake) and <strong>وَسْوَسَ</strong> (w-s-w-s, to whisper, as the Shayṭān does).</p>

  <p class="orient-body" style="margin-bottom:14px;">These are the most frequent moulds you will meet in Quranic Arabic. Knowing them lets you categorise an unfamiliar word at a glance — verb or noun, doer or done-to, place or instrument, basic or causative.</p>
  <ChartBlock label="Chart · Major Derivation Patterns (using root ك ت ب)">
    <GrammarTable
      caption="Major Patterns at a Glance"
      headers={["Pattern", "Function", "ك ت ب example", "Translation"]}
      rows={[
        ["فَعَلَ", "Past-tense verb (he did)", "كَتَبَ", "he wrote"],

        ["مَفْعَلٌ", "Place or time of the action", "مَكْتَبٌ", "desk / office"],
        ["أَفْعَلَ", "Causative verb (made someone do)", "أَكْتَبَ", "made someone write"],

  <Callout type="rule" title="Pattern Recognition at a Glance">
    A <strong>مَ</strong> prefix on a noun usually marks a <strong>place, time, or instrument</strong> (مَسْجِدٌ — masjid, place of prostration). A <strong>تَ</strong> prefix on a verb often marks <strong>reflexive or reciprocal</strong> action (تَعَلَّمَ — taught oneself / learned). An <strong>اِ</strong> prefix often marks <strong>middle voice or intensive</strong> action (اِسْتَغْفَرَ — sought forgiveness). A doubled middle letter typically signals <strong>intensive or causative</strong> meaning (عَلَّمَ — taught, made someone know).

  <h3 style="font-family:'Geist',sans-serif;font-weight:500;font-size:16px;color:#efe7d4;margin:0 0 12px;">Looking Up a Word — The Six-Step Workflow</h3>
  <p class="orient-body" style="margin-bottom:14px;">This is the most practical skill for independent Quran study. Arabic dictionaries do not organise words alphabetically the way English ones do; they organise them by <strong>root</strong>, so you must extract the root from any word form you encounter before you can look it up.</p>
  <ChartBlock label="Chart · From Inflected Word to Dictionary Entry">
    <GrammarTable
      caption="The Six-Step Lookup Workflow"
      headers={["Step", "What to do", "Example: وَالْمُسْلِمُونَ"]}
      rows={[
        ["1", "Strip prefixes: conjunctions (وَ, فَ), the article (الْ), and prepositions (بِ, لِ, كَ).", "→ مُسْلِمُونَ"],
        ["2", "Strip suffixes: plural endings (ـُونَ, ـِينَ), pronoun suffixes (ـهُ, ـهَا), and tanwīn.", "→ مُسْلِم"],
        ["3", "Remove pattern letters: مُ-, تَ-, اِ-, أَ-, inserted ت, doubled letters. Leave only the 3 (or 4) root consonants.", "مُ is a pattern prefix → root letters are س ل م"],
        ["4", "Watch for weak letters. ا in hollow verbs traces back to و or ي (قَالَ → ق و ل); a final ـى often traces back to ي (هَدَى → ه د ي).", "Not weak — clean trilateral root س ل م"],
        ["5", "Arrange the root in dictionary order (ا ب ت ث ج ح خ د ذ ر ز س ش ص ض ط ظ ع غ ف ق ك ل م ن ه و ي) and look up the entry.", "Open Hans Wehr at س, then س ل, then س ل م"],
        ["6", "Read the family of derivatives under the root entry and choose the meaning that fits your verse's context.", "س ل م → safety, peace, submission → مُسْلِم = one who submits"],
      ]}
      rtl={false}
    />
  </ChartBlock>
  <Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes to Avoid">
    Do not keep <strong>pattern letters</strong> as part of the root — the مُ in مُسْلِمٌ belongs to the مُفْعِلٌ mould, not to the root. Do not confuse <strong>long vowels with root consonants</strong> — the ا in قَالَ stands in for the original و, so the root is ق و ل, not ق ا ل. And always <strong>split off attached pronouns</strong> before looking up the head word: رَبُّهُ is رَبُّ (lord) + ـهُ (his), and you look up رَبُّ.
  </Callout>
</section>
